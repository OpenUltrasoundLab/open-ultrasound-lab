From prototype to product: FDA 510(k), Q-Sub and EU MDR for diagnostic ultrasound

A beamformer that works on the bench is not yet a product. Between the prototype and the first clinical sale sit a design history that a regulator can audit, an acoustic-output package that survives statistics, a predicate argument, a submission clock with holds that stop it, and in Europe a notified body whose queue is measured in months. This module treats that path as an engineering system with inputs, failure modes and timing, so you can plan it, defend it and avoid the deficiencies that stall most submissions.

36.1The regulatory map for an ultrasound engineer

Roadmap step 1 of 9 · idea and classification

In the United States a diagnostic ultrasound system or transducer is a Class II device, cleared through a premarket notification, the 510(k), in which the sponsor shows that the device is substantially equivalent (SE) to a legally marketed predicate. Three classification regulations in 21 CFR Part 892 cover almost all imaging hardware, and each maps to a three-letter product code whose database page lists the consensus standards FDA expects to see. For a general-purpose cart or handheld with Doppler the usual primary code is IYN (21 CFR 892.1550); transducers fall under ITX (892.1570), pulsed-echo imaging under IYO (892.1560), and software and AI functions under the image-processing regulations.

In the European Union the same system is a medical device under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR). Annex VIII, Rule 10 makes active diagnostic devices that supply energy absorbed by the body Class IIa, and the Commission's classification guidance gives diagnostic ultrasound as its example of that indent. In FDA review language an AI letter is an additional-information request that puts a 510(k) on hold. This module writes "AI letter" or "AI request" for that, and "AI-enabled" or "machine learning" for artificial intelligence.

36.2Design controls under the QMSR: from prototype to design history

Roadmap step 2 of 9 · design controls

Since 2 February 2026 the U.S. quality system regulation, 21 CFR Part 820, has been the Quality Management System Regulation (QMSR), which incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference. For a development team the consequence is simple. A research prototype can be built outside design controls, but the moment a design is meant to become a product it needs a design plan, and every claim the submission will later make has to trace back to a controlled design input. The design requirements apply even to devices under an investigational device exemption (IDE), so a clinical feasibility study does not buy an exemption.

Regulatory detail

The QMSR incorporates the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standard ISO 13485:2016 together with clause 3 of ISO 9000:2015. On 2 February 2026 FDA stopped using its former inspection technique (QSIT) and now inspects under Compliance Program 7382.850. Risk management is explicitly required, and the design and development requirements (820.10(c), i.e. ISO 13485 clause 7.3) still apply to devices under an IDE (21 CFR 812.1(a)).

Reviewers and inspectors both read the design file, and two habits make it reviewable. The first is a live trace matrix that links each user need to a design input, its output, the verification or validation test, the risk control and the labeling statement. The second is writing design inputs that can be measured: "attenuation coefficient bias within ±0.05 dB/cm/MHz on phantoms of 0.3–1.0 dB/cm/MHz" (an illustrative target) can be verified, whereas "accurate attenuation imaging" cannot. Notified bodies report missing traceability between software tests and specifications as a frequent finding, and FDA reviewers look for the same links in the eSTAR performance-testing section.

Regulatory detail

The finding is listed in the Team-NB (European association of notified bodies) best-practice guidance (BPG) on technical documentation, v2, April 2023.

Risk management follows ISO 14971:2019, a process standard: identify hazards and hazardous situations, estimate and evaluate the risk, control it, verify the controls, and weigh residual and overall risk against benefit. The standard leaves the acceptability criteria to the manufacturer; a 5 × 5 severity × probability matrix with acceptable, review and unacceptable zones is one common, illustrative policy. Controls are applied in priority order, inherently safe design first, then protective measures, and information for safety (labeling, training) last, and each control needs a test proving that it works, because an unverified control is not a control. Ultrasound teams tend to miss two kinds of hazard: those that appear only in combinations of modes, transducers and user-interface functions (recall cases 3 and 12 in §36.10), and those created by quantitative outputs that clinicians act on (case 1).

A starting hazard list from public recalls

ISO 14971:2019 carries FDA recognition 5-125. The public recall records suggest these starting hazards: transesophageal (TEE) probe tip over-temperature when thermal sensing is not configured, probe-face heating in an untested mode combination, an inaccurate quantitative value (attenuation) after a software update, patient leakage current from a wrong insulation component, infection from incomplete reprocessing of an endocavity probe, and a remote exploit through a network DICOM service.

36.3The FDA ultrasound guidance, section by section

Roadmap step 3 of 9 · testing and standards

The one document every ultrasound engineer should read in full is FDA's Marketing Clearance of Diagnostic Ultrasound Systems and Transducers (2023). Its section 5.2 lists what a 510(k) should contain, from indications and device description through predicate comparison, acoustic output, clinical safety and effectiveness and labeling to the track-specific items. Module 22 derives every acoustic quantity; here we use them.

Guidance versions and the contents of 5.2

Final guidance issued 21 February 2023; originally issued 27 June 2019; it superseded the 9 September 2008 version; docket FDA-2017-D-5372. Section 5.2 contents: indications for use (5.2.1), device description stating the track (5.2.2), predicate comparison (5.2.3), acoustic output and its test methodology (5.2.4), general clinical safety and effectiveness (5.2.5), labeling (5.2.6), and the Track 1 (5.2.7) or Track 3 (5.2.8) specifics.

The first decision is the track. Track 3 is for devices that conform to the output display of IEC 60601-2-37 (International Electrotechnical Commission): output is not judged per application, global maxima apply, and MI and TI are displayed so that the user can apply ALARA. Track 1 is for devices without that display, including any fixed-output device, and holds derated output to application-specific preamendments levels that are far lower for fetal and ophthalmic use. As the guidance puts it, "There is no Track 2."

Measurement uncertainty enters through the limits themselves. The guidance estimates the uncertainty of its own levels at ±30 % for intensities and ±15 % for MI, so a combined (Type A and Type B) uncertainty within those values generally needs no correction, while any excess reduces the levels by that excess. A thick-membrane hydrophone or an unverified spatial-averaging correction (Module 21) is enough to push a lab over.

The rest of 5.2 is mostly engineering evidence. The item that matters most for quantitative work is measurement accuracy (5.2.5.1): every clinical measurement needs its accuracy and range per transducer and mode from a justified method such as a phantom, and for Doppler the guidance rules out simulated or electronic data because they leave the transducer out of the test system. The same logic argues for phantoms, not simulations, when you validate attenuation, sound speed or shear-wave speed. Some of the text has aged, so read it with its date in mind: its software section still speaks of "level of concern" and the old 820.30 clauses, whereas software documentation now follows the June 2023 software guidance (Basic or Enhanced level, §36.8) and Part 820 is the QMSR.

36.4Changes without a new 510(k), and why quantitative modes are outside

Roadmap step 9 of 9 · changes and postmarket

Section 5.1 of the ultrasound guidance is an enforcement policy: FDA does not intend to enforce the 510(k) requirement for a modification of an already cleared ultrasound device when ten conditions all hold. They amount to keeping the device inside known territory. The intended use must be unchanged; only well-established modes may be introduced or affected; acoustic output and the interrogation parameters must stay within set bounds; there must be no novel mechanical or thermal effect, with acoustic radiation force impulse (ARFI) imaging named as the example of a novel mechanical effect; and the safety features the guidance expects (element check, surface-temperature protection, reprocessing labeling) must be in place. Even then the manufacturer updates the design history file.

The ten conditions of 5.1.2
  1. The intended use is unchanged (5.1.2.1, itself nine sub-conditions, including no new disease-specific indication, no contrast-agent use and prescription use only).
  2. It is not a reusable device that needs new reprocessing labeling and validation.
  3. Only well-established modes are introduced or affected (Table 2).
  4. Acoustic output stays within the recommended maxima.
  5. Interrogation parameters stay within 1–20 MHz centre frequency, 0–7 MPa peak rarefactional pressure, 1–100 cycles per pulse (not applicable to CW Doppler and coded excitation) and a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) of 100 Hz–20 kHz.
  6. No novel mechanical or thermal effects (ARFI imaging is the example of a novel mechanical effect).
  7. Measurement and processing features are clearly described, with user-editable parameters where possible.
  8. The transducer element check is present.
  9. Probe surface temperature protection meets IEC 60601-2-37.
  10. Endocavity labeling includes validated cleaning and disinfection instructions and identifies appropriate covers.

The guidance's examples of changes that fit: adding CW and pulsed-wave (PW) Doppler; an organ-volume algorithm based on well-established segmentation with its scientific basis disclosed; a new transducer with indications and output similar to one already cleared on the system; a B-mode noise-reduction filter described per 5.1.2.6.

Condition 3 is the one that matters for quantitative ultrasound. Table 2 lists the well-established modes, essentially B-mode, M-mode, the Doppler family, strain and harmonic imaging and their combinations, and Note 2 then states that a change introducing or affecting any other mode is outside the policy. It names the examples: shear wave elastography (SWE), acoustic attenuation mapping, transmission-based imaging and sound speed measurement. For anyone working on QUS, elastography or sound-speed estimation this is the most consequential sentence in the guidance, because a new quantitative mode, or a change that affects one, generally means a new 510(k) with phantom accuracy data, and very often a Pre-Sub first.

Running case

Decision at this step: attenuation mapping is named in Table 2, Note 2, so the liver attenuation feature fails condition 3 whatever its output. The section 5.1 shortcut is closed for the first clearance and for every later change that affects the feature: the team plans a 510(k) with phantom accuracy data and a Pre-Sub on the accuracy protocol. 36.5Changing a device after clearance: the 510(k)-or-file decision

Roadmap step 9 of 9 · changes and postmarket

Most changes to a cleared system fall outside the ultrasound policy of §36.4, or need the general test anyway, and that test is in the regulation. Under 21 CFR 807.81(a)(3) a new premarket notification is required when a marketed device "is about to be significantly changed or modified in design, components, method of manufacture, or intended use", and two kinds of change count as significant: "a change or modification in the device that could significantly affect the safety or effectiveness of the device", and "a major change or modification in the intended use of the device". The burden sits with the 510(k) holder. You decide, you document the decision, and FDA may ask to see that record later, for example during an inspection.

The full text of 807.81(a)(3)

(i) "a change or modification in the device that could significantly affect the safety or effectiveness of the device, e.g., a significant change or modification in design, material, chemical composition, energy source, or manufacturing process", and (ii) "a major change or modification in the intended use of the device". FDA's 510(k) page states that the 510(k) holder makes and documents this decision.

Two final guidances from October 2017 turn the test into flowcharts, one for device changes in general and one for software. Both compare the changed device with the original device, defined as the most recently cleared version rather than the predicate, and both assess each change on its own and together with the others, so that several small changes since the last clearance can cross the threshold together. The general guidance first asks whether the change is meant to significantly improve safety or effectiveness (if so, a new 510(k) is likely) and then walks it through three charts, A for labeling, B for technology, engineering and performance, and C for materials. Two definitions carry most of the weight: a new risk is a hazard or hazardous situation that did not exist for the original device, and an existing risk is significantly modified when its score, acceptability category or duration changes.

Guidance dockets and the questions of charts A, B and C

Deciding When to Submit a 510(k) for a Change to an Existing Device (docket FDA-2011-D-0453) and Deciding When to Submit a 510(k) for a Software Change to an Existing Device (FDA-2016-D-2021).

The first question is whether the change is made with the intent to significantly improve safety or effectiveness; if it is, a new 510(k) is likely. The change is then walked through three charts. A, labeling: A1 a change in the indications for use (sub-questions for single-use to reusable, prescription to over-the-counter, a new disease, condition or patient population, and whether a risk assessment finds new or significantly modified risks); A2 adding or deleting a contraindication, which generally could significantly affect safety or effectiveness; A3 warnings or precautions, routed back through the A1 sub-questions; A4 directions for use. B, technology, engineering and performance: B2 control mechanism, operating principle or energy type ("almost all changes in the control mechanism ... could significantly affect safety and effectiveness"); B3 sterilization, cleaning or disinfection; B4 packaging or expiration dating; B5 other design changes, with B5.1 (does it significantly affect use?), B5.2 (does a risk-based assessment identify new or significantly modified risks?), B5.3 (are clinical data needed for design validation?) and B5.4 (did verification or validation produce unexpected issues?). C, materials: C2 a change in material type, formulation, chemical composition or processing; C3 patient contact; C4 new or increased biocompatibility concerns; C4.1 the same material already used in a similar legally marketed device; C5 an effect on performance, which sends you to B5. A new risk is a hazard or hazardous situation that did not exist for the original device; an existing risk is significantly modified when its score, acceptability category or duration changes.

The software guidance asks five questions in order, and they recur throughout this module:

  1. Is the change made solely to strengthen cybersecurity, with no other impact on the software or device?
  2. Is it made solely to return the system to the specification of the most recently cleared device?
  3. (3a) Does it introduce a new risk, or modify an existing one, that could result in significant harm and is not effectively mitigated in the most recently cleared device? (3b) Does it create or require a new or modified risk control measure for a hazardous situation that could result in significant harm?
  4. Could it significantly affect clinical functionality or performance specifications directly associated with the intended use?

Yes to 1 or 2 points to documentation; yes to 3a, 3b or 4 points to a new 510(k). The guidance also warns that "a complete rewrite of the algorithm, even with the same performance claims and risk profile" can still need a submission, which is the trap in any "it gives the same numbers" argument.

Software change types

The guidance describes common change types (infrastructure, architecture, core algorithm, clarification of requirements, cosmetic, re-engineering and refactoring) and warns that "a complete rewrite of the algorithm, even with the same performance claims and risk profile" can still need a submission.

For an ultrasound change the questions are best taken in this order:

  1. Is the change covered by an authorized predetermined change control plan (PCCP), and does it follow that plan's protocol (§36.12)?
  2. Are all ten conditions of the ultrasound policy met (Section 36.4; gate 2 of Demo 36.3)?
  3. Walk every aspect of the change, software included, through the 2017 charts.
  4. Check the cumulative effect against the last cleared version.

When the answer is "no new 510(k)", the analysis still has to be documented under the quality system, and FDA can ask for it. Industry calls this record a letter to file (or memo to file), an industry term rather than a regulatory form. A reviewer or investigator reading it should find what changed and why, the comparison with the most recently cleared version, each flowchart question with its answer and reasoning, the updated risk analysis, the verification and validation with acceptance criteria set before testing, the labeling impact, the running list of changes since the last clearance, and the approvals. It lives in the design history file (DHF) and the change-control record, and if verification later gives unexpected results the guidance says the "no 510(k)" decision should be reconsidered.

When a submission is needed, the next choice is between a Special and a Traditional 510(k). The Special 510(k) is for a manufacturer changing its own legally marketed device when performance data are not needed or "well-established methods are available", and when all data "can be reviewed in a summary or risk analysis format"; FDA intends to process it within 30 days of receipt. A new quantitative mode with a new accuracy protocol, or any change that needs full test reports, therefore usually goes Traditional.

Regulatory detail: Special 510(k)

Final guidance, September 2019, docket FDA-2018-D-3304. It can include some indications changes. A submission that does not fit is converted to a Traditional 510(k), with the review clock continuing toward the 90-day deadline.

A third route avoids the submission altogether: a PCCP authorized with the device lets you make the changes it describes, following its protocol, without a new 510(k), although changes to intended use are generally not suitable for one. §36.12 works through an ultrasound PCCP.

Regulatory detail: PCCP status

Statutory basis: section 515C of the FD&C Act, added by FDORA, the Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022. The PCCP guidance for AI-enabled software is final; a broader draft for all devices (August 2024, FDA-2024-D-2338) is still a draft.

Cybersecurity patches are the most frequent case. Software question 1 sends a change made solely to strengthen cybersecurity toward documentation, and FDA typically does not require premarket review for routine updates and patches. An operating-system upgrade is not such a patch, though: the guidance treats OS changes as infrastructure and porting to a new OS as architecture, so an upgrade has to pass questions 3 and 4.

Regulatory detail: patches and Part 806

FDA's postmarket cybersecurity guidance (final, December 2016) says FDA typically does not require premarket review for routine cybersecurity updates and patches. Changes that strengthen security against a controlled risk are treated as enhancements and are not reportable as corrections under Part 806; remediation of an uncontrolled risk ordinarily is reportable, with the enforcement-discretion conditions the guidance lists. For a cyber device, section 524B also requires the plan to deliver such updates.

In the EU, changes reach the notified body by a different path. For a Class IIa ultrasound system the technical documentation is sampled rather than certified device by device, so changes go to the NB through the change-notification procedure in your NB contract and through surveillance, and that procedure is worth agreeing early.

Regulatory detail: MDR change rules

Under Annex IX, section 2.4, the manufacturer informs the notified body (NB) of "any plan for substantial changes to the quality management system, or the device-range covered"; the NB assesses them and approves by a supplement to the QMS certificate. Section 4.10 requires NB approval of changes to a device that holds an EU technical documentation assessment certificate "where such changes could affect the safety and performance of the device or the conditions prescribed for use"; that certificate applies to Class III and certain Class IIb devices. For legacy devices still under a Directive certificate, any significant change in design or intended purpose ends the transitional status; MDCG 2020-3 rev.1 (May 2023) gives charts A–E, and its software chart treats bug fixes that restore the specification, security updates and standard third-party operating-system updates as not significant, and an algorithm change that affects the operating principle or the diagnosis as significant.

"Fit, form, function" is not FDA's test

Fit, form, function is configuration-management shorthand for deciding whether a changed part keeps its part number: fit (interfaces, dimensions, connectors), form (shape, material, appearance), function (what it does, how well). It overlaps with 807.81(a)(3) without matching it. A function change usually reaches B2/B5 and software question 4, a form change reaches chart C when the material touches the patient, and a fit change reaches B5.1 and B5.2; yet a new connector housing that is verified and adds no risk is still only documentation. Conversely, identical fit, form and function can still need a 510(k): a second supplier's lens rubber with a different formulation (chart C), a new manufacturing process that could affect safety or effectiveness (named in 807.81(a)(3)(i)), or a labeling-only new indication (A1).

    36.7Talking to FDA early: the Q-Submission program

    Roadmap step 4 of 9 · Pre-Sub

    36.8Predicates and the 510(k) package

    Roadmap step 5 of 9 · 510(k) and eSTAR

    FDA finds a device substantially equivalent under 21 CFR 807.100(b) when it has the same intended use as the predicate and either the same technological characteristics, or different ones that raise no different questions of safety and effectiveness and are shown, with clinical data if necessary, to be as safe and effective as a legally marketed device. The predicate itself must still be legally marketed, which rules out one removed at FDA's initiative or found misbranded or adulterated by a court.

    Regulatory detail: predicate eligibility and review outcomes

    Predicate eligibility: 807.100(b)(3). The possible outcomes of a 510(k) review are an SE order, a not-substantially-equivalent (NSE) order, a request for additional information, a hold for financial certification or disclosure (Part 54), or advice that no 510(k) is required.

    For an ultrasound system the usual strategy is one primary predicate that matches intended use and technology, plus reference devices that support specific features such as a transducer, a quantitative mode or an AI function; the 2026 summary K261835, for instance, lists one primary predicate (K253366) and three reference devices (K242005, K214039, K253370). The indications-for-use table (clinical applications × modes × transducers) is the spine of the argument, because every cell that is new relative to the predicate needs a predicate or reference device with that cell, plus your own test data. The predicate should also be clean: FDA's draft predicate guidance (September 2023) asks for one cleared with well-established methods and with no associated design-related recall, so check the Recall database and TPLC for its product line before you cite it.

    The four predicate best practices (draft, September 2023)

    Choose a predicate cleared with well-established methods, that meets or exceeds expected safety and performance, that has no unmitigated use-related or design-related safety issues, and that has no associated design-related recall.

    Competitors' 510(k) summaries are the best free intelligence a team has. 21 CFR 807.92 fixes their content (submitter, device names, predicate, description, intended use, technological comparison and, for performance-based equivalence, the tests and their conclusions), and Demo 36.4 reads one recent ultrasound summary, K261835 (decided 30 July 2026), part by part.

    36.9The review clock: FDA days, calendar days and holds

    Roadmap step 6 of 9 · review (steps 6–7: review and clearance)

    FDA's review goals come from the user-fee agreement, MDUFA V. For 95 % of 510(k)s, FDA communicates acceptance or refusal within 15 calendar days of receipt, holds a Substantive Interaction (SI) within 60 calendar days and reaches a decision within 90 FDA days, a count that stops whenever the submission is on hold. That last detail is what makes schedules slip, because the holds are on the sponsor's side of the ledger.

    Regulatory detail: MDUFA V period and TTD goals

    MDUFA V covers fiscal years FY2023–2027. The shared Total Time to Decision (TTD) goal, in calendar days, is 128 (FY2023), 124 (FY2024) and 112 (FY2025–2027), possibly 108 in FY2026–2027 if conditions are met.

    Regulatory detail: screening, RTA and the AI hold

FDA's eSTAR pages do not state a fixed number of days for the screening itself. The RTA policy guidance (April 2022) still defines what "complete" means and is worth reading as a checklist.     36.10Learning from failures: Recall database, MAUDE and TPLC

    Roadmap step 9 of 9 · changes and postmarket

    Three public FDA databases turn other teams' field problems into your design inputs.     36.11The EU route, run in parallel

    Roadmap parallel track · EU route, parallel to steps 2–9

    Rule 10 makes diagnostic ultrasound Class IIa, and the claim, not the hardware, is what can move it up. A device becomes Class IIb when it is specifically intended for monitoring vital physiological parameters where variations could result in immediate danger to the patient, or for diagnosis when the patient is in immediate danger; the Commission's guidance contrasts continuous surveillance in anaesthesia, intensive care or emergency care (IIb) with readings at routine check-ups (IIa). Whether a continuous hemodynamic-monitoring claim for a TEE system would be IIb is our example to discuss with your notified body, not a statement from the guidance. Standalone software (MDSW) is classified separately by Rule 11, from IIa for information used in diagnostic decisions upward with the severity of what those decisions could cause, and when several rules apply the strictest one decides.

    Regulatory detail: Rule 10 and Rule 11 text

    The Rule 10 monitoring examples are variations in cardiac performance, respiration and central nervous system activity; the contrast between continuous surveillance and routine check-ups is in MDCG 2021-24 rev.1. Rule 11: Class IIa when software provides information for diagnostic or therapeutic decisions, IIb when those decisions could cause a serious deterioration of health or a surgical intervention, III when they could cause death or irreversible deterioration. When several rules apply, the strictest rule giving the highest class decides (Annex VIII, 3.5).

    For Class IIa and IIb the notified body audits the QMS and assesses the technical documentation (TD) of representative devices, sampled per category of devices for IIa and per generic device group for IIb. The queue is what matters for planning: in the Commission's latest survey about half of notified bodies (51 %) reported 13–18 months to a new QMS-plus-product certificate and almost a third (31 %) reported 19–24 months. The EU route is therefore the long pole, to be planned in parallel and not as a follow-on to FDA clearance.

    Regulatory detail: TD sampling and the NB survey

    Per MDCG 2019-13 rev.1 (December 2024), at least one representative device per category of devices for IIa and per generic device group for IIb before the QMS certificate, with further sampling over the certificate's life. The Commission's 20th survey of notified bodies (data to 28 February 2026) counted 52 MDR-designated NBs, 32,898 applications and 18,010 certificates; for QMS plus product certification, 51 % of NBs reported 13–18 months to a new certificate and 31 % reported 19–24 months, while 65 % of applications reached a signed written agreement in under 2 months.

    36.12AI-enabled ultrasound features in a submission

    Roadmap step 3 of 9 · testing and standards (AI evidence; PCCP feeds step 9)

    The central demand is independence: test data sequestered from development and drawn from different sites, since a single site is "generally not appropriate". Ultrasound has its own leakage paths, and what follows is this module's engineering advice rather than guidance text. Consecutive frames and repeated clips of one patient are nearly identical, so a split by image puts the same patient in training and test; split by patient, and by exam date for longitudinal data. A site shares scanners, presets, sonographers and population, so hold out whole sites. Each probe model has its own transfer function and speckle size, so test every labeled probe, and if a probe had no development data, say so and show its results separately. A later collection period is a cheap check for drift. And once the test set has been used to choose between models, it has become development data.

    Subgroup claims add a sample-size question, because a subgroup result is only as useful as its confidence interval. For a proportion such as sensitivity, the 95 % half-width under the normal approximation is (36.6); invert it for the sample size needed to reach a target precision.

    (36.6)
    \(h\)
    half-width of the 95 % confidence interval (proportion)
    \(\hat{p},\ p\)

    \(n\)
    number of cases in the subgroup that the proportion is computed over (positives for sensitivity)

    The output also has to be used correctly.     3. An impact assessment: benefits, risks and their mitigations.

    36.13From clearance to commercial launch

    Roadmap step 7 of 9 · clearance, then step 8: launch

    The SE letter lets you sell, but it does not set up the systems that selling needs. FDA "does not typically perform 510(k) pre-clearance facility inspections", the submitter "may market the device immediately after 510(k) clearance is granted", and the manufacturer "should be prepared for an FDA quality system (21 CFR 820) inspection at any time after 510(k) clearance" (FDA's 510(k) page). Everything a launch needs therefore either has to be in place before the first commercial shipment or starts running on that day, and most of it can be prepared during the review, so that the gap between the letter and the first shipment is weeks rather than months.

    Before the first shipment the establishment is registered and the system and each transducer are listed with the 510(k) number, within 30 days of starting operations and renewed each year between 1 October and 31 December. Labels carry a unique device identifier (UDI), with the GUDID data due no later than the date the label must bear it, and every label, brochure and sales slide stays within the cleared indications, because promoting an uncleared use creates a new intended use that needs its own clearance. From the first shipment, two reporting clocks run: a Part 803 report within 30 calendar days of becoming aware of a reportable event (5 work days when remedial action is needed), and a Part 806 report within 10 working days of starting a correction or removal that reduces a risk to health. Production needs validated special processes, which for transducers means bonding, lens casting and stack lamination. A device made abroad also needs a U.S. agent, a registered importer and matching codes on each entry.

    Reimbursement runs on its own, slower track. Clearance creates no billing code and no coverage, so an exam on a new system is usually billed with the existing Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code, and a genuinely new measurement may need years to earn its own code, as ultrasound elastography did.

    36.16How experts think about it

    Experienced teams treat a submission as the last output of the design process, not a document project at the end. Three habits follow. First, every claim (an indication, a measurement, a mode) is a design input with a test and a trace, so the submission writes itself from the design history. Second, they locate the risk early: new quantitative modes, AI functions, new indications and anything outside the guidance's comfort zone get a Pre-Sub before verification, and the predicate argument is built cell by cell. Third, they plan in calendar time, with holds, response teams and notified-body queues in the schedule, and they read other teams' recalls as free test cases.

    36.18Interview and qualifying-exam questions

    I1Walk me through the regulatory path for a new handheld ultrasound with an AI view classifier, in the U.S. and the EU. · onsite

    U.S.: Class II, 510(k), likely IYN (892.1550) with ITX and IYO as subsequent codes and a software code for the AI function; Track 3 output package; a Pre-Sub on the AI validation plan (test set, sites, probes, acceptance criteria) and, if the AI may be retrained, a PCCP; software documentation level (Basic or Enhanced), HF category from a URRA (a phone-based user interface almost certainly has critical tasks), cyber-device obligations under 524B (app, cloud). EU: Rule 10 IIa for the probe system; if the AI is standalone MDSW, Rule 11 (IIa for diagnostic decision support unless decisions could cause serious deterioration); NB application early, TD and GSPR from the same design history, clinical evaluation with PMCF for the AI.

    Follow-up: what is the long pole? Usually the NB certificate (13–24 months reported) and the AI test-set collection.

    I2Which changes to a cleared ultrasound system can ship without a new 510(k) under the ultrasound guidance, and what disqualifies an attenuation-imaging update? · screen

    Section 5.1: all ten conditions must hold (unchanged intended use, not a reusable device needing new reprocessing data, only Table 2 modes, output within maxima, 1–20 MHz / ≤ 7 MPa / 1–100 cycles / PRF 100 Hz–20 kHz, no novel effects, described and editable measurements, element check, surface temperature per 60601-2-37, endocavity labeling). Examples: adding CW/PW Doppler, a similar new probe, a B-mode noise filter. Attenuation mapping is named in Table 2, Note 2 as outside the policy, so any change that introduces or affects it fails condition 3 regardless of output.

    Follow-up: then what? Apply the 2017 "Deciding When to Submit a 510(k)" guidance and document the decision; for a new or changed quantitative output that affects accuracy, expect a 510(k).

    I3How do you compute and report the statistical maximum MI for the output table, and where does measurement uncertainty enter? · onsite

    Measure MI on n production-representative probes (each at its own global-maximum setting found by the search of Module 22), then \(x_{\mathrm{stat}} = \bar{x} + ks\) with the one-sided normal tolerance factor for g = P = 0.9 (36.3): k = 2.066 for n = 10. Compare \(x_{\mathrm{stat}}\) with 1.9 (or check \(I_{\mathrm{SPPA.3}}\) ≤ 190). Uncertainty: if the combined budget is within ±15 % for MI (±30 % for intensities) no adjustment; above that, reduce the limit by the excess.

    Follow-up: why not just report the largest of the n values? With n = 10 the sample maximum covers roughly the 90th percentile only about 65 % of the time (1 − 0.9¹⁰); the tolerance limit gives a stated confidence.

    I4What makes a good Pre-Sub question? Rewrite "Do we need clinical data for our SWE mode?" · screen

    A specific proposal, the guidance or standard it relies on, and a yes/no outcome. Rewrite: "SWE is outside the 5.1 policy (Table 2, Note 2). We propose phantom accuracy testing of shear-wave speed on elasticity phantoms spanning __ to __ m/s at __ depths per transducer, acceptance criteria in Table 5, plus a retrospective agreement study against [reference] on __ subjects from __ sites, and no prospective clinical study. Does FDA agree this approach could support the indication in draft IFU §3?" Keep the Pre-Sub to 3–4 topics and 7–10 questions.

    Follow-up: what would you not ask? "What is our classification?" (513(g)) and "Please review our data" (not a pre-review).

    I5Your 510(k) gets an AI letter at day 60 with a major deficiency on Doppler velocity accuracy. Options and clocks? · onsite

    The clock stops at the letter; you have 180 calendar days to submit a complete response, or the 510(k) is considered withdrawn. If the fix is uncertain, send a Submission Issue Request within 60 days of the letter; FDA aims to give feedback in about 21 days. Technically: re-test on a flow or string phantom with the real transducers, measured versus actual velocity per transducer, never simulated or electronic data (5.2.5.1). Answer every deficiency explicitly, in the letter's order.

    Follow-up: how does this change the launch date? Calendar time = FDA days + hold days (36.5); a 100-day response adds 100 days even if FDA's own goal is met.

    I6Two candidate predicates match your intended use; one had a design-related recall. Which do you choose? · onsite

    Prefer the one without a design-related recall: FDA's draft predicate best practices (September 2023) list "no associated design-related recall" and no unmitigated safety issues among the criteria. If the recalled one is the better technological match, cite the other as primary and the recalled one only as a reference for a specific feature, showing that the recalled failure mode is addressed in your design and tests.

    Follow-up: where do you look? The Recall database and TPLC for the product code, plus MAUDE for failure modes (not rates).

    I7Is our cart system a cyber device, and what must the submission contain? · screen

    Likely yes: it includes sponsor-validated software, can connect to networks (DICOM, remote service, updates) and could be vulnerable. Under 524B the submission must contain a plan to monitor, identify and address postmarket vulnerabilities with coordinated disclosure, evidence of processes giving reasonable assurance of cybersecurity (regular and out-of-cycle patches) and an SBOM covering commercial, open-source and off-the-shelf components; the February 2026 guidance details threat modeling, architecture views and testing.

    Follow-up: what changes after clearance? Patching and vulnerability handling become part of postmarket obligations; plan the update path in the design.

    I8How would you validate an AI segmentation feature to a reviewer's satisfaction? What did the K261835 summary disclose and what was missing? · research

    Pre-specified endpoints and acceptance criteria; a test set sequestered from training at the patient level, from multiple sites, scanners and probes, covering the intended population and subgroups; reference standard from qualified readers with a documented protocol; report confidence intervals and failure cases. K261835 disclosed, for one feature, 728 images from 67 subjects, one probe, acceptance ≥ 0.80, results 93.8 % accuracy, sensitivity 0.94, specificity 0.99, Dice 0.85, and no train/test overlap. Visible gaps: a single probe and no stated site count.

    Follow-up: how would a PCCP change this? It pre-authorizes defined retraining with a modification protocol and impact assessment, so the validation design becomes part of the authorized plan.

    I9Name three ultrasound recall root-cause categories and the design control that addresses each. · screen

    Thermal behaviour in a specific mode or function combination (surface temperature tests over all transducer × mode × UI-function combinations, plus fault injection on the thermal guard); a quantitative value displayed wrongly after a software change (regression of quantitative outputs on reference phantoms as a release gate); production test escapes (leakage and hi-pot test procedure validation, test-record release gates, incoming inspection of insulation components). Labeling, reprocessing and supplier-contaminated gel are three more.

    Follow-up: why are such records useful even though they are not rates? They are concrete failure modes with known causes, ideal test cases.

    I10Why can you not use MAUDE counts to compare the failure rates of two probes? · screen

    No denominator (installed base and use hours unknown), passive and under-reported, reporting practices differ between firms and over time, duplicates and incomplete reports exist, and a report does not establish causation. FDA states MDR data should not be used to evaluate rates or compare devices.

    Follow-up: what would you use instead? Your own complaint and service data with installed-base denominators, or a designed field study.

    I11Under the MDR, when could a diagnostic ultrasound device be Class IIb, and how does Rule 11 interact with embedded AI? · research

    Rule 10 IIb: specifically intended for monitoring vital physiological parameters where variations could result in immediate danger, or for diagnosis in clinical situations where the patient is in immediate danger. A continuous ICU or theatre monitoring claim could trigger it; whether it does for a given TEE claim is a question for the NB. AI embedded in the device's software is part of the device and takes its class; AI marketed as standalone MDSW is classified by Rule 11 (IIa for diagnostic decision information unless decisions could cause serious deterioration or surgery, IIb, or death or irreversible deterioration, III). The strictest applicable rule decides.

    Follow-up: what changes at IIb? TD sampling per generic device group and annual PSURs.

    I12What does a notified body expect in a GSPR checklist that a list of test reports does not give, and can you claim equivalence to a competitor's device? · research

    For each applicable requirement: the method of conformity, the standard and its edition (with a gap analysis if not current or not harmonised), the rationale when a requirement does not apply, and the exact location of the evidence; consistency of intended purpose, variants and Basic UDI-DI across documents (Team-NB best-practice paper). Equivalence to another manufacturer's Class IIa device is allowed under MDCG 2020-5 with technical, biological and clinical equivalence and sufficient access to the data; the contract requirement of Art. 61(5) applies only to implantable and Class III devices.

    Follow-up: what changed for U.S. firms on 2 February 2026? The QMSR took effect, incorporating ISO 13485:2016, which brings the U.S. and EU quality systems much closer.

    I13Your team wants to replace the SWE reconstruction on a cleared system with a faster algorithm that "gives the same numbers". New 510(k) or letter to file? · onsite

    Start from 21 CFR 807.81(a)(3) and the 2017 software guidance, comparing with the most recently cleared version. SWE is outside the ultrasound 5.1 policy (Table 2, Note 2), so that shortcut is closed. A new reconstruction is a core-algorithm change, and shear-wave speed bias and precision are performance specifications tied to the intended use: software question 4 is likely yes, and the guidance warns that even a complete rewrite with the same performance claims can need a submission. Plan a 510(k) (Special only if the phantom methods are well established and the indications are unchanged) with bias and precision over the stiffness and depth range per probe. If the push pulse changes, condition 6 (novel mechanical effects) also needs assessing. Follow-up: "What goes in the letter to file if you conclude no 510(k)?" (Each question answered with its rationale, the risk update, verification against pre-set criteria and the cumulative change list.)

    I14Design the test set for an AI view classifier that will be labeled for three phased-array probes. What would make a reviewer doubt its independence? · research

    Split by patient, never by frame or clip; hold out whole sites (the draft AI guidance wants test data from sites different from development, and a single site is "generally not appropriate"); include all three probes and report per-probe results; collect later than the development data; annotate the reference blinded to the AI output; and never use the test set for model selection. Size it for the subgroup claims: at sensitivity 0.90, ±0.05 needs about 139 positives per subgroup by the Wald approximation (36.6). Report sex, age, race, ethnicity, body mass index, disease severity, site and equipment. Follow-up: "How would a PCCP let you add a fourth probe later?" (Description of modifications naming the probe type, a protocol with sequestered new-probe test data and acceptance criteria, an impact assessment.)

    I15You receive the SE letter on Monday. What must be true before the first system ships, and which reporting duties apply from then on? · screen

    Registration and listing done (within 30 days of starting operations; the new device listed with its 510(k) number); UDI on labels and GUDID data submitted by the UDI date; labeling and promotional material limited to the cleared indications; a released production lot from validated processes; complaint handling, Part 803 and Part 806 procedures live; service and installation ready; for an imported device, a U.S. agent, a registered initial importer and correct DEV/LST/PM# codes on the entry. FDA does not typically inspect before a 510(k) clearance, but can inspect at any time after it. From the first shipment: Part 803 reports within 30 calendar days of becoming aware of a reportable event (5 work days for remedial-action events), Part 806 reports within 10 working days of starting a correction or removal, annual registration 1 October–31 December. Follow-up: "Can sales quote the new attenuation feature for fatty-liver staging?" (Only if that claim is in the cleared indications.)

    Q1MI measured on 5 probes: x̄ = 1.55, s = 0.08. Statistical maximum at g = P = 0.9? Within Track 3?

    Yes: the intensity limit holds and the pressure-related condition is met through the \(I_{\mathrm{SPPA.3}} \le 190\) branch (36.1). Expect the reviewer to look closely at how \(I_{\mathrm{SPPA.3}}\) was derived.

    Q3You add a sound-speed estimate to a cleared system. Does the section 5.1 policy cover it?

    No. Sound speed measurement is named in Table 2, Note 2 as outside the policy; apply the 2017 change guidance and plan for a 510(k) with phantom accuracy data, likely after a Pre-Sub.

    At least 5 days before the meeting and no later than day 70, so by day 69; the sponsor submits draft minutes within 15 days of the meeting, by day 89.

    \(T_{\mathrm{FDA}}\) = 55 + 28 = 83 (within the 90-day goal); \(T_{\mathrm{cal}}\) = 83 + 140 = 223 (36.5).

    Q7Your Class IIa ultrasound system is involved in an incident that caused an unanticipated serious deterioration of a patient's health. EU reporting deadline?

    Immediately, and no later than 10 days after awareness (Art. 87; MDCG 2023-3 rev.2). Day 0 is when the first person in your organization received the information.

    At least every two years (Art. 86; MDCG 2022-21). Since 28 May 2026: actor registration, UDI/device registration, notified bodies and certificates, and market surveillance.

    Q9A software change is made solely to patch an operating-system vulnerability, with no other effect. Where does the software flowchart send it, and must it be reported under Part 806?

    Question 1 (solely cybersecurity, no other impact) points to documentation, not a new 510(k). Under the 2016 postmarket cybersecurity guidance a patch that strengthens security against a controlled risk is an enhancement, not a reportable correction; remediating an uncontrolled risk ordinarily is reportable under Part 806 within 10 working days, subject to the guidance's enforcement-discretion conditions.

    Q10From which date do the AI Act's high-risk requirements apply to an AI function in a Class IIa ultrasound system, and why is it high-risk at all?

    From 2 August 2028 (Art. 113 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744; originally 2 August 2027). It is high-risk under Art. 6(1) because the MDR is Annex I legislation and a Class IIa device needs third-party (notified-body) conformity assessment; the AI Act requirements are assessed inside the MDR procedure (Art. 43(3)).
