16.1The piezoelectric effect and the constitutive equations

A poled ferroelectric ceramic (PZT) or crystal (PMN-PT) carries a remanent polarization along its poling axis, labelled 3. Stress changes that polarization, and the change appears as charge on the electrodes: the direct effect, used on receive. A field changes the strain: the converse effect, used on transmit. For small signals both are linear and, by thermodynamics, governed by the same coefficient, which is why one device is both the speaker and the microphone and why its transmit and receive responses are linked by reciprocity.

Four field variables are in play: stress T (Pa), strain S, electric field E (V/m) and electric displacement D (C/m²). Pick any two as independent and you get one of four equivalent forms. Superscripts say what is held constant: E (short circuit), D (open circuit), T (free), S (clamped).

Why D is the right variable inside a plate. The electrodes are equipotential and there is no free charge inside the dielectric, so Gauss's law gives \(\partial D_3 /\partial z = 0\): \(D_3\) is uniform through the thickness while the strain varies with z. Two consequences carry the whole module. First, in the wave equation \(\partial T_3 /\partial z = \rho \partial^2 u/\partial t^2\), the uniform \(h_{33} D_{3}\) term drops out, so waves inside the plate travel at the stiffened speed \(v^D\) and the plate is simply an acoustic transmission line. The piezoelectric coupling acts only at the two faces, where the term \(h_{33} D_{3}\) jumps to zero outside the plate. Second, integrating \(E_3\) across the thickness t gives the terminal voltage:

\[ \begin{aligned} V &= \int_0^t E_3\,dz = \frac{D_3\,t}{\varepsilon^{S}} - h_{33}\,[u(t) - u(0)] \\ \Rightarrow\ V &= \frac{I}{j\omega C_0} - \frac{h_{33}}{j\omega}\,(v_t - v_0), \\ C_0 &= \frac{\varepsilon^{S} A}{t} \end{aligned} \]

Intuition

An open-circuited piezoelectric is stiffer because straining it builds charge whose field pushes back. Shorting the electrodes lets the charge flow away and removes that extra stiffness. The difference between the two stiffnesses is the coupling: \(c^E = c^D (1 - k^2)\). Dually, a clamped plate cannot move, so it stores less charge per volt: \(\varepsilon^S = \varepsilon^{\mathrm{free}} (1 - k^2)\) for that mode, where \(\varepsilon^{\mathrm{free}}\) is free only along that mode (for the thickness mode, \(C_{\mathrm{LF}} t/A\) of (16.6)), not the fully free tabulated \(\varepsilon_{33}^T\) (for PZT-5H \(\varepsilon_{33}^S/\varepsilon_{33}^T = 0.43\), not \(1 - k_t^2 = 0.74\)).

16.2Coupling coefficients and what k² means

A coupling coefficient is a ratio of energies. Drive the material quasi-statically through one cycle on one port, and \(k^2\) is the fraction of the stored energy that can be taken out on the other port. Equivalently it is the normalized cross term of the energy density, so it depends on the mode, that is, on which stresses and strains the geometry lets be zero.

\[ \begin{gathered} k_{33}^2 = \frac{d_{33}^2}{s_{33}^{E}\,\varepsilon_{33}^{T}}, \qquad k_{31}^2 = \frac{d_{31}^2}{s_{11}^{E}\,\varepsilon_{33}^{T}} \\ k_t^2 = \frac{e_{33}^2}{c_{33}^{D}\,\varepsilon_{33}^{S}} = \frac{h_{33}^2\,\varepsilon_{33}^{S}}{c_{33}^{D}} \end{gathered} \]

Pitfall

Quoting \(k_{33} = 0.75\) (or 0.92 for PMN-PT) for an array element is the most common overstatement in transducer talk. A wide element works at \(k_t\), a well-diced sliver approaches \(k_{33}^{\prime}\), and only a pillar reaches \(k_{33}\). Also keep \(k_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (from \(f_r /f_a\) of any resonance) apart from the material constant \(k_t\).

Why 1-3 composites win. Thin ceramic pillars in a soft polymer matrix are laterally unclamped, so they work closer to the bar mode \((k_{33} \approx 0.75)\) than to the plate mode (0.51), while the polymer lowers the density and stiffness. 16.4Thickness-mode resonance and the impedance of a plate

16.5The Mason and KLM equivalent circuits

Both circuits are three-port networks (one electrical port, two acoustic ports for the back and front faces) that reproduce the plate equations exactly in 1D. In the acoustic ports "voltage" is force F = pA and "current" is particle velocity u, so an acoustic impedance in MRayl becomes a mechanical impedance \(Z = \rho vA\) in kg/s. Mason (1942/1948) puts the coupling at the electrical side with a transformer and a negative capacitance; Krimholtz, Leedom and Matthaei (Electronics Letters, 1970) move it to the middle of the acoustic line.

Why KLM is the working model

In KLM the acoustic path is an unbroken transmission line, so backing, bond lines, matching layers, a lens and the load just hang off its ends as more line sections. The electromechanical physics sits in two frequency-dependent elements (\(X_1\), \(\varphi\)) at one node. The sinc in \(\varphi\) also tells you at a glance which harmonics are excited: \(\varphi = 0\) at \(f = 2f_a\), \(4f_a\), … because a uniform field across the thickness cannot drive a mode whose strain integrates to zero.

16.8Electrical tuning, cables and source impedance

A small array element is a high-impedance capacitor (750 Ω at 5 MHz for the reference element) facing a 50 Ω pulser and preamplifier through a cable. The electrical port wastes power by mismatch exactly as an unmatched acoustic face does, and three standard fixes attack different parts of the mismatch:

  • Series inductor \(L = 1/(\omega_m^2 C_0)\) cancels the series reactance and leaves the series resistance (346 Ω at 4.5 MHz for the reference element). Right when the source is low impedance.
  • Shunt inductor, same formula with \(C_0\) (plus the cable capacitance if it sits at the far end), resonates the parallel capacitance. Right when the receiver is high impedance; across a 50 Ω source it changes the reference design by only 0.03 dB because a 50 Ω source already behaves like a voltage source to a 750 Ω element.
  • Transformer (or an L-network) scales the remaining real part toward 50 Ω. A 1 : 2.5 turns ratio (\(n = 0.4\) system : element, impedance ratio 6.25) on top of the series inductor takes the reference design from −10.1 dB to −2.6 dB.

Cables: coaxial cable of characteristic impedance \(Z_c\) and velocity \(v_c\) has capacitance \(1/(Z_c v_c)\) per metre: 100 pF/m for 50 Ω at 2×10⁸ m/s, so a 2 m probe cable carries 200 pF, about five times \(C_0\). If the system impedance equals \(Z_c\), a lossless cable only adds delay: the source sees the same available power and the mismatch at the element is unchanged, so \(\lvert H\rvert\) does not move. If the receiver is high impedance, the cable capacitance divides the voltage: \(C_0 /(C_0 + C_{\mathrm{cable}}) = -15\) dB for 2 m in the lumped open-circuit limit, −9.6 dB in the full line model with a 1 kΩ receiver. That is the reason for preamplifiers in the probe handle or micro-beamforming ASICs at the element (Module 19).

16.10How experts think about it

A transducer engineer sees the plate as a half-wave acoustic line whose two faces are driven by the uniform charge, coupled to a capacitor \(C_0\) through a frequency-dependent transformer. Bandwidth comes from how hard the faces are loaded (backing, and the load transformed by matching layers) and from \(k_t\), sensitivity from how much of that load is useful (the front) and from how well \(C_0\) is matched to the electronics. Every number on a datasheet is a consequence of \(Z_p\), \(k_t\), \(\varepsilon^S\), thickness, area and those loads.

\(k^2\) is the fraction of the stored energy that crosses between the electrical and mechanical sides.

Hold a strain S open-circuited: stored energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}c^D S^2\). Short the electrodes at fixed S: the stress drops to \(c^E S\) and the energy \(\tfrac{1}{2}(c^D - c^E)S^2 = k^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}c^D S^2\) leaves as charge (Demo 16.1). 16.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What does \(k_t^2 = 0.26\) mean physically, and why is it not "26 % efficiency"? · screen

In a quasi-static cycle, 26 % of the energy stored on one side can be extracted on the other; equivalently \(c^E = 0.74 c^D\) and \(\varepsilon^S = 0.74\,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{free}}\) (permittivity free only along the thickness, \(C_{\mathrm{LF}} t/A\); the fully free \(\varepsilon_{33}^T\) is larger) for that mode. It is a coupling, not an efficiency: a lossless transducer converts all the absorbed electrical power to acoustic power at matched conditions; \(k^2\) decides how wide the band over which that can be done is (the \(f_r \text{–}f_a\) gap) and how hard you can load the plate before sensitivity collapses. Follow-up: "So what limits efficiency?" Mismatch at the electrical port, backing absorption, and material losses (\(Q_m\), \(\tan \delta\)).

I2Why is \(k_t\) of PZT-5H 0.51 when \(k_{33}\) is 0.75? · screen

Boundary conditions. \(k_{33}\) is for a bar with free lateral faces \((T_1 = T_2 = 0)\), \(k_t\) for a plate clamped laterally by its own extent \((S_1 = S_2 = 0)\). In the plate the \(d_{31}\) lateral contraction is blocked, so the stiffness and permittivity that enter are \(c_{33}^D\) and \(\varepsilon_{33}^S\), and the coupling drops. A sliver frees one direction \((k_{33}^{\prime} \approx 0.70)\), a composite pillar both. Follow-up: "What width-to-thickness ratio does a sliver need?" ≲ 0.6, to push the width mode well above the thickness mode.

I3An air-loaded PZT-5H plate shows \(\lvert Z\rvert\) minimum at 4.41 MHz and maximum at 5.00 MHz. What are \(k_t\) and the thickness? · onsite

\(f_r /f_a = 0.882\), \(x_r = (\pi /2)(0.882) = 1.385\), \(k_t^2 = x \cot x = 0.260\), \(k_t = 0.51\). Thickness from the antiresonance: \(t = v^D /(2f_a) = 4600/(10^{7}) = 0.46\) mm. Small-gap shortcut would give \(k_t = 0.54\). Follow-up: "And if the plate were already bonded to a backing?" Then the extrema shift and broaden; the IEEE formula no longer applies, fit a KLM model instead.

I4Sketch the KLM model and give its element values. Why do transducer designers prefer it to Mason? · onsite

Electrical port: \(C_0 = \varepsilon^S A/t\) in series with \(jX_1\), \(X_1 = (k_t^2 /\omega C_0)\operatorname{sinc} (f/f_a)\), then a transformer \(\varphi = k_t \sqrt{\pi /(\omega_0 C_{0}Z_{0})}\operatorname{sinc} (f/2f_a)\) feeding the centre of an acoustic line of length t, impedance \(Z_0 = \rho v^D A\), split into two halves to the back and front ports. Preferred because the acoustic path stays a continuous line: backing, bond lines, matching layers and the load attach as more line sections or as ABCD factors, and the frequency dependence of the coupling is explicit. Follow-up: "Show me it agrees with Mason." Free plate: \(X_1 + \varphi^2 (Z_0 /2)\tan (kt/2) = (k_t^2 /\omega C_0)\tan x/x\).

I5Why does a thickness transducer have a passband near \(3f_0\) but none near \(2f_0\)? · onsite

The electric field is uniform across the thickness, so it drives the plate only through the face terms h D; an even mode moves both faces in the same direction and its strain integrates to zero, giving no net drive. In KLM, \(\varphi \propto \sin (kt/2)\) is zero at \(2f_a\), and \(\varphi^2\) at \(3f_a\) is sinc²(1.5)/sinc²(0.5) = 1/9 of its value at \(f_a\). Follow-up: "How would you build a dual-band probe?" Stack two plates or use the third harmonic deliberately, or a CMUT; mention superharmonic contrast imaging.

I6An array element shows 750 Ω capacitive at 5 MHz and the system is 50 Ω. What do you do, and what do you gain? · onsite

Cancel \(C_0\) with a series inductor \(L = 1/(\omega^2 C_0)\) (29.5 µH at 4.5 MHz for 42.4 pF), leaving \(R \approx 346\) Ω, then transform the real part toward 50 Ω (1 : 2.5 turns). In the reference design that is −21.6 → −10.1 → −2.6 dB two-way. A shunt inductor alone does nothing useful against a 50 Ω source. Follow-up: "Cost?" Inductor Q and size in the connector, narrower band if the tuning Q exceeds the acoustic Q, and element-to-element tolerance.

I7Why does a heavier backing broaden the band and lower sensitivity? Give a formula. · screen

The plate is a half-wave resonator losing ≈ \(4(Z_B + Z_F)/Z_p\) of its energy per period, so \(Q \approx (\pi /2)Z_p /(Z_B + Z_F)\): 36 air-backed into water, 8.3 at 5 MRayl. The energy that leaves through the back is absorbed, so the forward fraction \(Z_F /(Z_B + Z_F)\) drops and the two-way loss grows about as its square. Follow-up: "Then how do you get 70 % bandwidth at good sensitivity?" Matching layers raise the effective front load; use light backing.

I8Your measured impedance of a kerfed array element shows an extra resonance a little above the thickness resonance that KLM does not predict. What is it, and what do you do? · research

A lateral (width) mode of the element, or of the kerf-filler/element structure, coupling through \(d_{31}\) when the width-to-thickness ratio is not small enough (≈ 0.6 or less separates them). KLM is 1D and cannot show it. Sub-dice the element, change the kerf filler, or move to a composite; verify with 2D/3D finite elements (Module 17) and a measured impedance sweep of every element. Follow-up: "Why does it hurt imaging?" It narrows and distorts the pulse spectrum, adds ringing, and increases crosstalk.

I9Why do single-crystal and composite probes have wider bandwidth than PZT probes with the same matching? · research

Higher \(k_t\) (0.6–0.85 versus 0.5) widens the \(f_r \text{–}f_a\) gap, i.e. the intrinsic electromechanical bandwidth, so the plate can be loaded more heavily for the same sensitivity; composites also lower Z (10–20 MRayl), which makes the transformation to 1.5 MRayl easier with fewer layers. Follow-up: "Any downside?" Crystal: \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) and \(E_c\) limits, cost, property spread; composite: polymer loss, lateral modes if not fine enough, and lower \(\varepsilon\) raising the electrical impedance.

I10How would you build a KLM model of a real, lossy element from measurements? · research

Measure the bare plate: \(C_0\) well above resonance gives \(\varepsilon^S\); \(f_a\) and t give \(v^D\); \(f_s\), \(f_p\) give \(k_t\) by IEEE 176; peak widths give \(Q_m\) and the off-resonance phase gives \(\tan \delta\) (or fit complex constants to the whole curve, as in Sherrit's method). Then measure each layer's Z and attenuation separately, build the ABCD chain, and compare the predicted electrical impedance of the finished element and the pulse-echo response against measurements, adjusting bond-line thickness last. Follow-up: "What will not fit?" Lateral modes, kerf crosstalk and lens effects: move to FE.

I11You designed a 5 MHz half-wave plate and the pulse-echo centre is 4.3 MHz. Name the causes and how you would separate them. · onsite

(1) Low-impedance drive and receive put the response near \(f_r = 0.88 f_a\), not \(f_a\). (2) Matching layers designed below \(f_a\) and their mass load the face. (3) The tuning inductor resonates with \(C_0\). (4) Lens and tissue attenuation downshift further in practice. Separate them with the model: switch off tuning, vary layer design frequency, change the receiver impedance, and compare with the measured impedance curve. Follow-up: "How do you hit 5 MHz?" Thin the plate (raise \(f_a\) to ≈ 5.7 MHz) and re-centre the layers.

16.14Self-check

Q1What PZT-5H thickness gives \(f_a = 7.5\) MHz, and where will the bare-plate \(f_r\) be?

\(t = 4600/(2 \times 7.5\times 10^{6}) = 0.307\) mm. \(f_r = 0.882 \times 7.5 = 6.61\) MHz for \(k_t = 0.51\).

Q2\(C_0\) and \(\lvert Z\rvert\) at 5 MHz for a 10 mm diameter, 0.46 mm thick PZT-5H disc?

\(A = 78.5\) mm², \(C_0 = 1470 \times 8.854\times 10^{-12} \times 78.5\times 10^{-6}\) \(/\, 0.46\times 10^{-3} = 2.22\) nF, \(\lvert Z\rvert = 1/(2\pi \times 5\times 10^{6} \times 2.22\times 10^{-9}) = 14.3\) Ω: too low for 50 Ω, so a large element wants a step-up transformer (n > 1).

Q3A crystal plate has \(f_r /f_a = 0.749\). What is \(k_t\), and what does the small-gap formula say?

\(x = (\pi /2)(0.749) = 1.176\), \(k_t^2 = x \cot x = 0.490\), \(k_t = 0.70\). Small gap: \((\pi^2 /4)(0.251) = 0.620\), \(k_t = 0.79\), 12 % high.

Q4Estimate Q for a PZT-5H plate (34.5 MRayl) into water with air backing, and with a 5 MRayl backing.

\((\pi /2)(34.5/1.5) = 36\); \((\pi /2)(34.5/6.5) = 8.3\). Fractional −3 dB bandwidths ≈ 3 % and 12 % (one-way, open circuit).

Q5Why does a 2 m, 50 Ω cable not change \(\lvert H\rvert\) in a 50 Ω system, yet costs ≈ 10 dB into a 1 kΩ preamp?

A lossless line terminated at the system end by its own impedance only rotates phase: available power and reflection magnitude at the element are unchanged. Into a high-impedance input the line looks like 200 pF in parallel with the element, dividing its voltage by about \(C_0 /(C_0 + C_{\mathrm{cable}})\) (−15 dB lumped, −9.6 dB with 1 kΩ in the full model).

Q6A PZT-5H/soft-epoxy composite at \(\nu = 0.3\): \(k_t\) and Z from Smith–Auld? Why is \(\varepsilon\) lower?

\(k_t \approx 0.67\), \(Z \approx 11.0\) MRayl (\(v^D \approx 3610\) m/s). \(\bar{\varepsilon}^S \approx 457 \varepsilon_0\) because only 30 % of the area is ceramic, so \(C_0\) and the electrical impedance change by ≈ 3× for the same element.

Q7Which tuning inductor do you use in front of a 50 Ω pulser, and which at the input of a high-impedance preamp at the end of a cable?

Series \(L = 1/(\omega^2 C_0)\) for the 50 Ω source (cancel the series reactance), then a transformer for the real part. Shunt \(L = 1/(\omega^2 (C_0 + C_{\mathrm{cable}}))\) at the high-impedance input, to resonate the total capacitance.
