The DFT and FFT, from correlation to butterflies

Every FFT bin is the correlation of your signal with one rotating arrow. This module builds the DFT from that single idea, computes it by hand for N = 4 and N = 8, states exactly what the real and imaginary part of each bin mean, and then derives the radix-2 FFT one butterfly at a time.

You will be able to

  • Compute \(X[k]\) by hand and read \(\mathrm{Re}\,X\), \(\mathrm{Im}\,X\), \(\lvert X\rvert\) and \(\angle X\) as cosine content, sine content, amplitude and phase
  • Predict bin values for a tone (\(A N/2\)), a constant (\(N c\)) and the Nyquist bin, and explain why the upper half mirrors
  • Explain leakage, scalloping (−3.92 dB) and zero padding with the drifting-probe picture
  • Derive the radix-2 FFT, draw the N = 8 butterfly with bit-reversed input, and count \(N\log_2 N\) vs \(N^2\)
  • Build correct frequency axes in MATLAB with fft, fftshift and odd or even N

02.1The inner product: how much of this shape is in my signal?

Take a signal \(x[n]\) and a probe shape \(p[n]\), both N samples long. Multiply sample by sample and add. The result is the inner product:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle x, p\rangle &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,p[n] && (\text{real } p) \\ \langle x, p\rangle &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,p^{*}[n] && (\text{complex } p) \\ c_p &= \frac{\langle x, p\rangle}{\langle p, p\rangle} \end{aligned} \]
(02.1)
\(x[n]\)
signal samples, n = 0 … N − 1
\(p[n]\)
probe shape (template), same length
\(p^{*}\)
complex conjugate of the probe
\(c_p\)
projection coefficient: how many copies of p are in x
\(\langle x,p\rangle\)
inner product: multiply sample by sample and sum
\(N\)
record length, samples
\(n\)
sample index, 0 … N−1
\(\sum\)
sum over the index shown
Large and positive: x looks like p. Large and negative: x looks like −p. Near zero: p is not in x. Dividing by \(\langle p, p\rangle\) gives "how many copies of p", the projection coefficient.

You already use this every day. DAS beamforming is an inner product of the channel data with a delay-and-apodization pattern; a matched filter is an inner product with the pulse at each lag. A DFT bin is the same operation with a sampled sinusoid as the probe.

By hand: four samples, one cosine probe

Let \(x = [1, 2, 3, 4]\) and the probe be one cycle of cosine over four samples, \(c[n] = \cos(2\pi n/4) = [1, 0, -1, 0]\). Products: \([1\cdot 1,\ 2\cdot 0,\ 3\cdot(-1),\ 4\cdot 0]\) \(= [1, 0, -3, 0]\). Sum: \(\langle x, c\rangle = -2\). Since \(\langle c, c\rangle = 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 2\), x contains \(-2/2 =\) −1 times that cosine. With the sine probe \(s[n] = \sin(2\pi n/4) = [0, 1, 0, -1]\): products \([0, 2, 0, -4]\), sum \(\langle x, s\rangle = -2\), so x also contains −1 times the sine. The figure draws both.

Key idea

An inner product answers one question about one shape. The DFT asks that question N times, once per probe frequency, with probes chosen so that the answers do not interfere (02.3).

02.2One probe is not enough: correlate with a cosine and a sine at once

Suppose \(x[n] = A\cos(2\pi k_0 n/N + \varphi)\) with unknown amplitude A and phase \(\varphi\), and \(0 < k_0 < N/2\) an integer. Correlate it with a cosine at the same frequency. Use \(\cos a\cos b\) \(= \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)]\) with \(\theta = 2\pi k_0 n/N\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{n} A\cos(\theta + \varphi)\cos\theta &= \frac{A}{2}\sum_{n}\big[\cos\varphi + \cos(2\theta + \varphi)\big] \\ &= \frac{AN}{2}\cos\varphi + 0 \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{n} A\cos(\theta + \varphi)\sin\theta &= \frac{A}{2}\sum_{n}\big[\sin(2\theta + \varphi) - \sin\varphi\big] \\ &= -\frac{AN}{2}\sin\varphi \end{aligned} \]

02.3Why the probes do not interfere: orthogonality of the roots of unity

The DFT uses the N probes \(e^{j2\pi kn/N}\), k = 0 … N − 1. Correlating probe k with probe l gives a sum of the arrows \(e^{j2\pi(k-l)n/N}\). Let \(d = k - l\) and \(r = e^{j2\pi d/N}\), so the terms are \(r^0, r^1, \dots, r^{N-1}\). The geometric series gives

\[ \begin{gathered} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} r^{n} = \frac{1 - r^{N}}{1 - r} = 0 \quad (r^N = e^{j2\pi d} = 1,\ r \ne 1) \\ \Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{\,j2\pi(k-l)n/N} = N\,\delta[k-l] \end{gathered} \]
(02.5)
\(d = k - l\)
difference of the two probe indices, integer
\(r\)

\(\delta[\cdot]\)

\(N\)

\(n\)

\(k,\ l\)

\(j\)

If d is a multiple of N, r = 1, every term is 1 and the sum is N. Otherwise \(r \ne 1\) and the sum is zero. So the N probes are mutually orthogonal, each with energy N.

Geometrically the terms are the N-th roots of unity, visited in steps of d. Placed tip to tail they walk around a closed polygon and return to the start, so the sum is zero. The cards draw it for N = 8.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, a component at bin l contributes nothing to any other bin \(k \ne l\), which is why a bin-centred tone lands in its own bin and nowhere else. Second, the transform can be undone. Multiply \(X[k]\) by \(e^{+j2\pi km/N}\), sum over k, and swap the order of summation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k} X[k]\,e^{j2\pi km/N} &= \sum_{n} x[n] \sum_{k} e^{j2\pi k(m-n)/N} \\ &= \sum_{n} x[n]\cdot N\,\delta[m-n] = N\,x[m] \end{aligned} \]
(02.6)
\(X[k]\)
DFT of \(x\), as in (02.4)
\(x[n]\)

\(m,\ n\)

So \(x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_k X[k]\,e^{+j2\pi kn/N}\): the inverse DFT (02.13). The 1/N comes from the probe energy N.
Pitfall

Orthogonality needs whole periods inside the N-sample record. A tone at 3.5 bins is not one of the N probes, so it is not orthogonal to its neighbours and spreads into all bins (02.7). Sine and cosine at the same bin are also orthogonal, \(\sum\cos\theta\sin\theta = \tfrac12\sum\sin 2\theta = 0\), except at k = 0 and k = N/2, where the sine probe is identically zero.

The last step needs x real (\(x^{*} = x\)). So Re X is even about N/2, Im X is odd, \(\lvert X\rvert\) is even, \(\angle X\) is odd. Bin N − k is frequency −k: \(e^{-j2\pi(N-k)n/N}\) and \(e^{+j2\pi kn/N}\) are the same samples, the aliasing of Module 04 inside the DFT itself.

Physically: a real cosine is the sum of two counter-rotating arrows, \(\cos\theta = (e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta})/2\) (Module 01). The DFT reports each arrow in its own bin, k for the positive one and N − k for the negative one, each with half the amplitude. Their imaginary parts are opposite and cancel in x[n].

DC and Nyquist are real

The probe for k = 0 is all ones and the probe for k = N/2 is \((-1)^n\); both are real, so \(X[0]\) and \(X[N/2]\) are real for real x. The sine probe at Nyquist is \(\sin(\pi n) = 0\) at every sample, so the sine part of a Nyquist tone is invisible: \(A\cos(\pi n + \varphi) = A\cos\varphi\cdot(-1)^n\). Its phase cannot be recovered, only \(A\cos\varphi\).

Ultrasound link

An RF line of N = 1024 samples at \(f_s\) = 40 MHz has bins \(\Delta f = f_s/N\) = 39.06 kHz apart. A 5 MHz probe with a 3–7 MHz band occupies bins 77–179 and their mirror 845–947; the rest is noise. Complex I/Q data break the mirror symmetry: after demodulation (Module 08) the bins above N/2 are genuine negative frequencies, which is how Doppler separates flow toward and away from the probe.

Padded zeros add nothing to \(\sum x[n]e^{-j\omega n}\); the padded DFT samples the same DTFT on a finer grid (02.17). Two equal, in-phase tones 0.5 bin apart in 16 samples stay one lobe at any padding (left); 2 bins apart they separate (right). Near 1/T the outcome depends on the tones' relative phase (two peaks for 25 % of phases at 0.5 bin, 58 % at 1 bin, all at 1.5 bins), and padding changes neither.

Exact formula
Padding does help locate an isolated peak, display a smooth spectrum, and avoid circular wrap in FFT convolution (pad to L + M − 1).
The FFT is the DFT split into even and odd samples, recursively.
\(X[k] = E[k] + W^kO[k]\)\(X[k+N/2] = E[k] - W^kO[k]\)

\(W_N^2 = W_{N/2}\) turns each half into an N/2-point DFT, and \(W_N^{k+N/2} = -W_N^k\) lets one product serve two outputs (02.20). For x = [1, 2, 3, 4]: E = [4, −2], O = [6, −2], X = [10, −2 + 2j, −2, −2 − 2j].

Schematic
Radix-2 needs \(N = 2^p\). Other lengths use mixed radix or Bluestein; libraries such as FFTW handle any N.
N log N, not N²: a 1024-point FFT needs about 200 times fewer multiplies.
1 048 576 vs 5 120ratio \(2N/\log_2 N\)

\(\log_2 N\) stages × N/2 butterflies × 1 multiply = \((N/2)\log_2 N\) (02.21). At N = 1024 that is 5 120 against 1 048 576, a ratio of 205; at N = 65 536 it is 8 192.

Exact formula
Real run time at large N is set by memory traffic, not multiplies. To get a few bins only, direct correlation (or the Goertzel recursion) costs N per bin and can win.
Decimation in time reads the input in bit-reversed order.
N = 8: 0 4 2 6 1 5 3 7

Each even/odd split sorts by the lowest remaining bit of n, so after \(\log_2 N\) splits position p holds sample bitrev(p): 1 = 001 goes to 100 = 4, 3 = 011 goes to 110 = 6. One permutation, then in-place butterflies with natural-order output.

Schematic
Decimation in frequency moves the reversal to the output; autosort (Stockham) variants avoid it at the cost of a second buffer.
fft returns bins 0 … N−1; above N/2 they are negative frequencies, and fftshift puts DC in the middle.
X(1) = DC\(f_k = k f_s/N,\ k < N/2\)\((k-N) f_s/N,\ k > N/2\)

For N = 8 at \(f_s\) = 40 MHz the bins are 0, 5, 10, 15, ±20, −15, −10, −5 MHz. After fftshift: −20, −15, …, 15 MHz, with axis (-floor(N/2):ceil(N/2)-1)*fs/N.

Exact formula
For odd N there is no Nyquist bin and ifftshift ≠ fftshift; always undo with ifftshift.
A delay is a linear phase, and the DFT's delays are circular.
\(x[n-m] \leftrightarrow W^{km}X[k]\)−360°·km/N

Shift the impulse to n = 1 and every bin becomes \(W^k\): magnitude 1, phase −45° per bin at N = 8. The slope of the phase is the delay. The 4-sample pulse centred at n = 1.5 has \(\angle X[1] = -67.5°\) (02.9).

Exact formula
The shift wraps around the record end. Fractional delays require a band-limited signal and the signed (−N/2 … N/2) bins with a real Nyquist bin.
Two real FFTs fit in one complex FFT, and a 2D FFT is rows, then columns.
\(X = (Z[k] + Z^{*}[N-k])/2\)fft2 = fft over rows, then columns

With \(z = x + jy\), the conjugate-symmetric part of Z is X and the antisymmetric part is jY (02.22). Two RF lines per transform halve the FFT count. The 2D kernel \(W_M^{km}W_N^{ln}\) factors (02.23), so a 256 × 256 fft2 costs 2 × 256 FFTs of length 256, the same \((N/2)\log_2 N\) count as one 65 536-point FFT.

The two-for-one trick costs some extra additions and loses accuracy when one signal is far weaker than the other. Separability needs a rectangular grid.

02.13Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What does the imaginary part of an FFT bin physically mean? · screen

It is minus the correlation of the signal with a sine at that bin's frequency, i.e. the quadrature content. For real x, x[n] contains \(\frac{2}{N}[\mathrm{Re}\,X[k]\cos(2\pi kn/N)\) \(- \mathrm{Im}\,X[k]\sin(2\pi kn/N)]\), so \(-\frac{2}{N}\mathrm{Im}\,X[k]\) is the sine amplitude (02.10). A pure sine at bin 1 of an 8-point FFT gives \(X[1] = -4j\). It is not "imaginary" in any physical sense: it is the 90°-shifted projection, and it changes if you move the time origin. Follow-up: what is \(\mathrm{Im}\,X[0]\)? (Zero for real x; there is no sine at DC.)

I2A 1 V amplitude sinusoid sits exactly on bin 100 of a 1024-point FFT. What does abs(X(101)) read, and how do you convert to volts? · screen

\(AN/2 = 512\). Divide by N/2 for the one-sided amplitude (1 V). With a Hann window it reads 256 (coherent gain 0.5). If the tone sits half a bin off, the rectangular peak drops to \(512 \times 2/\pi \approx 326\) (−3.92 dB). Follow-up: where is the other half? (Bin 924, the conjugate.)

I3Why do the upper N/2 bins mirror the lower ones for real data, and when do they not? · screen

\(e^{-j2\pi(N-k)n/N} = e^{+j2\pi kn/N}\), so bin N − k is frequency −k, and for real x it equals \(X[k]^{*}\). A real cosine is two counter-rotating phasors, so each appears twice with half amplitude. Complex I/Q data have no such symmetry: after demodulation the upper bins are genuine negative frequencies, which encode flow direction in Doppler.

I4Derive the radix-2 FFT butterfly. · onsite

Split n into even and odd: \(X[k] = \sum x[2m]W_N^{2mk}\) \(+ W_N^k\sum x[2m+1]W_N^{2mk}\). Use \(W_N^2 = W_{N/2}\), so the sums are N/2-point DFTs \(E[k]\), \(O[k]\). They have period N/2 and \(W_N^{k+N/2} = -W_N^k\), so \(X[k] = E + W^kO\) and \(X[k + N/2] = E - W^kO\). Recursion gives \(\log_2 N\) stages of N/2 butterflies: \((N/2)\log_2 N\) multiplies. Follow-up: why is the input bit-reversed? (Each split sorts by the lowest remaining bit.)

I5Does zero padding improve frequency resolution? · screen

No. It samples the same DTFT more finely; the main-lobe width is set by the record length, ≈ \(1/T = f_s/N\). Padding a 2 µs gate (80 samples at 40 MHz) to 256 gives a 156 kHz grid, but two components closer than about 500 kHz cannot be reliably separated. It does improve peak location by interpolation and prevents wrap-around in FFT convolution. Follow-up: what does improve resolution? (A longer gate, or model-based methods such as AR/Burg with their own bias, Module 27.)

I6What is the scalloping loss, and how would you remove it for amplitude measurements? · onsite

The peak-bin amplitude deficit for a tone between bins: \(\lvert D_N(\tfrac12)\rvert/N \approx 2/\pi\), −3.92 dB, rectangular window; −1.42 dB with Hann. Remedies: a flat-top window (wide main lobe, small amplitude error), zero padding and peak interpolation (parabolic on log magnitude), or fitting the known kernel. Follow-up: what does that cost in noise bandwidth? (Wider windows raise ENBW, 1.5 bins for Hann, more for flat-top.)

I7A tone is 20 dB below the per-sample noise. How long an FFT do you need to see it at 10 dB SNR in its bin? · onsite

Bin SNR = \((N/2)\) × per-sample SNR, so \(N/2 = 10^{(10 + 20)/10} = 1000\), N ≈ 2000; take 2048 (gain 30.1 dB). That assumes the tone is coherent over the whole record, sits on a bin, and the noise is white. Follow-up: with Hann? (ENBW 1.5 costs 1.76 dB, so go to 4096 or accept 8.3 dB.)

I8How do you build the frequency axis for fftshift(fft(x)) with N odd? · screen

f = (-floor(N/2):ceil(N/2)-1)*fs/N, which for N = 7 is \((-3 \dots 3)\cdot f_s/7\): no Nyquist bin. For N even it is −N/2 … N/2 − 1, with \(-f_s/2\) first. Undo with ifftshift, not fftshift, which differ by one sample for odd N. Follow-up: where is DC after the shift? (Index floor(N/2) + 1 in MATLAB.)

I9Show that the DFT's inverse is \(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{F}^{H}\) without inverting anything. · research

\((\mathbf{F}^{H}\mathbf{F})_{lk} = \sum_n e^{j2\pi(l-k)n/N} = N\,\delta[l-k]\) by the geometric series with \(r^N = 1\). So \(\mathbf{F}^{H}\mathbf{F} = N\mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{F}\) is \(\sqrt N\) times a unitary matrix, and Parseval follows. Follow-up: what are the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{F}/\sqrt N\)? (Only ±1 and ±j: \(\mathbf{F}^4 = N^2\mathbf{I}\).)

I10You need the spectrum of 128 RF lines of 2048 samples each, real-time. What FFT tricks do you use? · research

Pack pairs of lines as \(x + jy\) and split with the conjugate-symmetry formulas (02.22) (64 complex FFTs instead of 128); keep only N/2 + 1 bins; batch the transforms (GPU or FFTW plans reused across lines); if only a band is needed, demodulate and decimate first (Module 08), so N shrinks by the decimation factor. Cost per line ≈ \((N/2)\log_2 N\) = 11 264 complex multiplies vs 4.2 million direct. Follow-up: how does the 2D FFT of the whole frame relate? (Rows then columns: k-space, Module 04.)

02.15Self-check

Q1Compute the 4-point DFT of x = [1, 0, −1, 0] by hand. What does each bin mean?

\(X[0] = 0\), \(X[1] = 1 + 0 + (-1)(-1) + 0 = 2\), \(X[2] = 1 - 0 + (-1)(1) - 0 = 0\), \(X[3] = 2\). x is \(\cos(2\pi n/4)\) with A = 1: two real lines of \(AN/2 = 2\) at bins 1 and 3, no DC, no Nyquist, no sine part (Im = 0).

Q2An 8-point DFT of a real signal has \(X[3] = 2 - 6j\). What is \(X[5]\), and what sinusoid does the pair represent?

\(X[5] = X[3]^{*} = 2 + 6j\). The pair is \(\frac{2}{8}[2\cos(2\pi 3n/8) + 6\sin(2\pi 3n/8)]\) \(= 0.5\cos(3\pi n/4) + 1.5\sin(3\pi n/4)\), amplitude \(\frac{2}{8}\lvert 2 - 6j\rvert = 0.25 \times 6.32 = 1.58\), phase \(\angle X[3] = -71.6°\).

Q3Why is \(X[4]\) of a real 8-point signal always real, and what happens to \(\sin(\pi n)\)?

The bin-4 probe is \(e^{-j\pi n} = (-1)^n\), real, so \(X[4] = \sum x[n](-1)^n\) is real. \(\sin(\pi n) = 0\) at every integer n, so a Nyquist sine is invisible; the bin only records \(A\cos\varphi\).

Q4Using the radix-2 butterfly, compute the 2-point DFT of [a, b], then the 4-point DFT of [0, 1, 0, 0].

2-point: [a + b, a − b]. For [0, 1, 0, 0]: E = DFT[0, 0] = [0, 0], O = DFT[1, 0] = [1, 1]. \(X[0] = 0 + 1 = 1\), \(X[1] = 0 + (-j)(1) = -j\), \(X[2] = 0 - 1 = -1\), \(X[3] = 0 - (-j) = j\). That is \(W^k\): an impulse at n = 1 has unit magnitude and phase −90° per bin.

Q5\(f_s\) = 40 MHz, N = 1000. Which MATLAB index holds 5 MHz, and which holds −5 MHz?

\(\Delta f\) = 40 kHz, so 5 MHz is bin 125, index X(126). −5 MHz is bin N − 125 = 875, index X(876). After fftshift, DC sits at index 501 and 5 MHz at 626.

Q6A tone sits at 10.5 bins in a 64-point rectangular-window FFT. Roughly how much lower is the peak than for 10.0, and what is \(\lvert X[12]\rvert\) relative to \(\lvert X[10]\rvert\)?

Peak loss ≈ 3.92 dB (\(2/\pi\)). Using \(D_N(\delta) \approx N\,\mathrm{sinc}(\delta)\): bins 10 and 11 have \(\delta = \pm 0.5\), \(\lvert\mathrm{sinc}\rvert = 0.637\); bin 12 has \(\delta = -1.5\), \(\lvert\mathrm{sinc}\rvert = 0.212\). Ratio 0.212/0.637 = 1/3, i.e. −9.5 dB below the two peak bins.

Q7How many complex multiplies does a radix-2 FFT of N = 4096 need, compared with the direct DFT?

\((N/2)\log_2 N = 2048 \times 12 = 24\,576\), against \(N^2 = 16\,777\,216\): 683 times fewer.
