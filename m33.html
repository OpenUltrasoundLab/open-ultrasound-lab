Aberration correction (capstone)

Aberration is what is left of the sound-speed problem after the beamformer has done its best with one speed: a delay error that changes across the aperture, and usually across the image too. Correcting it is delay estimation (Module 11) on signals whose coherence is set by the van Cittert–Zernike theorem (Module 07), turned into an eigenvector problem by the SVD (Module 06) and into a matrix change of basis by the reflection matrix (Module 05). This capstone builds the phase-screen model, the classical estimators (nearest-neighbour correlation, speckle brightness, time reversal), the isoplanatic patch that limits all of them, correction through a sound-speed map (Module 32), and the reflection and distortion matrices that make the correction local, and closes with transcranial, iterative and learned methods and the metrics that judge them.

You will be able to

  • Model an aberrator as a random delay screen and predict its Strehl ratio, PSF halo and coherence loss from two numbers, the rms delay and the correlation length
  • Derive why nearest-neighbour cross-correlation drifts as a random walk, when multi-lag least squares fixes it, and why a blurred transmit focus biases every speckle-based estimate until you iterate
  • Show that speckle-brightness maximization, time reversal and DORT all return the dominant eigenvector of an aperture covariance, and say what that eigenvector focuses on
  • Compute the isoplanatic patch of a screen at depth \(d\), including the distance beyond which a borrowed correction is worse than none
  • Turn a Module 32 sound-speed map into a space-variant correction and budget its errors (straight rays, blur, speed bias)
  • Build the focused reflection matrix and the distortion matrix of a speckle medium, extract a local aberration law by SVD, iterate it, and read the singular spectrum as a count of isoplanatic modes

33.1The phase-screen model: two numbers that predict the damage

Fat, muscle and connective tissue in the body wall have speeds from about 1450 to 1600 m/s and irregular boundaries. After the beamformer has removed the geometric delays for \(c_0\), each element still sees an excess delay \(\tau(x)\) that depends on the path. The simplest model that captures the damage puts all of it in a thin screen against the array: a random delay function of the element position, Gaussian with rms \(\sigma_\tau\) and a correlation length \(\ell\). In matrix language (Module 05) it is a diagonal matrix of phasors applied on transmit and again on receive, and its correction is the conjugate.

\[ \phi(x) = 2\pi f_0\,\tau(x), \qquad \langle \tau(x)\,\tau(x+\Delta x)\rangle = \sigma_\tau^{2}\,\rho(\Delta x), \qquad \rho(\Delta x) = e^{-\Delta x^{2}/\ell^{2}}, \qquad \sigma_\phi = 2\pi f_0\,\sigma_\tau \]
(33.1)

\(\phi(x)\)

\(\rho,\ \ell\)

\(f_0\)

\(\Delta x\)

At 5 MHz, 10 ns of delay is 0.314 rad. A 40 ns rms screen is 1.26 rad: a fifth of the 200 ns period (72°), enough to remove two thirds of the focal intensity. The model is narrowband (one phase per element) and puts the aberrator at the array, which makes it exactly isoplanatic; Section 33.4 moves it away.

Strehl ratio. The on-axis focal intensity relative to the unaberrated focus is the squared mean phasor across the aperture. For a Gaussian screen its expectation is exact, and it has two simple limits:

\[ S = \Bigl\lvert \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} e^{j\phi_i} \Bigr\rvert^{2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[S] = \frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j} e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}\,[1-\rho(x_i - x_j)]} \;\approx\; \begin{cases} e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}} & \sigma_\phi \lesssim 1,\ \ell \ll D \\[4pt] \dfrac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\ell}{\sigma_\phi\,D} & \sigma_\phi \gg 1 \end{cases} \]

The double sum uses \(\mathbb{E}[e^{j(\phi_i-\phi_j)}] = e^{-\frac12\mathrm{Var}(\phi_i - \phi_j)}\) for Gaussian phases. m33_phase_screen_psf.m (128 elements, \(\ell\) = 4 mm, 400 screens): at 40 ns the Monte Carlo Strehl is 0.311, the double sum 0.315, \(e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}\) only 0.206; at 80 ns 0.078, 0.079 and 0.002, where the large-\(\sigma\) form gives 0.073. Two way (same screen on transmit and receive) the Strehl of a realization is exactly \(S^{2}\); its mean \(\mathbb{E}[S^{2}]\) = 0.131 at 40 ns is above \(\mathbb{E}[S]^{2}\) = 0.099 because \(S\) fluctuates between realizations.

Why the Maréchal-type \(e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}}\) underestimates: elements closer than \(\ell\) share their phase and keep adding coherently, so a large aperture behaves like \(D/\ell\) partly independent cells, and a fraction of order \(\ell/(\sigma_\phi D)\) of the intensity survives in the focus. The intensity that leaves the focus forms a halo. Its width follows from the slope of the screen: the local wavefront tilt has rms \(\sqrt2\,c\,\sigma_\tau/\ell\), so for a strong screen the mean intensity is a Gaussian of rms width \(s\). 33.2Estimating the law from echoes: nearest-neighbour correlation, drift and least squares

Flax and O'Donnell (IEEE Trans. Ultrason. Ferroelectr. Freq. Control, 1988) made the screen measurable without a guide star. Adjacent elements look at the same focal region through almost the same patch of screen, so their echoes are highly correlated, for a point reflector and, by van Cittert–Zernike, for speckle (Module 07: lag-one coherence \(1 - 1/N\) for an ideal focus). Cross-correlate each element with its neighbour (Module 11), read the delay difference, and integrate:

\[ \hat d_i = \tau_{i+1} - \tau_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \hat\tau_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\hat d_i, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat\tau_k - \tau_k) = (k-1)\,\sigma_\varepsilon^{2}, \qquad \overline{e^{2}}\,\Big\rvert_{\text{mean, ramp removed}} \approx \frac{N}{15}\,\sigma_\varepsilon^{2} \]
(33.4)

\(\hat\tau_k\)

\(\overline{e^{2}}\)

The integration turns independent errors into a random walk: the raw error grows as \(\sqrt{k}\), and even after removing the best line (which only steers the beam, and which speckle cannot determine anyway) the rms error is \(\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{N/15}\) = 2.9 \(\sigma_\varepsilon\) for 128 elements (checked by Monte Carlo: \(N/15\) to 0.1 %). If instead the error is element noise, each noise sample enters two adjacent differences with opposite signs and the sum telescopes: no drift at all. Real data sit between the two.

Using more pairs breaks the random walk. Every pair \((i, j)\) up to a lag \(L\) gives a measured difference \(\hat d_{ij}\) of known reliability, and the profile is the weighted least-squares solution of an overdetermined system (Module 13) whose unknowns sit on the nodes of a graph and whose data sit on its edges, with one extra row to fix the constant:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{t}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{t}} \sum_{1 \le j - i \le L} w_{ij}\,\bigl(\tau_j - \tau_i - \hat d_{ij}\bigr)^{2} + \bigl(\mathbf{1}^{T}\mathbf{t}\bigr)^{2} = \bigl(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,\hat{\mathbf{d}}, \qquad w_{ij} = \frac{\lvert\rho_{ij}\rvert^{2}}{1 - \lvert\rho_{ij}\rvert^{2}} \]
(33.5)

\(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\) is a weighted graph Laplacian whose only null vector is the constant; the \(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\) term removes it. With \(L = 1\) the solution is the integrated nearest-neighbour estimate; each added lag adds a path between distant elements that does not accumulate the intermediate errors.

m33_nn_crosscorr_correction.m synthesizes speckle channel data (128 elements, 60 % bandwidth, dynamic receive focus) through a 40 ns, 4 mm screen applied on transmit and receive, and finds two regimes. Noiseless speckle: the adjacent-element correlation is 0.992, the pair jitter only 0.68 ns, yet the residual after correction is 5.7 ns rms, three times the random-walk prediction, and least squares does not reduce it. That residual is a bias: the aberrated transmit beam insonifies a broad, irregular spot, and the echoes estimate the law that focuses on that spot, not on the intended point. Transmitting again with the pass-1 estimate narrows the spot and halves the residual (2.6 ns, Strehl 0.992). 0 dB channel SNR: correlation 0.50, jitter 4.5 ns, and now the drift dominates: after the transmit iteration the nearest-neighbour residual is 8.3 ns (Strehl 0.93) and least squares over 8 lags gives 4.0 ns (Strehl 0.98). The random-walk bound \(\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{N/15}\) = 13.1 ns overestimates because part of the error is element noise, which telescopes.

Pitfall

Judging an aberration estimator by its pair jitter. Sub-nanosecond jitter is easy in bright speckle; the error that matters is the bias from the blurred transmit focus, which no amount of averaging or least squares removes. Iterate the transmit (Måsøy, Varslot and Angelsen reported near-ideal correction after two to three transmit iterations, J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 2005), or estimate where the focus is already good.

33.3Speckle brightness, time reversal and DORT: one beam, one eigenvector

Nock, Trahey and Smith (J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 1989) replaced the correlation by a figure of merit: adjust the element delays to maximize the brightness of a speckle region, which is largest when the beam is focused. Zhao and Trahey (1991) compared such quality factors for diffuse and point targets. Brightness has a structure worth seeing. Let \(\mathbf{s}\) be the vector of focused channel signals of a pixel and \(\mathbf{w}\) the receive weights; the beamformed output is \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{s}\) and its mean energy is a quadratic form in the aperture covariance:

\[ B(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E}\bigl\lvert \mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{s}\bigr\rvert^{2} = \mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{w}, \qquad \mathbf{C} = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{H}\bigr] = \mathbf{\Phi}\,\mathbf{C}_0\,\mathbf{\Phi}^{H}, \qquad \mathbf{\Phi} = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(e^{j\phi_i}\bigr), \qquad \max_{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2} = N} B \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{w} \propto \mathbf{u}_1(\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{\Phi}\,\mathbf{u}_1(\mathbf{C}_0) \]

The unconstrained maximizer is the dominant eigenvector (Varslot, Mo, Angelsen and Krogstad, J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 2004, showed that the brightness-maximizing filter focuses on the regions of high insonification of the aberrated transmit beam). Its phase is the screen phase plus the phase of \(\mathbf{u}_1(\mathbf{C}_0)\), which is flat (up to a ramp from the spot's centroid) only when the insonified spot is compact and symmetric. A phase-only search (\(\lvert w_i\rvert = 1\)) is solved by coordinate ascent, \(w_i \leftarrow e^{j\arg(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w})_i}\), which never decreases \(B\).

Mallart and Fink (J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 1991; the aberration and focusing-criterion paper followed in 1994) gave the van Cittert–Zernike reading: the speckle behind a focused transmit radiates like an incoherent source shaped like the transmit focal intensity, so \(\mathbf{C}\) is the covariance a real source of that shape would produce through the screen. A point-like source gives a rank-one \(\mathbf{C}\) and a perfect estimate; an aberrated, broad source spreads the eigenvalues, and an asymmetric one biases the eigenvector beyond a removable ramp. That is the bias of Section 33.2 again, and the same remedy applies: transmit with the estimate, re-measure, repeat.

Time reversal and DORT. With discrete scatterers the inter-element response matrix is \(\mathbf{K} = \mathbf{H}\,\mathrm{diag}(a)\,\mathbf{H}^{T}\) (monochromatic, single scattering, \(\mathbf{H}\) the element-to-scatterer Green's matrix including the aberrator). One time-reversal round trip (emit \(\mathbf{e}\), receive \(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{e}\), phase-conjugate, re-emit) returns \(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{K}^{*}\mathbf{e}^{*}\); two round trips apply \(\mathbf{K}^{*}\mathbf{K}\) (the time-reversal operator, equal to \(\mathbf{K}^{H}\mathbf{K}\) since \(\mathbf{K}\) is symmetric), and iterating is power iteration, which converges to the brightest scatterer. Prada and Fink (Wave Motion, 1994) decomposed the time-reversal operator instead:

\[ \mathbf{K}^{H}\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{v}_s = \lambda_s\,\mathbf{v}_s, \qquad \mathbf{v}_s \propto \mathbf{h}_s^{*}, \qquad \lambda_s = \lvert a_s\rvert^{2}\,\lVert\mathbf{h}_s\rVert^{4} \quad \text{(well-resolved scatterers: } \mathbf{h}_s^{H}\mathbf{h}_{s'} \approx 0) \]
(33.7)

33.4Distributed aberration and the isoplanatic patch

A screen against the array is a diagonal matrix: one law corrects every point of the image. Real aberrators are thick and sit millimetres to centimetres from the probe, so rays to different image points cross different parts of them. Keep a thin screen but move it to depth \(d\). The straight ray from element \(x_u\) to a point \((x_p, z)\) crosses the screen at \(x_u(1 - d/z) + x_p\,d/z\), so moving the point laterally by \(\Delta x\) slides the footprint of the whole aperture across the screen by \(\delta = \Delta x\,d/z\). A law measured on one point and borrowed for another leaves the residual \(\phi(a_u + \delta) - \phi(a_u)\):

\[ \delta = \Delta x\,\frac{d}{z}, \qquad S(\Delta x) \approx e^{-2\sigma_\phi^{2}\,[1 - \rho(\delta)]}, \qquad \Delta x_{\times} = \frac{\ell\,z\sqrt{\ln 2}}{d}, \qquad \Delta x_{1/2} = \frac{\ell\,z}{d}\sqrt{-\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{\ln 2}{2\sigma_\phi^{2}}\right)} \]
(33.8)

The residual of a borrowed law has variance \(2\sigma_\phi^{2}(1-\rho)\), which exceeds the uncorrected \(\sigma_\phi^{2}\) once \(\rho < 1/2\): outside the patch, a correction is worse than none. m33_isoplanatic_patch.m (40 ns, \(\ell\) = 4 mm, z = 40 mm, 128 elements, finite-aperture exact formula and Monte Carlo): half-widths at \(S = 1/2\) of 16.5, 8.3 and 4.3 mm for d = 5, 10 and 20 mm; the large-aperture form gives 8.0 mm at 10 mm. The borrowed law falls to the uncorrected level at 22.5, 11.0 and 5.1 mm, against \(\Delta x_\times\) = 26.6, 13.3 and 6.7 mm (the finite aperture keeps the uncorrected Strehl above \(e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}}\)). The exponential in (33.8) is not a small-phase expansion: like \(e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}}\) in (33.2), it is the exact Gaussian average in the limit \(\ell \ll D\). At finite aperture, elements closer than \(\ell\) add the floor of Section 33.1, which lifts the uncorrected Strehl (0.349 at d = 10 mm, against 0.206) more than the borrowed one (0.349 against 0.304 at 11 mm) and so moves the crossing inward.

Three consequences shape every modern method. First, the patch scales as \(z\ell/d\): a shallow, coarse aberrator is benign, a deep or fine one is not, which is why the skull (thick, so its far side sits at a sizeable fraction of the target depth, finely structured, and with a phase error of several radians, which shrinks \(\Delta x_{1/2}\) further as about \(1/\sigma_\phi\)) is the hardest case. Second, in depth the footprint also changes, so patches are finite axially too. Third, the patch is a property of the screen statistics, not of the estimator: no single-law method, however accurate at its guide point, beats \(\Delta x_\times\). Methods that work over the whole field must either estimate many local laws (Section 33.6) or model the medium between the probe and the image (Section 33.5).

33.5Correction from a sound-speed map: the Module 32 route

Module 32 estimated the slowness field \(s(\mathbf r) = 1/c(\mathbf r)\). 33.7Frontiers and judgement: iterative matrix methods, the skull, learned correction and the metrics

Iterative, alternating, shrinking. The full matrix pipeline of Lambert et al. (IEEE Trans. Med. Imaging, 2022, Part II) alternates the correction between the transmit and receive sides, works in either the plane-wave or the transducer basis, and shrinks the local window at each step (from 12 × 16 mm to 6.2 × 8.1 mm in their in vivo example, each window holding of the order of 5000 independent virtual sources). On a gallbladder in a volunteer they report the transverse −3 dB resolution going from 1.69 to 0.50 mm and their focusing factor rising from 0.31 to 0.48 over four steps. The structure echoes CLASS from optics (closed-loop accumulation of single scattering; Kang et al., Nat. Commun., 2017): in a reflection matrix expressed in the angular (pupil) basis, find the input and the output phase corrections that maximize the total confocal (single-scattering) intensity, updating one side while holding the other, and iterate. "CLASS-like" in ultrasound usually means exactly that alternating, energy-maximizing update; CLASS itself searches the phase channel by channel and uses no SVD. The distortion-matrix SVD can stand in for each half-step: with a receive law \(\mathbf{w}\) the confocal energy of a patch is \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{w}\), CLASS maximizes it over unit-modulus laws, and \(\mathbf{u}_1\) maximizes it when the amplitudes are left free (this is the module's reading, not a statement from either paper).

Through the skull. Bone is roughly twice as fast as soft tissue, strongly attenuating and variable in thickness, and it sits against the probe. The thin-screen picture then fails three ways: amplitude varies as much as phase, the aberrator is thick (the patch is finite in depth too), and for shallow targets \(d/z\) is large, so patches are small (33.8). Three strategies coexist. Priors: CT-derived speed and density maps give the delays, as in transskull therapy focusing (Aubry and coworkers, J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 2003); this is the Section 33.5 route with a better map. Guide stars: in transcranial ULM, Demené et al. (Nat. Biomed. Eng., 2021) corrected skull aberration and brain motion by adaptive speckle tracking before localizing, and the same group then estimated phase-aberration laws from the microbubble echoes in several isoplanatic patches (Robin et al., arXiv:2210.02774, 2022); Module 31 showed why this is necessary: an uncorrected tilt displaces every bubble. Matrices: 2D-array matrix imaging (Bureau et al., Nat. Commun., 2023) estimates the transmission matrix voxel by voxel in 3D, with transcranial imaging as the target.

Learned correction. Networks trained on simulated channel data can output aberration laws, corrected delays or corrected images directly. They inherit the Module 32 caveat: the forward model moves into the training distribution, so skull, abdominal-wall and probe-lens mismatches become domain gaps. Physics-anchored designs (unrolled matrix or autofocus iterations, differentiable beamformers, Module 14) keep the measured data in the loop and are the safer path to deployment.

33.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Model an abdominal-wall aberrator in two numbers and predict the focal loss at 5 MHz for 40 ns rms. · screen

A thin delay screen at the array: rms \(\sigma_\tau\) and correlation length \(\ell\) (Gaussian). \(\sigma_\phi = 2\pi f_0\sigma_\tau\) = 1.26 rad. Infinite-aperture Strehl \(e^{-1.58}\) = 0.21; with 128 elements and \(\ell\) = 4 mm the exact mean is 0.315 because nearby elements stay in phase; two way about 0.1. The −6 dB width hardly changes until about 30 ns, then the halo takes over. Follow-up: what if the frequency halves? \(\sigma_\phi\) halves and the exponent quarters: about 0.67.

I2Why does nearest-neighbour cross-correlation drift, and how do you fix it? · screen

Integrating \(N-1\) independent differences is a random walk: error \(\propto\sqrt{k}\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{N/15}\) after removing the mean and ramp. Fixes: weighted least squares over many lags (graph Laplacian plus a constant constraint), better SNR, longer kernels. Element noise telescopes and does not drift. Follow-up: in bright speckle the jitter is sub-ns but the residual is 5.7 ns; why? A bias from the blurred transmit spot, removed by transmitting with the estimate and re-measuring.

I3Show that speckle-brightness maximization and DORT give the same aberration law. · onsite

Brightness of the beamformed pixel is \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{w}\) with \(\mathbf{C}\) the aperture covariance, maximized over \(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^{2} = N\) by the dominant eigenvector. Iterative time reversal is power iteration on \(\mathbf{K}^{H}\mathbf{K}\), and DORT diagonalizes it; for speckle behind a focused transmit, \(\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{vr}}\mathbf{\Phi}^{H}\), so the eigenvector is the screen times the eigenvector of the virtual reflector. Follow-up: when is that second factor flat? When the insonified spot is compact and symmetric, which is why you iterate.

I4Derive the isoplanatic patch for a thin screen at depth d. When is a correction worse than none? · onsite

The straight ray from \(x_u\) to \((x_p, z)\) crosses the screen at \(x_u(1-d/z) + x_p d/z\); moving the point by \(\Delta x\) shifts the footprint by \(\delta = \Delta x\,d/z\). The borrowed law leaves a residual of variance \(2\sigma_\phi^{2}(1-\rho(\delta))\): Strehl \(e^{-2\sigma_\phi^{2}(1-\rho)}\), worse than uncorrected when \(\rho < 1/2\), at \(\Delta x_\times = \ell z\sqrt{\ln 2}/d\) = 13.3 mm for \(\ell\) 4, z 40, d 10 mm. Follow-up: finite aperture? The crossover moves in (11.0 mm) because the uncorrected Strehl is higher than \(e^{-\sigma_\phi^{2}}\).

I5You have a CUTE sound-speed map from Module 32. Turn it into an aberration correction and write its error budget. · onsite

Integrate slowness from every element to every pixel (straight rays or eikonal) and beamform with those times. Errors: map resolution (a low-pass truth: 3 mm blur cost 8.2 against 5.2 ns rms in m33_sos_map_correction.m), map bias (a 10 m/s error in the fat speed: 7.3 ns, mostly defocus), path model (straight rays always overestimate times: 3–11 ns behind a wavy fat layer), and the mean speed CUTE cannot see. Follow-up: how would you check it in vivo? Measure the residual law with a matrix method; a large smooth residual points to the mean or the path model.

I6What is the focused reflection matrix, and how does it measure focusing without a point target? · onsite

\(\mathbf{R}_{xx} = \mathbf{G}_0^{H}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{G}_0^{*}\): responses between virtual transducers at \(x_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{out}}\) after focusing in software. The diagonal is the confocal image; in speckle the mean intensity versus offset is the cross-correlation of transmit and receive PSF intensities, so its width (Lambert et al.'s \(F = w_0/w\)) or its confocal energy fraction measures the local focus anywhere. Follow-up: what spoils it? Multiple scattering adds a broad background (useful in itself) and noise adds a floor.

I7Walk through distortion-matrix correction. Why re-centre, why SVD, why iterate, and how big should the window be? · research

Focus on transmit, keep element receive, multiply each column by the conjugate geometric wavefront of its focal point: every point of a patch now looks like one virtual source at the origin through the same screen. \(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{D}^{H}\) averages the speckle over the columns; its first singular vector is screen × virtual-reflector eigenvector. Correcting the input shrinks the reflector, so iterate (0.83 → 0.018 rad in three steps in the script). Window: many resolution cells for averaging, smaller than a patch; Lambert et al. shrank it from 12 × 16 to 6.2 × 8.1 mm. Follow-up: transmit vs receive laws with plane-wave transmits? Estimate both, alternating sides.

I8Your speckle-based estimator converges, but the image is shifted relative to CT. What happened? · research

A residual linear phase (tilt) is unobservable from speckle: it moves both foci together, so focus metrics (Γ, F, CF) look perfect while every point is displaced, a bias field as in Module 31's transcranial ULM. Remedies: anchor the tilt with known targets, a sound-speed map, or bubbles as guide stars with known geometry; in matrix methods watch for linear ramps removed during the estimation. Follow-up: how big? 10 ns across 10 mm at 50 mm depth moves a point by 77 µm (Module 31).

I9What breaks when you move from abdominal wall to transcranial imaging, and what do you do instead? · research

Bone is about twice as fast, attenuating and variable in thickness, so amplitude matters as much as phase, the aberrator is thick (finite patches in depth) and \(d/z\) is large for shallow targets (small patches). Options: CT-based priors (map route), guide stars (Robin et al. 2022 derived one aberration law per isoplanatic patch from microbubble echoes; Demené et al. 2021 used adaptive speckle tracking), 3D matrix imaging with 2D arrays (Bureau et al. 2023). Follow-up: what is the cheapest first step? Lower frequency: the same delays cost a quarter of the phase variance at half the frequency.

I10Which metrics would you report for a new aberration-correction method, and which would you avoid? · research

A Strehl-like or energy-fraction focus measure (Γ or the confocal energy), point-target FWHM where available, gCNR with region sizes (Module 26), and target positions for geometric fidelity; all before and after, at several depths and lateral positions to show the patch. Avoid CF or SLSC images as the main evidence (they reward noise suppression too) and contrast in dB on a display range chosen afterwards. Follow-up: why not FWHM alone? It saturates: 0.38 → 0.40 mm at 20 ns while the Strehl is already 0.73.

I11Propose a method that combines a sound-speed map and a matrix estimate. What does each contribute? · research

Use the map (Module 32) to compute space-variant starting delays, so the residual aberration is small and the patch large; then estimate the residual laws with local distortion matrices, which converge fast because the virtual reflector is already compact. Feed the residual delays back as tomographic data with the correct (bent-ray) forward model to update the map, and iterate. The map supplies locality and the tilt/mean anchor; the matrix supplies the measured truth where the map's resolution, bias or path model fail. Follow-up: what would you validate first? On a phantom with a known aberrator, compare map-only, matrix-only and combined Γ maps and target positions.
