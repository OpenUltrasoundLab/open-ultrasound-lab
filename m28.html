Microbubble physics, contrast imaging and the ULM signal source

A contrast microbubble is a gas spring with a liquid mass, tuned to megahertz by its size and stiffened by its shell. Everything you do in CEUS and ULM follows from that oscillator: why bubbles outshine tissue, how pulse sequences separate them, how time–intensity curves measure perfusion, and why ULM must trade bubble density against acquisition time.

You will be able to

  • Derive the Minnaert resonance, correct it for surface tension, heat exchange and a lipid shell, and quote \(f_0R_0\) with its assumptions

  • Predict what pulse inversion, amplitude modulation and their combination keep and reject, including the residues from tissue harmonics, motion and amplitude errors
  • Fit bolus and flash-replenishment time–intensity curves in the right (linear) domain
  • Turn a CEUS cine loop into reported perfusion parameters, and say which of them survive a change of scanner, gain or frame rate
  • Size the ULM concentration from the PSF, and estimate how long capillaries take to fill

In 60 seconds
  1. Why bubbles are visible: Gas is thousands of times more compressible than blood, so a microbubble pulsates and, far below resonance, out-scatters a same-size rigid sphere by about 10⁸. Its shell resists the surface-tension pressure that dissolves free gas, stretching lifetime from milliseconds to minutes. Single bubbles are detectable, which contrast imaging and localization microscopy rely on. §28.1
  2. Resonance: A bubble is a liquid mass on a gas spring, so resonance falls with size: \(f_0R_0 \approx 3.3\) MHz·µm for a free air bubble (\(R_0\) radius, \(f_0\) resonance). Shell stiffness raises it (a shell elasticity of 0.5 N/m moves a 2 µm bubble from 1.44 to 2.99 MHz). Agents resonate in the clinical band, hence their strong low-pressure response. §28.2
  3. Nonlinear echo: The gas spring stiffens more in compression than it softens in expansion, so echoes carry harmonics where tissue is still linear; a buckling lipid shell adds even-order signal at a few kPa. A free 2 µm bubble at resonance gives a second harmonic 13 dB below the fundamental at 10 kPa. Real-time contrast imaging runs at low output. §28.3 §28.4
  4. Contrast pulse sequences: Contrast sequences transmit scaled or inverted pulse copies and sum the echoes, cancelling the linear part and keeping nonlinear bubble signal. Pulse inversion (+1, −1) keeps even orders; amplitude modulation (1, ½) also keeps odd-order energy at the transmit frequency. The floor is what else fails to cancel: propagation harmonics in tissue and motion between pulses. §28.5
  5. Perfusion from time–intensity curves: After a destructive flash, bubbles refill the slice as \(I = A(1-e^{-\beta t})\): plateau A tracks microvascular blood volume, rate β tracks velocity over slice thickness, Aβ tracks flow. Fit linear intensity, since log data inflate mean transit time about twofold. Gain, attenuation and dose scale amplitudes, not timing, so only timing agrees across scanners. §28.6
  6. Separating bubbles from tissue in slow time: The singular value decomposition (SVD) clutter filter sorts components by spatial coherence and motion, not bubble physics, and a slow capillary bubble shares the Doppler shift of moving tissue (6.5 Hz at 1 mm/s, 5 MHz). A cut that removes tissue removes slow bubbles too, so put pulse inversion or amplitude modulation first. §28.7
  7. ULM concentration trade-off: Ultrasound localization microscopy (ULM) needs isolated bubbles, and isolated bubbles per frame peak at density \(n^* = 1/A\), with A the exclusion area around each bubble: 4.5 per mm² at 5 MHz. Capillaries receive bubbles slowly, so diluting to reduce overlap lengthens acquisition in proportion; full maps take minutes because of flux. §28.8
From the field
  • Fix output, focus, depth, gain and frame rate across perfusion studies you will compare. The mechanical index comes from the derated beam peak, not the pressure at the bubble, so response varies with depth and focus. Even then only timing transfers between scanners: in a phantom rise time agreed within 9 %; amplitudes did not. §28.6
  • Fit time–intensity curves in linear intensity, avoid saturation and normalize to a reference region. Log-compressed data inflate mean transit time about twofold, averaging log pixels reads 2.51 dB low, and a saturated plateau flattens the curve. Settings, attenuation and the reference region change the result more than the choice of curve model. §28.6
  • Count pulses per bubble, not only the mechanical index. Every frame destroys some bubbles, biasing the refill rate β: +13 % at 10 frames/s for a 1 % loss per frame and β = 0.8 s⁻¹, while the flow index Aβ is unchanged. §28.6 §28.8
  • Do not assume pulse inversion removes tissue near the focus. At moderate output, harmonics generated by nonlinear propagation in tissue survive pulse inversion exactly like bubble harmonics. Lower output or amplitude modulation helps: amplitude modulation leaves a quarter of that tissue residue, 12 dB lower. §28.5
  • Put amplitude modulation or pulse inversion before the SVD filter when slow flow matters. A singular-value (SVD) cut that removes tissue motion also removes bubbles moving a few mm/s, because their Doppler shifts match: 1 mm/s gives 6.5 Hz at 5 MHz. Contrast-specific transmit removes tissue whatever its velocity. §28.7

28.1The agent: gas core, shell, size, and why compressibility wins

Clinical agents are suspensions of gas microbubbles, 1–10 µm in diameter, stabilized by a monolayer of phospholipid or a thin shell of denatured albumin. The gas is a heavy, poorly soluble fluorinated gas because an uncoated micron bubble does not last: the Laplace pressure \(2\sigma/R\) (144 kPa at \(R\) = 1 µm in water) pushes gas into solution, and the Epstein–Plesset estimate for a 1 µm air bubble in air-saturated water is of order milliseconds. Low solubility and diffusivity of the core gas and a shell that cancels most of the surface tension stretch that to minutes.

Why a bubble is so bright. Subscript s = sphere. A rigid fixed sphere (\(\kappa_s = 0\), \(\rho_s \to \infty\)) gives \(\sigma_s = (7\pi/9)k^{4}a^{6}\). A gas bubble below resonance has \(\kappa_s/\kappa = \rho c^{2}/(\gamma P_0) \approx\) 15 400 (air, adiabatic, 1 atm), so its monopole term is \(2.4\times 10^{8}\).

The ratio of bubble to rigid sphere of the same size is \((4/7)(15\,400)^{2} \approx 1.4\times 10^{8}\), and against a tissue inhomogeneity with 5 % compressibility and density contrast it is about \(10^{11}\). That is far below resonance. Near resonance the bubble gains another factor \(1/\delta^{2}\) (28.2, (28.5)). One bubble is therefore visible on its own in a beamformed image, which is the physical basis of ULM.

Intuition

Tissue scatters because it is a little stiffer or denser than its surroundings. A bubble scatters because it is about 15 000 times softer: the same incident pressure makes it change volume 15 000 times more than the water it replaces, and a pulsating volume is a monopole source.

Key takeaways
  • Agents are 1–10 µm bubbles of heavy, poorly soluble gas in a lipid or albumin shell; the shell cancels most of the Laplace pressure 2σ/R (144 kPa at 1 µm) and stretches lifetime from milliseconds to minutes.
  • Compressibility contrast (κs/κ ≈ 15 400 for air) drives a monopole: about 1.4×10⁸ times a rigid sphere and 10¹¹ times a 5% tissue inhomogeneity, far below resonance.
  • A first-pass clinical bolus gives of order 10⁵ bubbles per mL of blood (140 per µL).

28.2The bubble as a mass on a gas spring: Minnaert and beyond

Mass. Incompressible radial flow around a sphere has velocity \(u(r) = R^{2}\dot R/r^{2}\). Its kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\int \rho u^{2}\, 4\pi r^{2}\, dr = \tfrac12 (4\pi R^{3}\rho)\, \dot R^{2}\), so the effective mass is \(m = 4\pi R_0^{3}\rho\), three times the mass of the displaced liquid. Spring. A polytropic gas gives \(p_g = P_0(R_0/R)^{3\kappa}\), so a radial displacement \(x\) changes the pressure by \(-3\kappa P_0 x/R_0\) over the area \(4\pi R_0^{2}\): the stiffness is \(s = 12\pi\kappa P_0 R_0\); hence (28.2).

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_0^{2} &= \frac{s}{m} = \frac{3\kappa P_0}{\rho R_0^{2}} \\ \Rightarrow\quad f_0 R_0 &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{3\kappa P_0}{\rho}} \end{aligned} \]
(28.2)

Three corrections matter at micron sizes, and all three are larger than the difference between 3.26 and 3.29.

  • Surface tension. The gas sits at \(P_0 + 2\sigma/R_0\), and the tension term itself responds to \(R\). Linearizing gives \(\rho\omega_0^{2}R_0^{2} = 3\kappa P_0 + 2(3\kappa - 1)\sigma/R_0\). A free 1 µm air bubble resonates at 4.74 MHz, not 3.29 (+44 %); at 2 µm, 2.04 versus 1.64 MHz.
  • Heat exchange. The gas is adiabatic only if the thermal diffusion length \(\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{th}}/2\omega}\) is much smaller than \(R_0\). For air at 3 MHz it is 0.73 µm, comparable to the radius, so \(\kappa\) lies between 1 and \(\gamma\) and heat conduction adds a thermal damping (Prosperetti's analysis). For contrast agents the polytropic exponent is usually taken close to the heat-capacity ratio of the heavy gas (≈ 1.07–1.1), which is already near 1.
  • Shell. In the Marmottant model the elastic shell makes the surface tension radius dependent, \(\sigma(R) = \chi(R^{2}/R_b^{2} - 1)\). Its slope adds stiffness (28.3):
\[ \rho\,\omega_0^{2}R_0^{2} = 3\kappa P_0 + \frac{2(3\kappa + 1)\,\sigma_0}{R_0} + \frac{4\chi}{R_0} \]

28.4Nonlinear signatures: harmonics, subharmonics, compression-only behaviour, destruction

Harmonics. The gas spring is asymmetric: \((R_0/R)^{3\kappa}\) stiffens without bound in compression and softens in expansion, and the liquid inertia makes the collapse faster than the growth. Near resonance the radial excursion is amplified by \(Q\), so a bubble becomes nonlinear at incident pressures where tissue propagation is still nearly linear. In the demo a free 2 µm bubble driven at its resonance has a second harmonic of −13 dB already at 10 kPa (MI 0.007; 20-cycle burst); the tissue second harmonic after 40 mm at 100 kPa and 2.5 MHz is −28 dB (Module 15).

Subharmonics and ultraharmonics. Above a threshold the oscillation doubles its period and emits at \(f/2\) (and \(3f/2\), \(5f/2\)). For a bubble the threshold is lowest when the drive is near twice the resonance, because the \(f/2\) response then falls on the resonance. For the free 2 µm bubble at \(2f_0\) the model gives \(f/2\) at −43, −30, −13 and −4 dB for 50, 100, 150 and 200 kPa: a threshold between about 100 and 175 kPa. A 1 µm bubble, with almost twice the viscous damping, stays below −25 dB up to 200 kPa. Tissue propagation creates no subharmonic, so subharmonic imaging has a very high contrast-to-tissue ratio, and because the subharmonic amplitude of some agents depends on ambient pressure it has been studied for noninvasive pressure estimation. Phospholipid bubbles show subharmonics at lower pressures than the free-bubble model predicts; single-bubble optical measurements found subharmonic thresholds as low as 5 kPa near twice the resonance, far below viscoelastic-shell predictions, and attribute them to the buckling regime of the shell (Sijl et al., JASA 2010).

Compression-only behaviour. A lipid bubble near the buckling radius has no tension in compression (\(\sigma = 0\), soft) but a rising tension in expansion (stiff). It therefore compresses much more than it expands, an effect observed optically by de Jong et al. (UMB 2007). In the model at 3 MHz the buckled bubble's compression/expansion ratio is 3.0 at 10 kPa and 2.5 at 50 kPa, against 1.1–1.3 for the elastic one. The asymmetry is an even-order nonlinearity at very low pressure: exactly what pulse inversion and amplitude modulation detect.

Destruction. As pressure rises the bubble sheds lipid, fragments, loses gas by diffusion, or collapses inertially. Rayleigh–Plesset cannot model fragmentation, but \(R_{\mathrm{max}}/R_0 \ge 2\) is the classic rough criterion for inertial collapse (Flynn); the analysis that led to MI (Apfel and Holland, UMB 1991) used a related collapse-temperature threshold of 5000 K. Clinical practice follows the same logic: real-time CEUS runs at low MI, below about 0.3 and often 0.05–0.1 on modern systems (EFSUMB guidance), to keep bubbles intact and nonlinear; a few high-MI "flash" frames destroy them on purpose for replenishment studies. MI is defined from the derated peak rarefactional pressure of the beam (Module 22), not from the pressure at a given bubble, so the bubble's local pressure depends on depth, attenuation and focusing.

Key takeaways
  • Asymmetric gas spring plus Q amplification: a free 2 µm bubble at resonance shows a −13 dB second harmonic at 10 kPa (MI 0.007), tissue −28 dB after 40 mm at 100 kPa.
  • Driving at 2f₀ minimizes the subharmonic threshold: for the free 2 µm bubble f/2 rises from −30 to −4 dB between 100 and 200 kPa; tissue propagation makes none.
  • Buckled lipid shells compress 2.5–3× further than they expand: even-order signal at a few kPa.
  • Rmax/R₀ ≥ 2 is the rough inertial-collapse criterion; real-time CEUS runs below MI ≈ 0.3 (often 0.05–0.1) to keep bubbles intact.

28.5Contrast-specific pulse sequences

Write the echo of a scatterer as a power series in the transmit amplitude \(a\): \(e(a) = a_1 a\,p + a_2 a^{2}p^{2} + a_3 a^{3}p^{3} + \dots\), where the \(a_n\) are operators (filters) and \(p^{n}\) carries energy near \(n f_0\) (and, for odd \(n\), back at \(f_0\)). AM keeps the odd-order "nonlinear fundamental", which propagates and is received at \(f_0\): better penetration, at the price of an exact half-amplitude transmit (systems commonly fire half of the elements to get it). Vendors combine amplitude and phase steps over three or more pulses (the "contrast pulse sequence" modes of commercial scanners); the algebra is always the same: choose weights that null \(a_1\) and keep the orders you want.

Three things leave tissue in a contrast image, and the demo models each:

  • Nonlinear propagation. The incident pulse at depth already contains a tissue-generated second harmonic. To second order \(p_{\mathrm{inc}} = a\,p_1 + a^{2}\,\frac{\beta x}{2\rho c^{3}}\,\frac{d(p_1^{2})}{dt}\), which survives PI (it is even in \(a\)) and is attenuated four times in the half pulse of AM. At 100 kPa, 2.5 MHz, \(x\) = 40 mm, \(\beta\) = 4.5 the CW ratio \(B_2/B_1 = \sigma/2\) is −28 dB.
  • Motion. A scatterer moving at \(v\) between pulses shifts by \(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF})\). For a linear scatterer the PI residue is \(\lvert 1 - e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\rvert = 2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert\) of the echo: 10 mm/s at PRF 5 kHz and 2.5 MHz gives −27.8 dB, at 5 MHz −21.8 dB.
  • Amplitude and phase errors. AM needs the half pulse to be exactly half: a gain error \(e\) (dB) leaves \(\lvert 1 - 10^{e/20}\rvert\) of the linear echo, −38.7 dB for 0.1 dB and −24.5 dB for 0.5 dB.

From signal to clinical number: CEUS quantification in practice

The curves above are what the physics predicts. A clinical number is what is left after an operator, a scanner preset and an analysis package have each touched the data, and most of the spread between sites comes from those three, not from the model. It helps to follow one study from the first setting to the report, because each step has a lever that moves the result and a check that tells you whether it did. Figure 28.E lays the steps out; the text explains what each one does to the number.

Set-up comes first because it cannot be repaired afterwards. The contrast preset fixes the MI (mechanical index) low enough to keep bubbles intact, typically 0.05–0.1 on current systems, and the focus is placed below the region of interest so that the pressure across it is roughly uniform. Gain, TGC (time-gain compensation) and displayed dynamic range must be identical for every study that will be compared, and gain is set so that the peak never reaches the top of the display: a saturated peak cannot be linearized back. The frame rate matters more than it looks. Every imaging frame destroys a small fraction of the bubbles it insonifies, so a faster frame rate improves the sampling of the wash-in but bleeds bubbles from the slice, which biases the replenishment fit as shown below.

The agent goes in either as a bolus with a saline flush, giving the bolus curves of (28.10), or as a constant infusion interrupted by a high-MI flash, giving the replenishment curve. The cine loop is recorded long enough to capture the whole transit, three minutes in the large multicentre study of Lassau et al. (Invest. Radiol. 2014), and for liver lesions it continues into the late phase, which lasts until the agent clears from the circulation, usually several minutes after injection (Ferraioli and Meloni, Ultrasonography 2018). Quantification needs the stored cine as raw or linear data, or at least a DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) export whose display map is known.

The operator then removes motion and places the regions of interest. The liver moves with breathing by about a centimetre, as far as the size of a typical lesion, so a fixed ROI (region of interest) drawn on one frame samples lesion in some frames and parenchyma in others. Analysis packages register the frames on the simultaneously displayed fundamental B-mode image, where tissue is visible before the agent arrives, and frames that leave the plane are discarded rather than corrected. The lesion ROI covers the enhancing tissue and avoids necrosis and large feeding vessels; a reference ROI in normal parenchyma sits at the same depth, so that attenuation, local pressure and bubble destruction are similar in both. A ratio of lesion to reference then cancels most of what depends on the instrument rather than on the tissue.

Reliability is where the amplitude–timing split becomes concrete. That is how the best-validated quantitative cut-off was obtained. In 539 patients treated with antiangiogenic drugs at 19 centres, all imaged with one scanner model, one agent and one analysis method, a fall of AUC to below 0.6 of its baseline value at day 30 (a decrease of more than 40 %) was associated with freedom from progression and with overall survival (Lassau et al. 2014). Liver diagnosis takes the opposite route and reads timing patterns by eye. CEUS LI-RADS (Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System), the American College of Radiology system for patients at risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), uses the arterial phase (from about 10–20 s to 30–45 s after injection), the portal venous phase (to about 2 min) and the late phase. APHE (arterial phase hyperenhancement) means enhancement greater than the surrounding liver in the arterial phase that is neither rim-like nor peripheral discontinuous globular (the haemangioma pattern). Washout is graded by onset, early (before 60 s) or late (60 s or later), and by degree, mild or marked (the observation is nearly devoid of enhancement, "punched out", within 2 min). In the v2017 core, an observation of at least 10 mm with non-rim APHE and late, mild washout is LR-5 (definitely HCC), while rim APHE, early washout or marked washout point to LR-M, malignant but not specific for HCC (Wilson et al., Abdom. Check the ACR website for the current version before quoting the categories.

The instrument still enters these visual reads. Washout is a fall in lesion intensity relative to liver, and continuous low-MI scanning destroys bubbles in the scan plane at the rate \(k_d\) of (28.14), in lesion and liver alike, so long continuous insonation, a higher MI or a high frame rate can make enhancement fade faster than the agent clears. Late-phase looks are therefore best made intermittently, freezing or lifting the probe between looks. Depth matters too: a deep lesion sits behind more attenuation and sees a lower local pressure than a shallow reference, so a fixed gain makes it look darker than it is. And a gain set so high that the liver saturates can hide a mild washout entirely.

Pitfall

A perfusion number exported from one vendor's package and compared with a published value from another is two instruments apart before any biology. Peak intensity and AUC depend on the linearization, gain, depth and MI; only ratios on fixed equipment and timing parameters travel between systems, and even rise time and MTT disagree by up to 9 % and 21 % between scanners in a phantom.

Key takeaways
  • Fix MI (≈ 0.05–0.1), focus, gain, TGC, dynamic range and frame rate for every study to be compared; linearize each pixel before averaging (averaging log pixels reads 2.51 dB low) and never let the peak saturate.
  • Gain, attenuation and dose scale PE, AUC and WiR but leave TTP, rise time and MTT unchanged; across scanners rise time agrees within 9 % and MTT within 21 % in a phantom, while PE and AUC are not comparable.
  • Imaging frames bias replenishment: βobs = β + kd and Aobs = Aβ/(β + kd) with kd = −FR·ln(1 − p), +13 % in β at 10 frames/s and 1 % loss for β = 0.8 s⁻¹; Aβ is unchanged.
  • Validated clinical uses are a within-patient AUC ratio below 0.6 at day 30 on fixed equipment (antiangiogenic therapy) and the timing-based CEUS LI-RADS categories (LR-5: ≥ 10 mm, non-rim APHE, late (≥ 60 s) mild washout).

28.7Separating bubbles from tissue in slow time

Ultrafast ULM usually separates bubbles from tissue with the spatiotemporal SVD filter (Module 06; Demené et al., IEEE TMI 2015): stack \(N_t\) frames as a Casorati matrix, discard the largest singular components (tissue: spatially coherent, slowly varying, 30–60 dB brighter), keep the intermediate ones (moving bubbles), discard the smallest (noise). It exploits motion, not bubble physics. Two consequences matter for ULM design:

  • Slow bubbles. A bubble moving axially at \(v\) has Doppler \(2vf/c\): 6.5 Hz at 1 mm/s and 5 MHz, the same Doppler as tissue moving at 1 mm/s, which is ordinary physiological motion. The SVD cut that removes tissue removes capillary bubbles too. Nonlinear sequences (AM, PI) remove tissue by physics and do not care about velocity, at the price of two or three transmits per frame and motion sensitivity (28.5).
  • Decorrelation. Between frames a bubble moves \(v/\mathrm{FR}\) (FR = frame rate; 10 µm at 10 mm/s and 1 kHz), its amplitude changes as it crosses the beam and its echo flickers with its radial dynamics. Tracking needs displacements per frame well below the PSF size; very fast bubbles at low frame rates break the linking, and very slow ones sit inside the clutter band.
Key takeaways
  • The SVD filter keeps components by spatiotemporal coherence and motion, not by bubble physics.
  • A 1 mm/s bubble has fD = 2vf/c = 6.5 Hz at 5 MHz, like tissue motion: the cut that removes tissue removes slow capillary bubbles; AM or PI remove tissue regardless of velocity.
  • Tracking needs a per-frame displacement v/FR well below the PSF (10 µm at 10 mm/s and 1 kHz).

28.8ULM: the bubble as a point source, and the concentration trade-off

A point source with an unknown, flickering amplitude. A bubble of 2 µm radius is \(\lambda/150\) of a 5 MHz wavelength, so its image is the system PSF times a complex amplitude. Localization precision is then set by the PSF width and the energy-to-noise ratio, \(\sigma_x \ge w_x/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}}\) (Module 12). The bubble physics enters through the amplitude: \(\sigma_s\) varies by orders of magnitude across the size distribution at a given frequency (28.1, 28.2), so the ENR, and therefore the precision, is a distribution, dominated by near-resonant and large bubbles. Choosing \(f\) for ULM trades PSF width (\(\propto 1/f\)) against bubble response (resonant population at lower \(f\)) and attenuation.

The density in the image is \(n = C\cdot(\text{slice thickness})\cdot(\text{vascular fraction})\). The time trade-off. A capillary is mapped only when bubbles pass through it. With diameter \(d\) and speed \(v\) it receives \(C\cdot(\pi d^{2}/4)\cdot v\) bubbles per second: for \(d\) = 8 µm, \(v\) = 1 mm/s and \(C\) = 140 per µL, one bubble every 142 s. Lowering \(C\) to reduce overlap lengthens this wait in proportion. This is the argument of Hingot et al. (Sci. Rep. 2019): microvascular flow, not frame rate, sets the acquisition time for full capillary maps, typically minutes.

Safety and the MI budget in ULM

ULM needs the same bubbles to survive hundreds to thousands of frames, so it runs at low MI, and plane-wave sequences with no focus keep the peak pressure low. The time-averaged exposure is another matter: 0.5–1 kHz compounded frame rates raise \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA}}\) and TI compared with conventional imaging (Module 22). Contrast agents lower the pressure threshold for cavitation-related bioeffects; animal studies have shown microvascular effects when bubbles are destroyed at high MI (for example Skyba et al., Circulation 1998). The design rule is to keep the MI at the bubble below the destruction threshold of the agent and to report MI, frame rate, duration and agent dose together.

28.10How experts think about it

An expert pictures the bubble as a damped oscillator whose spring is gas plus shell and whose mass is three times the displaced liquid: resonance from size and stiffness, Q from shell viscosity, brightness from compressibility. For ULM the same expert thinks in densities and fluxes: the PSF sets how many bubbles fit in a frame, the vasculature sets how fast new ones arrive.

I1Derive the resonance frequency of a gas bubble in water. What is it for \(R_0\) = 2 µm? · screen

Radial flow \(u = R^{2}\dot R/r^{2}\) has kinetic energy \(\tfrac12(4\pi R^{3}\rho)\dot R^{2}\): effective mass \(4\pi R_0^{3}\rho\). Polytropic gas: stiffness \(12\pi\kappa P_0R_0\). \(\omega_0^{2} = 3\kappa P_0/(\rho R_0^{2})\), \(f_0R_0 \approx\) 3.3 MHz·µm for air, so 1.64 MHz at 2 µm. Then I would add surface tension (2.04 MHz for a free 2 µm air bubble) and say which \(\kappa\) I assumed. Follow-up: "And for a lipid-coated agent?" Add \(4\chi/R_0\): with \(\chi\) = 0.5 N/m and a perfluorocarbon-like \(\kappa\) about 3 MHz.

I2Why do microbubbles scatter so much more than tissue or red cells of similar size? · screen

Rayleigh scattering from a small sphere has a compressibility (monopole) term \(((\kappa_s - \kappa)/\kappa)^{2}\) and a density (dipole) term. Gas is about 15 000 times more compressible than water, so the monopole term is about \(2.4\times 10^{8}\), against order \(10^{-3}\) for a tissue inhomogeneity of a few percent: some \(10^{11}\) per particle of equal size. Near resonance there is another factor of order \(1/\delta^{2}\) (about 20–200 for real bubbles). Follow-up: "Then why not use bigger bubbles?" They do not pass the lung capillaries, and \(\sigma_s\) above resonance saturates at \(4\pi R_0^{2}\).

I3Write the Rayleigh–Plesset equation with a Marmottant shell and explain each term. How would you integrate it? · onsite

\(\rho(R\ddot R + 1.5\dot R^{2}) = p_g(R)(1 - 3\kappa\dot R/c) - P_0\) \(- 2\sigma(R)/R - 4\mu\dot R/R - 4\kappa_s\dot R/R^{2} - P_{\mathrm{ac}}(t)\) (28.6): liquid inertia; polytropic gas with first-order radiation damping; ambient pressure; radius-dependent tension (0 buckled, \(\chi(R^{2}/R_b^{2} - 1)\) elastic, \(\sigma_w\) ruptured); liquid viscosity; shell viscosity; drive. Integrate \((R, \dot R)\) with RK4 or an adaptive RK45, 100–200 steps per period with refinement during collapse, and check convergence by halving the step (\(R_{\mathrm{min}}\) should not move). Follow-up: "Why not Euler?" Collapse is stiff; explicit Euler needs orders of magnitude more steps and loses energy accuracy.

I4Compare pulse inversion and amplitude modulation. What does each cancel and keep, and where does the signal live? · onsite

With \(e(a) = \sum a_n a^{n}p^{n}\): PI sums \(e(1) + e(-1) = 2a_2p^{2}\), the even orders, at \(2f_0\). AM forms \(e(1) - 2e(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12 a_2p^{2} + \tfrac34 a_3p^{3}\), which includes an odd-order nonlinear signal in the fundamental band: better penetration. AM needs an exact half amplitude (0.1 dB error leaves −38.7 dB of tissue); PI needs exact inversion. Both are hurt by motion: \(2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert\) with \(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF})\). Follow-up: "Which removes tissue harmonics better?" AM, because the half pulse carries a quarter of the tissue harmonic; PI keeps it entirely.

I5In a contrast mode you see bright tissue around the focus before the agent arrives. Explain and fix. · onsite

Nonlinear propagation: near the focus the tissue-generated second harmonic is largest (\(B_2/B_1 \approx \beta\omega P x/2\rho c^{3}\), −28 dB at 100 kPa, 2.5 MHz, 40 mm), and it is even in amplitude, so PI keeps it. Fixes: lower MI (the residue falls 6 dB per halving of \(P\), the bubble signal much less), move the focus deeper or use unfocused transmits, switch to AM or AM + PI, and check for motion residue and transmit asymmetry. Follow-up: "How would you measure it?" Image a tissue-mimicking phantom without agent in contrast mode and plot the residue against MI.

I6What is compression-only behaviour, and why does it matter for low-MI imaging? · research

A phospholipid bubble near its buckling radius has no surface tension in compression and a rising one in expansion (Marmottant). It compresses several times further than it expands (de Jong et al. 2007 observed it optically; the model gives a ratio of 3 at 10 kPa). That asymmetry is a strong even-order response at very low pressure, which is why PI and AM work at MI below 0.1, and it varies from bubble to bubble with the initial surface tension. Follow-up: "Consequence for quantification?" The echo is not proportional to concentration times incident pressure, so calibrations at one MI do not transfer to another.

I7How would you measure perfusion with flash-replenishment, and what limits its accuracy? · onsite

Constant infusion, low-MI imaging, a few high-MI frames to clear the slice, then fit \(I(t) = A(1 - e^{-\beta t})\) on linearized intensity: \(A \propto\) blood volume, \(\beta \propto\) velocity/slice thickness, \(A\beta \propto\) flow. Limits: incomplete destruction, bubble destruction by the imaging frames (biases \(\beta\) upward), attenuation and shadowing, receiver saturation of the plateau, beam elevation profile, speckle noise (\(\beta\) is the noisiest parameter, 12 % in the MATLAB example). Follow-up: "Why not fit the log-compressed curve?" The model is linear in concentration; the log map changes the shape (MTT doubles in the bolus example).

I8Why is the subharmonic attractive for contrast imaging, and where is its threshold lowest? · research

Tissue propagation does not generate \(f/2\), so a subharmonic image has a very high CTR. Period doubling has a threshold that is lowest for a drive near \(2f_0\), where \(f/2\) hits the resonance; for a free 2 µm bubble the model gives \(f/2\) at −30 dB at 100 kPa and −4 dB at 200 kPa. Costs: long bursts (poor axial resolution), the threshold behaviour, and the need for a transducer with bandwidth at \(f\) and \(f/2\). Follow-up: "What else is it used for?" Ambient-pressure estimation, because the subharmonic amplitude of some agents depends on hydrostatic pressure.

I9How do you choose the microbubble concentration for ULM? · research

Model bubbles in the image as a Poisson process with areal density \(n\); a bubble is usable if no other lies within \(k\) FWHM: \(P_{\mathrm{iso}} = e^{-nA}\), \(A = \pi k^{2}F_xF_z\) (28.15). The isolated count \(n\,e^{-nA}\) peaks at \(n^{*} = 1/A\) (\(P_{\mathrm{iso}} = 1/e\)). At 5 MHz with \(F_x = \lambda\), \(F_z = 0.75\lambda\): 4.5 per mm². Convert \(n = C\) × slice × vascular fraction, then remember that large vessels are locally much denser. Follow-up: "And the price of a lower concentration?" Capillaries are sampled more slowly in proportion (next question).

I10Why does ULM take minutes to map capillaries, and how would you shorten it? · research

A capillary of diameter \(d\) and speed \(v\) receives \(C\cdot\pi d^{2}v/4\) bubbles per second: about one per 140 s for 8 µm, 1 mm/s and 140 per µL. Frame rate does not help; the concentration is capped by overlap at \(n^{*} = 1/A\). Ways out: localize at higher densities (overlap-tolerant or sparsity-based estimators, learned localizers), shrink \(A\) (higher frequency, 3D removes elevation overlap), accept a coarser map for capillaries (density rather than tracks), or separate subpopulations (for example by acoustic signature). Follow-up: "What did Hingot et al. show?" That microvascular flow sets the compromise between resolution and acquisition time.

I11Your SVD-filtered ULM data miss the slow capillary flow seen in histology. Why, and what would you change? · research

The SVD cut separates by slow-time spectrum and spatial coherence. A bubble at 1 mm/s has a Doppler of 6.5 Hz at 5 MHz, inside the tissue-motion band, so it is projected out with tissue. Options: an adaptive cut chosen from the spatial-similarity of singular vectors, lower cut with better motion correction, longer ensembles for finer frequency resolution, or a nonlinear sequence (AM) that removes tissue regardless of velocity, accepting the motion residue and the extra transmits. Follow-up: "How would you validate?" A flow phantom with known slow flow, or a microchannel, reporting detection rate versus velocity.

I12A multicentre trial plans to compare peak enhancement and AUC between sites that use different scanners. What do you tell the steering committee? · onsite

That amplitude parameters measure the instrument as much as the tissue. PE and AUC scale with gain, attenuation above the ROI, local pressure, dose and each manufacturer's linearization; in the QIBA flow-phantom study they varied by up to about 50 % even between repeated trials on one scanner and were not comparable between scanners, while rise time agreed within 9 % and MTT within 21 % across four scanners (Averkiou et al. 2020). I would fix one preset per scanner (MI, focus, gain, TGC, dynamic range, frame rate), export linear data, express amplitude parameters as ratios to a reference ROI at the same depth or as within-patient change from baseline on the same scanner, as in the day-30 AUC ratio below 0.6 of Lassau et al., and use timing parameters for between-site comparison. Follow-up: "Why does a ratio help?" In linear intensity a gain change is a multiplicative factor that cancels in a ratio and leaves TTP and MTT unchanged; in log-compressed data it is an additive offset with clipping, so it does neither.

I13What do arterial phase hyperenhancement and washout mean in CEUS LI-RADS, and how can the scanner fake or hide a washout? · screen

APHE is enhancement above the surrounding liver in the arterial phase (from about 10–20 s to 30–45 s after injection) that is neither rim-like nor peripheral discontinuous globular. Washout is a later fall below the liver, graded by onset (early, before 60 s; late, at 60 s or later) and degree (marked if the observation is nearly devoid of enhancement within 2 min). In the v2017 core, ≥ 10 mm with non-rim APHE and late, mild washout is LR-5; rim APHE, early or marked washout suggest LR-M (Wilson et al. 2018). LR-5 had a 98.5 % positive predictive value for HCC in 1006 nodules (Terzi et al. 2018). The scanner fakes washout by destroying bubbles: continuous insonation at a given MI and frame rate removes them at the rate \(k_d\) of (28.14), so intermittent late-phase scanning is safer. It hides washout with a gain high enough to saturate the liver, and depth makes a deep lesion look darker through attenuation and lower local pressure. Follow-up: "Could you quantify washout instead?" Timing of the lesion-to-liver crossover is robust across systems; its amplitude is not.

28.14Self-check

Q1What is the resonance of a free 3 µm air bubble by Minnaert and with surface tension?

3.29/3 = 1.10 MHz by Minnaert; with \(\sigma\) = 0.072 N/m, \(\rho\omega_0^{2}R_0^{2} = 3(1.4)(101.3\ \mathrm{kPa})\) \(+\, 2(3.2)(0.072)/(3\ \mathrm{\mu m})\) = 425.6 + 153.6 kPa, so \(f_0\) = 1.28 MHz (+17 %).

Q2A lipid bubble with \(R_0\) = 1.5 µm, \(\kappa\) = 1.07, \(\chi\) = 0.5 N/m and \(\sigma_0\) = 0.02 N/m. Estimate \(f_0\).

\(3\kappa P_0\) = 0.325 MPa; \(2(3\kappa + 1)\sigma_0/R_0 = 2(4.21)(0.02)/1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) = 0.112 MPa; \(4\chi/R_0\) = 1.333 MPa. Sum 1.77 MPa; \(f_0 = \sqrt{1.77\times 10^{6}/1000}\,/\,(2\pi\times 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m})\) = 4.46 MHz (28.3).

Q3For a free 2 µm air bubble at resonance, give \(Q\) and \(\sigma_s/\pi R_0^{2}\).

\(\delta_{\mathrm{rad}} = \omega R_0/c = 0.017\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{vis}} = 4\mu/(\rho\omega R_0^{2}) = 0.078\) at 2.04 MHz: \(\delta\) = 0.095, \(Q\) = 10.5, \(\sigma_s/\pi R_0^{2} = 4/\delta^{2} = 440\) (thermal damping would lower both).

Q4Which sequence keeps the cubic term, with what weight, and in which band does it appear?

AM (\(e(1) - 2e(\tfrac12)\)) and AM + PI (\(e(1) + 2e(-\tfrac12)\)) both keep \(\tfrac34 a_3p^{3}\); \(p^{3}\) has components at \(f_0\) and \(3f_0\), and the \(f_0\) part is the "nonlinear fundamental". PI cancels all odd orders.

Q5Tissue moves at 5 mm/s, PRF 2 kHz, f = 3 MHz. What is the PI tissue residue?

\(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}) = 0.01/(1540\times 2000)\) = 3.25 ns; \(2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert = 2\sin(0.0306) = 0.061\), i.e. −24.3 dB.

Q6A bolus fits a lognormal with \(\mu = \ln 8\) (s) and \(s\) = 0.5, \(t_0\) = 4 s. Give MTT and TTP.

\(\mathrm{MTT} = 8e^{0.125}\) = 9.07 s (from \(t_0\)); \(\mathrm{TTP} = t_0 + 8e^{-0.25} = 4 + 6.23\) = 10.23 s (28.11).

Q7At 7.5 MHz with \(F_x = \lambda\), \(F_z = 0.75\lambda\) and \(k\) = 1, what bubble density maximizes isolated localizations, and how many per mm² per frame?

\(\lambda\) = 0.205 mm; \(A = \pi(0.205)(0.154)\) = 0.0993 mm²; \(n^{*}\) = 10.1 per mm²; maximum \(1/(eA)\) = 3.7 isolated bubbles per mm² per frame.

Q8Why does a 1 µm free bubble need more pressure than a 2 µm one to emit a subharmonic at \(2f_0\)?

Viscous damping scales as \(1/(\omega R_0^{2})\): \(\delta_{\mathrm{vis}}\) = 0.134 at 1 µm versus 0.078 at 2 µm (each at its own resonance), so the parametric growth needs a larger drive. In the model at 200 kPa the 1 µm bubble's \(f/2\) is −26 dB, the 2 µm bubble's −4 dB.

Q9An infusion study has a true refill rate \(\beta\) = 0.5 s\(^{-1}\), and imaging at 8 frames/s destroys 2 % of the bubbles in the slice per frame. What \(\beta\) and plateau will the replenishment fit report, and what happens to \(A\beta\)?

\(k_d = -8\ln(0.98)\) = 0.162 s\(^{-1}\), so \(\beta_{\mathrm{obs}}\) = 0.66 s\(^{-1}\) (+32 %) and \(A_{\mathrm{obs}} = A\cdot 0.5/0.662\) = 0.76 \(A\) (28.14). Their product equals the true \(A\beta\): the flow index survives, blood volume and velocity do not.
