Microbubble physics, contrast imaging and the ULM signal source

A contrast microbubble is a gas spring with a liquid mass, tuned to megahertz by its size and stiffened by its shell. Everything you do in CEUS and ULM follows from that oscillator: why bubbles outshine tissue, how pulse sequences separate them, how time–intensity curves measure perfusion, and why ULM must trade bubble density against acquisition time.

You will be able to

  • Derive the Minnaert resonance, correct it for surface tension, heat exchange and a lipid shell, and quote \(f_0R_0\) with its assumptions
  • Integrate the Rayleigh–Plesset equation with a Marmottant shell (RK4) and read harmonics, subharmonics and compression-only behaviour from the emitted spectrum
  • Predict what pulse inversion, amplitude modulation and their combination keep and reject, including the residues from tissue harmonics, motion and amplitude errors
  • Fit bolus and flash-replenishment time–intensity curves in the right (linear) domain
  • Size the ULM concentration from the PSF, and estimate how long capillaries take to fill

28.1The agent: gas core, shell, size, and why compressibility wins

Clinical agents are suspensions of gas microbubbles, 1–10 µm in diameter, stabilized by a monolayer of phospholipid or a thin shell of denatured albumin. The gas is a heavy, poorly soluble fluorinated gas because an uncoated micron bubble does not last: the Laplace pressure \(2\sigma/R\) (144 kPa at \(R\) = 1 µm in water) pushes gas into solution, and the Epstein–Plesset estimate for a 1 µm air bubble in air-saturated water is of order milliseconds. Low solubility and diffusivity of the core gas and a shell that cancels most of the surface tension stretch that to minutes.

Subscript s = sphere. A rigid fixed sphere (\(\kappa_s = 0\), \(\rho_s \to \infty\)) gives \(\sigma_s = (7\pi/9)k^{4}a^{6}\). A gas bubble below resonance has \(\kappa_s/\kappa = \rho c^{2}/(\gamma P_0) \approx\) 15 400 (air, adiabatic, 1 atm), so its monopole term is \(2.4\times 10^{8}\).

The ratio bubble to rigid sphere of the same size is \((4/7)(15\,400)^{2} \approx 1.4\times 10^{8}\), and against a tissue inhomogeneity with 5 % compressibility and density contrast it is about \(10^{11}\). One bubble is therefore visible on its own in a beamformed image, which is the physical basis of ULM.

Intuition

Tissue scatters because it is a little stiffer or denser than its surroundings. A bubble scatters because it is about 15 000 times softer: the same incident pressure makes it change volume 15 000 times more than the water it replaces, and a pulsating volume is a monopole source.

28.2The bubble as a mass on a gas spring: Minnaert and beyond

Mass. Incompressible radial flow around a sphere has velocity \(u(r) = R^{2}\dot R/r^{2}\). Its kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\int \rho u^{2}\, 4\pi r^{2}\, dr = \tfrac12 (4\pi R^{3}\rho)\, \dot R^{2}\), so the effective mass is \(m = 4\pi R_0^{3}\rho\), three times the mass of the displaced liquid. Spring. A polytropic gas gives \(p_g = P_0(R_0/R)^{3\kappa}\), so a radial displacement \(x\) changes the pressure by \(-3\kappa P_0 x/R_0\) over the area \(4\pi R_0^{2}\): the stiffness is \(s = 12\pi\kappa P_0 R_0\); hence (28.2).

\[ \begin{aligned} \omega_0^{2} &= \frac{s}{m} = \frac{3\kappa P_0}{\rho R_0^{2}} \\ \Rightarrow\quad f_0 R_0 &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{3\kappa P_0}{\rho}} \end{aligned} \]

Three corrections matter at micron sizes, and all three are larger than the difference between 3.26 and 3.29.

  • Surface tension. The gas sits at \(P_0 + 2\sigma/R_0\), and the tension term itself responds to \(R\). Linearizing gives \(\rho\omega_0^{2}R_0^{2} = 3\kappa P_0 + 2(3\kappa - 1)\sigma/R_0\). A free 1 µm air bubble resonates at 4.74 MHz, not 3.29 (+44 %); at 2 µm, 2.04 versus 1.64 MHz.
  • Heat exchange. The gas is adiabatic only if the thermal diffusion length \(\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{th}}/2\omega}\) is much smaller than \(R_0\). For air at 3 MHz it is 0.73 µm, comparable to the radius, so \(\kappa\) lies between 1 and \(\gamma\) and heat conduction adds a thermal damping (Prosperetti's analysis). For contrast agents the polytropic exponent is usually taken close to the heat-capacity ratio of the heavy gas (≈ 1.07–1.1), which is already near 1.
  • Shell. In the Marmottant model the elastic shell makes the surface tension radius dependent, \(\sigma(R) = \chi(R^{2}/R_b^{2} - 1)\). Its slope adds stiffness (28.3):

28.4Nonlinear signatures: harmonics, subharmonics, compression-only behaviour, destruction

Harmonics. The gas spring is asymmetric: \((R_0/R)^{3\kappa}\) stiffens without bound in compression and softens in expansion, and the liquid inertia makes the collapse faster than the growth. Near resonance the radial excursion is amplified by \(Q\), so a bubble becomes nonlinear at incident pressures where tissue propagation is still nearly linear. In the demo a free 2 µm bubble driven at its resonance has a second harmonic of −13 dB already at 10 kPa (MI 0.007; 20-cycle burst); the tissue second harmonic after 40 mm at 100 kPa and 2.5 MHz is −28 dB (Module 15).

Subharmonics and ultraharmonics. Above a threshold the oscillation doubles its period and emits at \(f/2\) (and \(3f/2\), \(5f/2\)). For a bubble the threshold is lowest when the drive is near twice the resonance, because the \(f/2\) response then falls on the resonance. For the free 2 µm bubble at \(2f_0\) the model gives \(f/2\) at −43, −30, −13 and −4 dB for 50, 100, 150 and 200 kPa: a threshold between about 100 and 175 kPa. A 1 µm bubble, with almost twice the viscous damping, stays below −25 dB up to 200 kPa. Tissue propagation creates no subharmonic, so subharmonic imaging has a very high contrast-to-tissue ratio, and because the subharmonic amplitude of some agents depends on ambient pressure it has been studied for noninvasive pressure estimation. Phospholipid bubbles show subharmonics at lower pressures than the free-bubble model predicts; single-bubble optical measurements found subharmonic thresholds as low as 5 kPa near twice the resonance, far below viscoelastic-shell predictions, and attribute them to the buckling regime of the shell (Sijl et al., JASA 2010).

Compression-only behaviour. A lipid bubble near the buckling radius has no tension in compression (\(\sigma = 0\), soft) but a rising tension in expansion (stiff). It therefore compresses much more than it expands, an effect observed optically by de Jong et al. (UMB 2007). In the model at 3 MHz the buckled bubble's compression/expansion ratio is 3.0 at 10 kPa and 2.5 at 50 kPa, against 1.1–1.4 for the elastic one. The asymmetry is an even-order nonlinearity at very low pressure: exactly what pulse inversion and amplitude modulation detect.

Destruction. As pressure rises the bubble sheds lipid, fragments, loses gas by diffusion, or collapses inertially. Rayleigh–Plesset cannot model fragmentation, but \(R_{\mathrm{max}}/R_0 \ge 2\) is the classic rough criterion for inertial collapse (Flynn); the analysis that led to MI (Apfel and Holland, UMB 1991) used a related collapse-temperature threshold of 5000 K. Clinical practice follows the same logic: real-time CEUS runs at low MI, below about 0.3 and often 0.05–0.1 on modern systems (EFSUMB guidance), to keep bubbles intact and nonlinear; a few high-MI "flash" frames destroy them on purpose for replenishment studies. MI is defined from the derated peak rarefactional pressure of the beam (Module 22), not from the pressure at a given bubble, so the bubble's local pressure depends on depth, attenuation and focusing.

28.5Contrast-specific pulse sequences

Write the echo of a scatterer as a power series in the transmit amplitude \(a\): \(e(a) = a_1 a\,p + a_2 a^{2}p^{2} + a_3 a^{3}p^{3} + \dots\), where the \(a_n\) are operators (filters) and \(p^{n}\) carries energy near \(n f_0\) (and, for odd \(n\), back at \(f_0\)). Linear tissue has only \(a_1\). A multi-pulse sequence transmits several scaled or inverted copies and adds the echoes with weights that cancel \(a_1\):

PI (Simpson, Chin and Burns, IEEE TUFFC 1999) needs no filter to separate harmonic from fundamental, so it keeps the full transducer bandwidth, but its signal lives at \(2f_0\), where attenuation is higher. AM keeps the odd-order "nonlinear fundamental", which propagates and is received at \(f_0\): better penetration, at the price of an exact half-amplitude transmit (systems commonly fire half of the elements to get it). Vendors combine amplitude and phase steps over three or more pulses (the "contrast pulse sequence" modes of commercial scanners); the algebra is always the same: choose weights that null \(a_1\) and keep the orders you want.

Three things leave tissue in a contrast image, and the demo models each:

  • Nonlinear propagation. The incident pulse at depth already contains a tissue-generated second harmonic. To second order \(p_{\mathrm{inc}} = a\,p_1 + a^{2}\,\frac{\beta x}{2\rho c^{3}}\,\frac{d(p_1^{2})}{dt}\), which survives PI (it is even in \(a\)) and is attenuated four times in the half pulse of AM. At 100 kPa, 2.5 MHz, \(x\) = 40 mm, \(\beta\) = 4.5 the CW ratio \(B_2/B_1 = \sigma/2\) is −28 dB.
  • Motion. A scatterer moving at \(v\) between pulses shifts by \(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF})\). For a linear scatterer the PI residue is \(\lvert 1 - e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\rvert = 2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert\) of the echo: 10 mm/s at PRF 5 kHz and 2.5 MHz gives −27.8 dB, at 5 MHz −21.8 dB.
  • Amplitude and phase errors. AM needs the half pulse to be exactly half: a gain error \(e\) (dB) leaves \(\lvert 1 - 10^{e/20}\rvert\) of the linear echo, −38.7 dB for 0.1 dB and −24.5 dB for 0.5 dB.

Pitfall

"PI removes tissue" is true only for linear tissue at rest. Near the focus at moderate MI, tissue harmonics from propagation survive PI exactly like bubble harmonics do; the remedies are lower MI, AM (its tissue-harmonic residue is a quarter of PI's, 12 dB lower) or a sequence that uses the bubble's odd orders. And every multi-pulse method is a high-pass filter in slow time: motion between pulses sets the floor.

28.6Perfusion quantification: low-MI imaging and time–intensity curves

Real-time low-MI imaging follows the agent through the microcirculation. Two acquisition schemes give two kinds of time–intensity curve (TIC) from a region of interest:

28.7Separating bubbles from tissue in slow time

Ultrafast ULM usually separates bubbles from tissue with the spatiotemporal SVD filter (Module 06; Demené et al., IEEE TMI 2015): stack \(N_t\) frames as a Casorati matrix, discard the largest singular components (tissue: spatially coherent, slowly varying, 30–60 dB brighter), keep the intermediate ones (moving bubbles), discard the smallest (noise). It exploits motion, not bubble physics. Two consequences matter for ULM design:

  • Slow bubbles look like tissue. A bubble moving axially at \(v\) has Doppler \(2vf/c\): 6.5 Hz at 1 mm/s and 5 MHz, the same Doppler as tissue moving at 1 mm/s, which is ordinary physiological motion. The SVD cut that removes tissue removes capillary bubbles too. Nonlinear sequences (AM, PI) remove tissue by physics and do not care about velocity, at the price of two or three transmits per frame and motion sensitivity (28.5).
  • Bubbles decorrelate. Between frames a bubble moves \(v/\mathrm{FR}\) (10 µm at 10 mm/s and 1 kHz), its amplitude changes as it crosses the beam and its echo flickers with its radial dynamics. Tracking needs displacements per frame well below the PSF size; very fast bubbles at low frame rates break the linking, and very slow ones sit inside the clutter band.

28.8ULM: the bubble as a point source, and the concentration trade-off

A point source with an unknown, flickering amplitude. A 2 µm bubble is \(\lambda/150\) of a 5 MHz wavelength, so its image is the system PSF times a complex amplitude. Localization precision is then set by the PSF width and the energy-to-noise ratio, \(\sigma_x \ge w_x/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}}\) (Module 12). The bubble physics enters through the amplitude: \(\sigma_s\) varies by orders of magnitude across the size distribution at a given frequency (28.1, 28.2), so the ENR, and therefore the precision, is a distribution, dominated by near-resonant and large bubbles. Choosing \(f\) for ULM trades PSF width (\(\propto 1/f\)) against bubble response (resonant population at lower \(f\)) and attenuation.

The time trade-off. A capillary is mapped only when bubbles pass through it. Safety and the MI budget in ULM

ULM needs the same bubbles to survive hundreds to thousands of frames, so it runs at low MI, and plane-wave sequences with no focus keep the peak pressure low. The time-averaged exposure is another matter: 0.5–1 kHz compounded frame rates raise \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA}}\) and TI compared with conventional imaging (Module 22). Contrast agents lower the pressure threshold for cavitation-related bioeffects; animal studies have shown microvascular effects when bubbles are destroyed at high MI (for example Skyba et al., Circulation 1998). The design rule is to keep the MI at the bubble below the destruction threshold of the agent and to report MI, frame rate, duration and agent dose together.

28.10How experts think about it

An expert pictures the bubble as a damped oscillator whose spring is gas plus shell and whose mass is three times the displaced liquid: resonance from size and stiffness, Q from shell viscosity, brightness from compressibility. For ULM the same expert thinks in densities and fluxes: the PSF sets how many bubbles fit in a frame, the vasculature sets how fast new ones arrive.

28.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Derive the resonance frequency of a gas bubble in water. What is it for \(R_0\) = 2 µm? · screen

Radial flow \(u = R^{2}\dot R/r^{2}\) has kinetic energy \(\tfrac12(4\pi R^{3}\rho)\dot R^{2}\): effective mass \(4\pi R_0^{3}\rho\). Polytropic gas: stiffness \(12\pi\kappa P_0R_0\). \(\omega_0^{2} = 3\kappa P_0/(\rho R_0^{2})\), \(f_0R_0 \approx\) 3.3 MHz·µm for air, so 1.64 MHz at 2 µm. Then I would add surface tension (2.04 MHz for a free 2 µm air bubble) and say which \(\kappa\) I assumed. Follow-up: "And for a lipid-coated agent?" Add \(4\chi/R_0\): with \(\chi\) = 0.5 N/m and a perfluorocarbon-like \(\kappa\) about 3 MHz.

I2Why do microbubbles scatter so much more than tissue or red cells of similar size? · screen

Rayleigh scattering from a small sphere has a compressibility (monopole) term \(((\kappa_s - \kappa)/\kappa)^{2}\) and a density (dipole) term. Gas is about 15 000 times more compressible than water, so the monopole term is about \(2.4\times 10^{8}\), against order \(10^{-3}\) for a tissue inhomogeneity of a few percent: some \(10^{11}\) per particle of equal size. Near resonance there is another factor of order \(1/\delta^{2}\) (about 20–200 for real bubbles). Follow-up: "Then why not use bigger bubbles?" They do not pass the lung capillaries, and \(\sigma_s\) above resonance saturates at \(4\pi R_0^{2}\).

I3Write the Rayleigh–Plesset equation with a Marmottant shell and explain each term. How would you integrate it? · onsite

\(\rho(R\ddot R + 1.5\dot R^{2}) = p_g(R)(1 - 3\kappa\dot R/c) - P_0\) \(- 2\sigma(R)/R - 4\mu\dot R/R - 4\kappa_s\dot R/R^{2} - P_{\mathrm{ac}}(t)\) (28.6): liquid inertia; polytropic gas with first-order radiation damping; ambient pressure; radius-dependent tension (0 buckled, \(\chi(R^{2}/R_b^{2} - 1)\) elastic, \(\sigma_w\) ruptured); liquid viscosity; shell viscosity; drive. Integrate \((R, \dot R)\) with RK4 or an adaptive RK45, 100–200 steps per period with refinement during collapse, and check convergence by halving the step (\(R_{\mathrm{min}}\) should not move). Follow-up: "Why not Euler?" Collapse is stiff; explicit Euler needs orders of magnitude more steps and loses energy accuracy.

I4Compare pulse inversion and amplitude modulation. What does each cancel and keep, and where does the signal live? · onsite

With \(e(a) = \sum a_n a^{n}p^{n}\): PI sums \(e(1) + e(-1) = 2a_2p^{2}\), the even orders, at \(2f_0\). AM forms \(e(1) - 2e(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12 a_2p^{2} + \tfrac34 a_3p^{3}\), which includes an odd-order nonlinear signal in the fundamental band: better penetration. AM needs an exact half amplitude (0.1 dB error leaves −38.7 dB of tissue); PI needs exact inversion. Both are hurt by motion: \(2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert\) with \(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF})\). Follow-up: "Which removes tissue harmonics better?" AM, because the half pulse carries a quarter of the tissue harmonic; PI keeps it entirely.

I5In a contrast mode you see bright tissue around the focus before the agent arrives. Explain and fix. · onsite

Nonlinear propagation: near the focus the tissue-generated second harmonic is largest (\(B_2/B_1 \approx \beta\omega P x/2\rho c^{3}\), −28 dB at 100 kPa, 2.5 MHz, 40 mm), and it is even in amplitude, so PI keeps it. Fixes: lower MI (the residue falls 6 dB per halving of \(P\), the bubble signal much less), move the focus deeper or use unfocused transmits, switch to AM or AM + PI, and check for motion residue and transmit asymmetry. Follow-up: "How would you measure it?" Image a tissue-mimicking phantom without agent in contrast mode and plot the residue against MI.

I6What is compression-only behaviour, and why does it matter for low-MI imaging? · research

A phospholipid bubble near its buckling radius has no surface tension in compression and a rising one in expansion (Marmottant). It compresses several times further than it expands (de Jong et al. 2007 observed it optically; the model gives a ratio of 3 at 10 kPa). That asymmetry is a strong even-order response at very low pressure, which is why PI and AM work at MI below 0.1, and it varies from bubble to bubble with the initial surface tension. Follow-up: "Consequence for quantification?" The echo is not proportional to concentration times incident pressure, so calibrations at one MI do not transfer to another.

I7How would you measure perfusion with flash-replenishment, and what limits its accuracy? · onsite

Constant infusion, low-MI imaging, a few high-MI frames to clear the slice, then fit \(I(t) = A(1 - e^{-\beta t})\) on linearized intensity: \(A \propto\) blood volume, \(\beta \propto\) velocity/slice thickness, \(A\beta \propto\) flow. Limits: incomplete destruction, bubble destruction by the imaging frames (biases \(\beta\) upward), attenuation and shadowing, receiver saturation of the plateau, beam elevation profile, speckle noise (\(\beta\) is the noisiest parameter, 12 % in the MATLAB example). Follow-up: "Why not fit the log-compressed curve?" The model is linear in concentration; the log map changes the shape (MTT doubles in the bolus example).

I8Why is the subharmonic attractive for contrast imaging, and where is its threshold lowest? · research

Tissue propagation does not generate \(f/2\), so a subharmonic image has a very high CTR. Period doubling has a threshold that is lowest for a drive near \(2f_0\), where \(f/2\) hits the resonance; for a free 2 µm bubble the model gives \(f/2\) at −30 dB at 100 kPa and −4 dB at 200 kPa. Costs: long bursts (poor axial resolution), the threshold behaviour, and the need for a transducer with bandwidth at \(f\) and \(f/2\). Follow-up: "What else is it used for?" Ambient-pressure estimation, because the subharmonic amplitude of some agents depends on hydrostatic pressure.

I9How do you choose the microbubble concentration for ULM? · research

Model bubbles in the image as a Poisson process with areal density \(n\); a bubble is usable if no other lies within \(k\) FWHM: \(P_{\mathrm{iso}} = e^{-nA}\), \(A = \pi k^{2}F_xF_z\) (28.12). The isolated count \(n\,e^{-nA}\) peaks at \(n^{*} = 1/A\) (\(P_{\mathrm{iso}} = 1/e\)). At 5 MHz with \(F_x = \lambda\), \(F_z = 0.75\lambda\): 4.5 per mm². Convert \(n = C\) × slice × vascular fraction, then remember that large vessels are locally much denser. Follow-up: "And the price of a lower concentration?" Capillaries are sampled more slowly in proportion (next question).

I10Why does ULM take minutes to map capillaries, and how would you shorten it? · research

A capillary of diameter \(d\) and speed \(v\) receives \(C\cdot\pi d^{2}v/4\) bubbles per second: about one per 140 s for 8 µm, 1 mm/s and 140 per µL. Frame rate does not help; the concentration is capped by overlap at \(n^{*} = 1/A\). Ways out: localize at higher densities (overlap-tolerant or sparsity-based estimators, learned localizers), shrink \(A\) (higher frequency, 3D removes elevation overlap), accept a coarser map for capillaries (density rather than tracks), or separate subpopulations (for example by acoustic signature). Follow-up: "What did Hingot et al. show?" That microvascular flow sets the compromise between resolution and acquisition time.

I11Your SVD-filtered ULM data miss the slow capillary flow seen in histology. Why, and what would you change? · research

The SVD cut separates by slow-time spectrum and spatial coherence. A bubble at 1 mm/s has a Doppler of 6.5 Hz at 5 MHz, inside the tissue-motion band, so it is projected out with tissue. Options: an adaptive cut chosen from the spatial-similarity of singular vectors, lower cut with better motion correction, longer ensembles for finer frequency resolution, or a nonlinear sequence (AM) that removes tissue regardless of velocity, accepting the motion residue and the extra transmits. Follow-up: "How would you validate?" A flow phantom with known slow flow, or a microchannel, reporting detection rate versus velocity.

28.14Self-check

Q1What is the resonance of a free 3 µm air bubble by Minnaert and with surface tension?

3.29/3 = 1.10 MHz by Minnaert; with \(\sigma\) = 0.072 N/m, \(\rho\omega_0^{2}R_0^{2} = 3(1.4)(101.3\ \mathrm{kPa})\) \(+\, 2(3.2)(0.072)/(3\ \mathrm{\mu m})\) = 425.6 + 153.6 kPa, so \(f_0\) = 1.28 MHz (+17 %).

Q2A lipid bubble with \(R_0\) = 1.5 µm, \(\kappa\) = 1.07, \(\chi\) = 0.5 N/m and \(\sigma_0\) = 0.02 N/m. Estimate \(f_0\).

\(3\kappa P_0\) = 0.325 MPa; \(2(3\kappa + 1)\sigma_0/R_0 = 2(4.21)(0.02)/1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) = 0.112 MPa; \(4\chi/R_0\) = 1.333 MPa. Sum 1.77 MPa; \(f_0 = \sqrt{1.77\times 10^{6}/1000}\,/\,(2\pi\times 1.5\ \mathrm{\mu m})\) = 4.46 MHz (28.3).

Q3For a free 2 µm air bubble at resonance, give \(Q\) and \(\sigma_s/\pi R_0^{2}\).

\(\delta_{\mathrm{rad}} = \omega R_0/c = 0.017\), \(\delta_{\mathrm{vis}} = 4\mu/(\rho\omega R_0^{2}) = 0.078\) at 2.04 MHz: \(\delta\) = 0.095, \(Q\) = 10.5, \(\sigma_s/\pi R_0^{2} = 4/\delta^{2} = 440\) (thermal damping would lower both).

Q4Which sequence keeps the cubic term, with what weight, and in which band does it appear?

AM (\(e(1) - 2e(\tfrac12)\)) and AM + PI (\(e(1) + 2e(-\tfrac12)\)) both keep \(\tfrac34 a_3p^{3}\); \(p^{3}\) has components at \(f_0\) and \(3f_0\), and the \(f_0\) part is the "nonlinear fundamental". PI cancels all odd orders.

Q5Tissue moves at 5 mm/s, PRF 2 kHz, f = 3 MHz. What is the PI tissue residue?

\(\tau = 2v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}) = 0.01/(1540\times 2000)\) = 3.25 ns; \(2\lvert\sin(\pi f\tau)\rvert = 2\sin(0.0306) = 0.061\), i.e. −24.3 dB.

Q6A bolus fits a lognormal with \(\mu = \ln 8\) (s) and \(s\) = 0.5, \(t_0\) = 4 s. Give MTT and TTP.

\(\mathrm{MTT} = 8e^{0.125}\) = 9.07 s (from \(t_0\)); \(\mathrm{TTP} = t_0 + 8e^{-0.25} = 4 + 6.23\) = 10.23 s (28.11).

Q7At 7.5 MHz with \(F_x = \lambda\), \(F_z = 0.75\lambda\) and \(k\) = 1, what bubble density maximizes isolated localizations, and how many per mm² per frame?

\(\lambda\) = 0.205 mm; \(A = \pi(0.205)(0.154)\) = 0.0993 mm²; \(n^{*}\) = 10.1 per mm²; maximum \(1/(eA)\) = 3.7 isolated bubbles per mm² per frame.

Q8Why does a 1 µm free bubble need more pressure than a 2 µm one to emit a subharmonic at \(2f_0\)?

Viscous damping scales as \(1/(\omega R_0^{2})\): \(\delta_{\mathrm{vis}}\) = 0.134 at 1 µm versus 0.078 at 2 µm (each at its own resonance), so the parametric growth needs a larger drive. In the model at 200 kPa the 1 µm bubble's \(f/2\) is −26 dB, the 2 µm bubble's −4 dB.
