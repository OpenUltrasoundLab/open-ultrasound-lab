Correlation & time-delay estimation

Speckle tracking, element-to-element aberration delays, sound-speed phase shifts and ULM bubble pairing all come down to one task: estimate a small shift between two noisy, partly decorrelated echoes. This module covers the estimators (normalized cross-correlation, subsample fits, phase of the complex correlation, Kasai), how biased they are, how small their variance can get (the Walker–Trahey bound), and where they fail (false peaks).

You will be able to

  • Explain why the cross-correlation peak is the ML delay estimate in white Gaussian noise, and when NCC is a usable quality metric
  • Choose a subsample interpolator and predict its bias curve (zero at 0 and 0.5 samples, maximal in between)
  • Evaluate the Walker–Trahey bound for given \(f_0\), \(B\), \(T\), SNR and \(\rho\), and recognise the false-peak threshold
  • Use lag-one autocorrelation (Kasai) on IQ, compute the Nyquist velocity, and reason about phase wrapping
  • Size a tracking kernel against strain decorrelation, and explain cumulative drift in neighbour-element phase-screen estimates

In 60 seconds
  1. Cross-correlation is the matched filter for delay: Sliding one echo over another to find the best match gives the most likely delay of a known signal in noise. In pulse-echo, 1 ns is 0.77 µm of displacement and one 40 MHz sample is 19.3 µm, so micrometre radiation-force displacements are sample fractions needing subsample estimation. §11.1
  2. Subsample interpolation is biased: Fitting a parabola or cosine through the correlation peak pulls estimates toward whole-sample lags, by about \((f_0/f_s)^2\) samples with f₀ the centre frequency and \(f_s\) the sample rate: 0.012 sample at 5 and 40 MHz. The bias is systematic, so averaging does not remove it; sinc upsampling shrinks it. §11.2
  3. Jitter has a floor set by decorrelation: Random delay error (jitter) falls with higher frequency, wider bandwidth and longer kernel (correlation window). Once echoes differ, decorrelation dominates and SNR stops helping: at 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth, 2 µs kernel and 30 dB SNR, the Walker–Trahey bound rises from 0.41 ns at correlation 1 to 1.35 ns at 0.99. §11.3
  4. The bound is local; peak hopping is not: Below a threshold SNR the estimate jumps to a side peak a whole RF period away, 200 ns at 5 MHz, and the error leaps far above the jitter bound. Narrow bands, short kernels and wide search ranges raise that threshold, so outliers must be reported separately from jitter. §11.3
  5. Phase estimators are cheap but bounded in range: The Kasai estimator reads velocity from the phase change between pulses at one complex multiply per sample, but wraps at \(v_\text{Nyq} = c\,\mathrm{PRF}/(4f_0)\), with c the sound speed, PRF the pulse rate and f₀ the centre frequency. Loupas also estimates the local mean frequency; correlation search handles large motion at far higher cost. §11.4
  6. Kernel length has an optimum under strain: Longer kernels reduce jitter but coarsen axial resolution and decorrelate when tissue is compressed (strained), so the best length shrinks as strain grows: about 1.5 µs at 1 % strain and 0.5 µs at 3 %, at 20 dB SNR. Kernels shorter than about two RF periods fail by peak hopping. §11.5
  7. Integrated delays random-walk: Summing adjacent-element delay estimates across an array adds their errors, so error grows as √n after n elements and one peak hop shifts every later element; correlating with a beamsum reference avoids this. Differentiating displacement into strain also amplifies jitter: \(\sigma_\varepsilon \approx \sqrt2\,\sigma_u/\Delta z\), with σ_u the displacement jitter and Δz the step. §11.6
From the field
  • Calibrate the subsample interpolator on simulated shifts with your own pulse, sampling and kernel. Interpolation bias is systematic and grows as the square of centre frequency over sample rate, so it survives averaging. Parabolic interpolation at 5 MHz is off by up to 0.012 sample at 40 MHz sampling and 0.052 sample at 20 MHz. §11.2
  • Choose the kernel length for the strain you expect. A longer correlation kernel reduces jitter only until strain decorrelation sets the floor, and it costs axial resolution. At 20 dB SNR the optimum is about 1.5 µs at 1 % strain but 0.5 µs at 3 %. §11.5
  • Gate estimates with a correlation-quality threshold, and report the outlier fraction separately from jitter. Below threshold SNR, errors jump by whole RF periods. With narrowband RF the side peaks one period away can reach 0.8 or more of the normalized correlation, so a high peak does not prove the right lobe. §11.1 §11.3
  • Estimate aberration by correlating each element with a beamsum reference, not with its neighbour. Chaining adjacent-element delays accumulates error as a random walk: 2 ns per pair grows to about 16 ns at element 64, and one peak hop offsets every later element. §11.6

For a record longer than the signal, \(\sum s^2[n - D]\) does not depend on \(D\), so maximising the likelihood equals maximising the correlation \(\sum y[n]\, s[n - D]\): a matched filter evaluated over all candidate delays.

With \(s\) unknown and both records noisy, the ML estimator (Knapp & Carter, 1976) is a generalized cross-correlation: multiply the cross-spectrum \(G_{xy}(f)\) by the weight \(\lvert\gamma(f)\rvert^2 / \bigl[\lvert G_{xy}(f)\rvert\,(1 - \lvert\gamma(f)\rvert^2)\bigr]\) before the inverse transform, where \(\gamma\) is the coherence between \(x\) and \(y\). For white electronic noise and an in-band SNR well above 1, \(\lvert\gamma\rvert^2/(1 - \lvert\gamma\rvert^2) \approx S(f)/(2N_0)\) and \(\lvert G_{xy}\rvert = S(f)\), so the weight is nearly flat inside the transducer band and falls off outside it: plain correlation of band-pass-filtered RF is already close to ML. Filter out-of-band noise first; that is the pre-whitening step of the GCC.

For a long window, the expected NCC peak is the correlation coefficient between the two windows:

\[ \mathrm{E}\bigl[\hat{\rho}_{\max}\bigr] \approx \rho \cdot \frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{1 + \mathrm{SNR}} \]
(11.6)
\(\hat{\rho}_{\max}\)

Biased vs unbiased correlation estimates, and FFT correlation

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{R}_b(k) &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=\max(0,-k)}^{\min(N-1,N-1-k)} x[n]\, y[n+k] \\ \hat{R}_u(k) &= \frac{1}{N - \lvert k\rvert} \sum_{n=\max(0,-k)}^{\min(N-1,N-1-k)} x[n]\, y[n+k] \end{aligned} \]
(11.7)

\(\mathrm{E}[\hat{R}_b(k)] = (1 - \lvert k\rvert/N)\, R(k)\): a triangular taper that pulls the peak toward \(k = 0\) (a delay bias that is small only when \(\lvert D\rvert \ll N\)). \(\hat{R}_u\) is unbiased but its variance blows up at large lags, and it is not guaranteed positive semidefinite. NCC with a fixed kernel and a sliding search window (as used in the demos) avoids both: every lag uses exactly \(W\) products.

FFT correlation computes \(X^*(f)\,Y(f)\) and inverts it. The DFT makes this a circular correlation (Module 04): lags beyond the record wrap around. Zero-pad both records to length \(\ge N_x + N_y - 1\) to obtain the linear correlation. For kernel \(W = 80\) and search ±24 lags, a direct sum costs 80 × 49 ≈ 3.9k multiply–adds per estimate; FFT correlation wins only for long windows or many lags (cost \(\propto N\log N\)).

Two-way geometry: \(\Delta z = cD/2\), so 1 ns of delay is 0.77 µm of axial displacement at \(c\) = 1540 m/s, and one sample at \(f_s\) = 40 MHz (25 ns) is 19.3 µm. ARFI displacements are 1–20 µm, which means 0.05–1 sample. Every practical tracker is therefore a subsample estimator, and its bias (Section 11.2) matters as much as its jitter (Section 11.3).

Pitfall

A high peak NCC does not guarantee a correct delay. With narrowband RF the side peaks at \(\pm 1/f_0\) can be 0.8 or higher. NCC tells you the two windows are similar at the chosen lag, not that you chose the right lobe. Use envelope or IQ-magnitude correlation for a coarse search and RF or phase for refinement, or regularize with neighbours.

Key takeaways
  • With a known signal, maximising the likelihood equals maximising the correlation; for white noise and in-band SNR well above 1, correlation of band-pass-filtered RF is already close to the generalized-correlation ML estimator.
  • Expected peak NCC ≈ ρ·SNR/(1 + SNR), so a 0.7–0.9 threshold rejects unreliable kernels, but with narrowband RF the side peaks at ±1/f0 can reach 0.8 or more: a high NCC does not prove the right lobe.
  • The biased estimate tapers R(k) by (1 − |k|/N) and pulls the peak toward zero; a fixed kernel with a sliding search window avoids it. FFT correlation is circular unless both records are zero-padded to ≥ Nx + Ny − 1.
  • Two-way geometry: 1 ns = 0.77 µm and one sample at 40 MHz = 19.3 µm, so ARFI displacements of 1–20 µm are 0.05–1 sample.

The extremes sit in between, near \(d \approx 0.3\) and 0.7 for the RF fits here. The parabolic and cosine fits underestimate \(\lvert d - k_0\rvert\), so the estimates cluster toward integer lags. That is pixel locking (in PIV and speckle-tracking terms, "peak locking"), and it shows up as a sawtooth-like texture in displacement maps. The Gaussian fit applied to RF errs the other way (toward half-samples), with twice the parabolic magnitude.

The parabolic bias depends on the peak curvature relative to the sampling interval, so on \(f_0/f_s\). With \(f_0\) = 5 MHz and \(f_s\) = 40 MHz, the RF correlation completes a cycle in 8 samples and the parabola underestimates \(\lvert\delta\rvert\) by about 0.012 samples (0.3 ns, 0.23 µm) at worst. That is small, but it is systematic: averaging does not remove it, and it does not cancel when you take spatial derivatives for strain.

Intuition

An RF correlation peak looks like a cosine under a Gaussian envelope. A parabola fitted through three points of a cosine is too flat at the top, so it pulls the estimate toward the centre sample. The cosine fit has the right shape and is nearly exact; residual bias comes only from the envelope. The phase estimator skips the shape question: it reads the delay from the angle of the complex correlation, which is linear in \(\tau\).

Pitfall

The phase estimator converts angle to delay with an assumed \(f_0\). Frequency-dependent attenuation lowers the received centre frequency with depth (Module 08: at 0.5 dB/cm/MHz and 60 % bandwidth, 5 MHz drops by 0.75 MHz at 4 cm), so a fixed \(f_0\) gives a depth-dependent scale error. Loupas' 2D autocorrelator (Section 11.4) estimates the local mean RF frequency from the same data and removes this error.

Ultrasound link

Pinton, Dahl & Trahey (2006) compared normalized cross-correlation and phase-shift estimators for ARFI-scale displacements (below half a wavelength). Loupas' 2D autocorrelator generally matched NCC accuracy at a fraction of the computational cost. Upsampled or sinc-interpolated NCC is the bias-free reference you would use for a phase-screen ground truth.

Key takeaways
  • Bias is zero at d = 0, ½ and 1 and odd about ½; parabolic and cosine fits pull estimates toward integer lags (pixel locking), the Gaussian fit on RF errs toward half-samples with twice the parabolic magnitude.
  • Parabolic bias at 5 MHz is 0.012 sample (0.3 ns) at fs = 40 MHz and 0.052 sample (2.6 ns) at 20 MHz, about (f0/fs)²; it is systematic, so averaging does not remove it.
  • Sinc upsampling shrinks the parabolic bias as U−3 until the interpolation kernel sets a floor; the phase estimator has no interpolation bias but a +10 % f0 error gives up to 0.045 sample, which Loupas removes by estimating the local mean frequency.

11.3Variance limits: the Walker–Trahey bound and the threshold effect

Bias is systematic; jitter is random. For two jointly Gaussian records with coherence \(\gamma(f)\), the Cramér–Rao bound on any unbiased delay estimate from a window of duration \(T\) is (Carter 1987, building on Knapp & Carter 1976):

\[ \sigma_D^2 \;\ge\; \Bigl[\, 2T \int_0^\infty (2\pi f)^2\, \frac{\lvert\gamma(f)\rvert^2}{1 - \lvert\gamma(f)\rvert^2}\, df \,\Bigr]^{-1} \]
(11.10)

\(\gamma(f)\)

\(f\)

\(T\)

The Fisher information grows with window length \(T\), with the second moment of frequency (high frequencies give sharp peaks), and with \(\lvert\gamma\rvert^2/(1 - \lvert\gamma\rvert^2)\), which diverges as the records become identical.

Walker & Trahey (1995) applied this to speckle: a rectangular band of centre \(f_0\) and fractional width \(B\), with the coherence set by both the electronic SNR and the speckle correlation \(\rho\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \lvert\gamma\rvert = \rho \cdot \frac{\mathrm{SNR}_p}{1 + \mathrm{SNR}_p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{\lvert\gamma\rvert^2} - 1 = \frac{1}{\rho^2}\Bigl(1 + \frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}^2}\Bigr)^2 - 1 \\ (\mathrm{SNR} = \text{amplitude ratio}, \ \mathrm{SNR}_p = \mathrm{SNR}^2) \end{gathered} \]

This is the Walker–Trahey bound: the Carter form evaluated with flat coherence across the band, derived by (11.10)–(11.12). \(f_0\) in Hz and \(T\) in s give \(\sigma_D\) in s. Example: \(f_0\) = 5 MHz, \(B\) = 0.6, \(T\) = 2 µs, SNR = 30 dB, \(\rho\) = 1 gives \(\sigma_D \ge\) 0.41 ns (0.31 µm); \(\rho\) = 0.99 gives 1.35 ns.

The bound scales as \(\sigma \propto f_0^{-3/2}\), \(\propto T^{-1/2}\) and \(\approx \propto B^{-1/2}\) (for \(B \lesssim 1\)); at high SNR it reaches a floor \(\sigma \propto \sqrt{1/\rho^2 - 1} \approx \sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\) set by decorrelation. Once \(\rho < 1\), raising the SNR stops helping beyond the point where \(1/\mathrm{SNR}_p \approx 1 - \rho\).

The threshold effect

The CRLB is a local bound: it describes small errors around the true peak. The RF correlation has side peaks at \(\tau \approx \pm m/f_0\). When noise or decorrelation makes a side peak exceed the main peak, the error jumps by a whole RF period (200 ns, or 154 µm in displacement at 5 MHz). The error distribution becomes a mixture: CRLB-like jitter plus outliers spaced \(1/f_0\) apart, ending in a uniform distribution over the search range. Below a threshold SNR (or \(\rho\), or \(T\cdot B\)) the variance rises far above the CRLB. The Ziv–Zakai and Barankin bounds (Weiss & Weinstein 1983) account for these "ambiguity" errors and reproduce this knee. Narrow bandwidth, short kernels and large search ranges all move the threshold to higher SNR.

(a) Near-field phase-screen estimation (Flax & O'Donnell 1988)

(b) Speckle tracking and elastography

Quasi-static elastography estimates displacement \(u(z)\) with NCC or phase tracking and differentiates it to get strain, \(\varepsilon \approx \Delta u/\Delta z\). Differentiation amplifies jitter: for two independent estimates spaced \(\Delta z\) apart, \(\sigma_\varepsilon \approx \sqrt{2}\,\sigma_u/\Delta z\). With \(\sigma_u\) = 0.3 µm and \(\Delta z\) = 1 mm, \(\sigma_\varepsilon \approx\) 0.04 % strain. ARFI and SWE track 1–20 µm displacements at PRFs of 5–10 kHz with Loupas or Kasai. For SWE on the uML15-3F class of probes, the shear-wave arrival time is itself a time-delay estimate between lateral positions, and the same bias/variance machinery applies to cross-correlating particle-velocity traces.

(c) Sound-speed estimation from delays and phase shifts

Computed ultrasound tomography in echo mode (CUTE; Jaeger et al. 2015) beamforms the same region with several transmit steering angles under an assumed \(c\). If the true \(c\) differs locally, echoes from a given point arrive with angle-dependent delay errors. The phase shift between complex (IQ) images from neighbouring angles, estimated with a Kasai-type lag-one product across angles, is a line integral of the slowness error along the two paths. A linear inverse problem (Batch 2) then recovers the local \(c\). The phase estimator's \(\pm\lambda/4\) wrap and its noise set the usable angle spacing; at 5 MHz, 1 ns corresponds to 1.8° of phase.

(d) ULM bubble tracking and pairing

After localization, ULM links the bubble positions in frame \(k\) to those in frame \(k+1\). Two families of methods do this: NCC of small image patches around each detection (the 2D version of 11.1), and global assignment that minimises total travel distance, solved with the Hungarian (Kuhn–Munkres) algorithm or a Kalman-filter variant. At 1 kHz and 10 mm/s the displacement per frame is 10 µm (≈ \(\lambda/30\) at 5 MHz). The search radius must exceed the maximum flow displacement but stay small enough to avoid mispairing in dense bubble populations. That is the same threshold effect as in 11.3, now in assignment space. See Target A (ULM).

11.8How experts think about it

Every delay estimator is a peak finder on a correlation function shaped like a cosine at \(f_0\) under an envelope of width ~\(1/B\). Experts separate three error types and size each before trusting a number: bias (interpolator shape mismatch, wrong \(f_0\)), jitter (the local CRLB, set by \(f_0\), \(B\), \(T\), SNR and \(\rho\)), and false peaks (whole RF periods, which no bound describes). Then they convert to µm with the two-way factor and ask which of the three dominates.

Cross-correlation is not a heuristic: in white Gaussian noise its peak is the maximum-likelihood delay, so fix the data (filter, whiten) before inventing a new estimator.

11.10Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why is the cross-correlation peak the maximum-likelihood delay estimate, and when is it not? · screen

For a known template in white Gaussian noise, the log-likelihood is \(-\sum (y - s_D)^2/2\sigma^2\); the energy of the shifted template does not depend on \(D\), so the likelihood is maximized by maximizing \(\sum y\,s_D\), the correlation. With both records noisy and the signal unknown, the ML processor is Knapp & Carter's generalized cross-correlation: weight the cross-spectrum by \(\lvert\gamma\rvert^2/[\lvert G_{xy}\rvert(1 - \lvert\gamma\rvert^2)]\). For white noise and high in-band SNR that is flat in band and small outside, so band-pass filtering plus plain correlation is close to ML. It fails for coloured noise or clutter (pre-whiten), for shape changes between records (strain, decorrelation), and below threshold, where the ML estimate is dominated by false peaks. Follow-up: what does PHAT weighting (\(1/\lvert G_{xy}\rvert\)) do? It whitens to phase only: sharp peaks and robustness to reverberation, but it amplifies low-SNR bands, so it is ML only when the coherence is constant across the band.

I2Compare parabolic, cosine and phase-zero subsample estimators: bias, variance, cost, failure modes. · onsite

All three start from the integer NCC peak. Parabolic: model-free and cheap, but the wrong shape for an RF peak; bias 0.012 sample at \(f_s/f_0\) = 8 and 0.052 at \(f_s/f_0\) = 4, toward the integer sample (pixel locking). Cosine: fits \(R \propto \cos(\omega(k - \delta))\), estimates \(\omega\) from the data, bias ≈ 0.0014 sample, residual from the envelope; same cost. Phase-zero: \(\delta = -\angle C(k_0)/(2\pi f_0 T_s)\) on the analytic signals, no interpolation bias if \(f_0\) equals the spectral centroid; biased by \((k_0 - d)\,e/(1+e)\) for an \(f_0\) error \(e\). Above threshold all three have jitter near the CRLB (Demo 11.3); bias, not jitter, separates them. Every symmetric fit is exact at \(d\) = 0 and ½. Follow-up: why is the Gaussian fit the worst on RF and the best on envelope data? Because it models a Gaussian peak: right for \(\lvert\mathrm{IQ}\rvert\) correlation, too sharp-shouldered for a cosine, so on RF it overshoots toward half-samples with 0.025 sample bias.

I3Derive the Kasai estimator and its Nyquist velocity. · screen

Slow-time IQ at a fixed gate: \(z[m] = A\,e^{j(\varphi_0 + m\Delta\varphi)} + n\), with the two-way path change per pulse \(2v/\mathrm{PRF}\), so \(\Delta\varphi = 2\pi f_0 (2v/c)/\mathrm{PRF}\) \(= 4\pi f_0 v/(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF})\). The lag-one autocorrelation \(R(1) = \sum z[m+1]\,z^*[m]\) \(\approx \lvert A\rvert^2 (M-1)\, e^{j\Delta\varphi}\), so \(\hat{v} = c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}\cdot\angle R(1)/(4\pi f_0)\). \(\angle R(1) \in (-\pi, \pi]\), so \(\lvert v\rvert < c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}/(4f_0)\): 0.385 m/s at 5 MHz, 5 kHz, i.e. \(\lambda/4\) = 77 µm per pulse. Equivalently \(\angle R(1)/(2\pi T_{\mathrm{PRF}})\) is the mean Doppler frequency of the slow-time spectrum. Follow-up: is it optimal? Not for a tone at high SNR: the sum telescopes to the first and last pulses, \(\sigma \propto 1/(M-1)\), variance 1.8× the CRLB at \(M\) = 8. Loupas adds the fast-time lag to estimate the local \(\bar{f}\) and remove downshift bias.

I4Why do false peaks appear at multiples of the RF period, and how do you suppress them? · onsite

The expected RF correlation is the inverse transform of the power spectrum: an envelope of width ~\(1/B\) times \(\cos(2\pi f_0\tau)\). Each cosine maximum near \(\tau = m/f_0\) is a local peak with height equal to the envelope there; for a Gaussian pulse, \(\exp(-\pi^2 B^2/(8\ln 2))\) at \(m = 1\): 0.53 at 60 % bandwidth, 0.93 at 20 %. Noise, decorrelation and the finite kernel can push one above the main peak, giving errors of exactly one period (200 ns, 154 µm at 5 MHz). Suppression: wider bandwidth; longer kernels (at least ~5 periods); a coarse search on envelope or \(\lvert\mathrm{IQ}\rvert\) correlation, then RF or phase refinement; small search ranges guided by neighbours or a previous frame; spatial regularization or median filtering of the displacement field; rejecting low-NCC kernels. Follow-up: why do hops persist at high SNR with a 2.5-period kernel? A short window of speckle is nearly a sinusoid, so the window one period away matches it almost as well; the finite-kernel fluctuation, not the noise, decides.

I5State the Walker–Trahey bound and give its scalings. What jitter do you expect at 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth, 2 µs kernel, 30 dB? · onsite

\(\sigma_D \ge \sqrt{\frac{3}{2\pi^2 f_0^3 T(B^3+12B)}\bigl[\frac{(1+1/\mathrm{SNR}^2)^2}{\rho^2} - 1\bigr]}\) (11.13), the Carter coherence CRLB for a rectangular band with flat coherence \(\rho\cdot\mathrm{SNR}_p/(1+\mathrm{SNR}_p)\). Scalings: \(f_0^{-3/2}\), \(T^{-1/2}\), \(\approx B^{-1/2}\) for \(B < 1\), and a decorrelation floor \(\propto \sqrt{1/\rho^2 - 1}\). Numbers: prefactor \(8.2\times10^{-17}\) s², bracket 0.002 → 0.41 ns (0.31 µm); with \(\rho\) = 0.99, 1.35 ns. Follow-up: your simulation reaches 0.3 ns at 40 dB but the bound says 0.13 ns. Why? Interpolator bias and window-edge decorrelation (\(W - \lvert D\rvert\) overlap) set a floor; the bound assumes an unbiased estimator and an infinite stationary record.

I6How do the CRLB and the Ziv–Zakai bound relate, and what is the threshold? · research

The CRLB is local: it uses the Fisher information, the curvature of the log-likelihood at the true delay, so it only describes small errors around the right peak. The Ziv–Zakai bound converts estimation into binary detection: for a delay with a uniform prior of width \(A\), \(\mathrm{MSE} \ge \frac{1}{A}\int_0^A h\,(A - h)\,P_{\min}(h)\,dh\), where \(P_{\min}(h)\) is the minimum error probability of deciding between delays \(D\) and \(D + h\) (a valley-filling refinement tightens it). At high SNR only small \(h\) contribute and it converges to the CRLB. At low SNR \(P_{\min}(h)\) stays near ½ even for large \(h\), notably near \(h = m/f_0\) where the correlation side peaks are high, and the bound rises toward the prior variance \(A^2/12\) (a standard deviation of 346 ns for ±24 lags at 40 MHz). The SNR where the side-peak terms switch on is the threshold. Follow-up: how would you design around it? Increase \(T\cdot B\) or SNR until the side-peak error probability is negligible, shrink \(A\) with prior information (tracking, coarse-to-fine), and report outlier rates separately.

I7How do you choose the kernel length for quasi-static elastography or speckle tracking? · onsite

Three constraints. (1) Jitter: \(\sigma \propto T^{-1/2}\) through the bound. (2) Decorrelation: under strain \(\varepsilon\) the window ends drift by \(\varepsilon T/2\), and \(1 - \rho^2\) grows as \((\varepsilon T f_0)^2\); the optimum is where the noise and decorrelation terms balance, about \(\varepsilon T f_0 \approx 0.075\) at 20 dB (\(T \approx\) 1.5 µs at 1 %, 5 MHz, \(\sigma \approx\) 2.1 ns; Monte Carlo 2.5 ns). (3) Resolution and false peaks: axial resolution ≈ \(cT/2\) (1.16 mm for 1.5 µs), and kernels below ~5 periods hop. In practice: 5–15 periods with 50–80 % overlap, companding or multi-step (coarse-to-fine) tracking for strains above 2–3 %, and a final displacement-gradient estimator over several points. Follow-up: what is the strain filter? Varghese & Ophir's curve of elastographic SNR versus applied strain: bounded at low strain by jitter and at high strain by decorrelation.

I8How would you estimate a near-field phase screen from channel data, and what limits it? · research

Model a thin delay screen \(\tau_i\) at the aperture. Cross-correlate each element with its neighbour (Flax & O'Donnell) on speckle or a point target, integrate the \(\Delta\hat{\tau}\), apply the correction, re-transmit and iterate, since a better transmit focus raises the inter-element coherence. Limits: (1) \(\rho\) between neighbours is set by van Cittert–Zernike (\(1 - 1/N\) at focus for a uniform \(N\)-element transmit), so jitter has a decorrelation floor; (2) integration is a random walk, \(\sqrt{n}\,\sigma_\Delta\) (\(\sqrt{63} \times 2\) ns ≈ 16 ns at element 64 for \(\sigma_\Delta\) = 2 ns), and one false peak adds a \(1/f_0\) step to every later element; (3) mean and linear terms are unobservable from diffuse speckle; (4) the thin-screen, single-isoplanatic-patch assumption fails for distributed aberrators, where the correction is only valid near the region used. Better: correlate against a beamsum reference, multi-lag least squares with NCC weights, or estimate from the full set of pairs (distortion or reflection matrix methods). Follow-up: why does multi-lag LS sometimes not help? If pair errors are dominated by Markov-like speckle decorrelation, a lag-\(l\) pair is as noisy as \(l\) adjacent pairs and carries little new information; longer lags also hop more.

I9A displacement map shows a faint sawtooth texture with a period of one axial sample. What is it, and how do you remove it? · screen

Pixel (peak) locking: the subsample interpolator is biased toward integer lags, with zero bias at 0 and ½ sample and extremes in between, so a smoothly varying true displacement acquires a periodic error of one-sample period. At \(f_s/f_0\) = 8 the parabolic amplitude is ~0.012 sample (0.23 µm); after differentiation it becomes a strain artefact. Fixes: cosine fit or band-limited upsampling of the correlation, phase-zero estimation on IQ with the local \(\bar{f}\) (Loupas), or a higher sampling rate. Averaging does not help because the error is deterministic in \(d\). Follow-up: can you calibrate and subtract the bias curve? Only approximately: each kernel's interpolation error varies with its own local spectrum (±0.011 sample in m11_ncc_subsample_bias.m), comparable to the mean bias.

I10Your ARFI phase tracker underestimates displacement by about 15 % at 4 cm depth in liver, but not near the surface. Why? · onsite

Frequency-dependent attenuation shifts the received centre frequency down with depth (\(f_c = f_0 - 2\alpha_0\sigma_f^2 z\) for a Gaussian spectrum: 0.75 MHz at 4 cm for 0.5 dB/cm/MHz, 5 MHz, 60 %). The phase measures \(2\pi f_c D\), and dividing by the nominal \(f_0\) gives \(\hat{D} = D\cdot f_c/f_0 = 0.85\,D\). Fix: Loupas' 2D autocorrelator, which estimates the local mean frequency from the fast-time lag-one correlation, or a depth-dependent \(f_c(z)\) from the spectral centroid. Follow-up: does NCC with parabolic interpolation have the same problem? No scale error, because it measures time directly; its bias depends on \(f_s/f_c\) and is small at 40 MHz.

I11Why is lateral speckle tracking so much worse than axial, and what would you do about it? · onsite

Jitter scales as \(1/\sqrt{-R''(0)}\). Axially the RF correlation carries the \(f_0\) carrier, so its curvature is \((2\pi f_{z0})^2 + 1/(2\sigma_z^2)\); laterally there is only the beam envelope, \(1/(2\sigma_x^2)\). For 5 MHz and a 0.6 mm lateral FWHM the predicted ratio is about 15. Lateral sampling is also coarse (line spacing comparable to the beam), and on RF a separable 2D fit through the integer axial row couples axial subsample offsets into the lateral estimate (15.5 µm vs 2.7 µm with axial-first fitting in m11_block_matching_2d.m). Remedies: fit axially first or fit a 2D model with a cross term; denser lateral sampling or interpolated beams; transverse-oscillation beamforming or angle compounding to create a lateral carrier; combine axial estimates from several steering angles. Follow-up: why does the envelope help laterally but hurt axially? It removes the carrier: the axial curvature drops (axial error 3–4× worse) and the coupling disappears.

I12How would you use time-delay estimation to measure local sound speed? · research

Beamform the same region from several transmit (or receive) steering angles with an assumed \(c\). A local sound-speed error makes the echo from a given point arrive with angle-dependent delay errors, a line integral of the slowness error along each path. Measure the phase shift between complex images from neighbouring angles with a Kasai-type lag-one product (CUTE, Jaeger et al. 2015), stack these into a linear system relating phase shifts to the slowness map, and solve it with regularization. At 5 MHz, 1 ns of delay is 1.8° of phase, so the \(\pm\lambda/4\) wrap and the phase jitter fix the usable angle step and the averaging region; the Walker–Trahey bound with the inter-angle coherence predicts the noise. Follow-up: what limits it? Decorrelation between angles (larger steps lower \(\rho\)), the ill-conditioning of limited-angle tomography (poor axial resolution of \(c\)), and the \(f_0\) used to convert phase to delay.

11.12Self-check

Q1An ARFI tracker reports a peak displacement of 0.4 samples at \(f_s\) = 40 MHz. What is that in ns of two-way delay and in µm of axial displacement (\(c\) = 1540 m/s)?

0.4 × 25 ns = 10 ns of two-way delay. \(\Delta z = cD/2 = 1540 \times 10\times10^{-9}/2\) = 7.7 µm. Forgetting the factor 1/2 (treating the delay as one-way) doubles the displacement: a common error when porting radar or sonar code.

Q2Compute the Nyquist velocity for \(f_0\) = 5 MHz and PRF = 5 kHz. What is the maximum displacement between pulses before the Kasai phase wraps?

\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}/(4f_0)\) \(= 1540 \cdot 5000 / (4 \cdot 5\times10^6)\) = 0.385 m/s. Per pulse this is \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}/\mathrm{PRF}\) = 77 µm = \(\lambda/4\) (\(\lambda\) = 308 µm). In two-way delay that is half an RF period, 100 ns: the phase step reaches \(\pi\).

Q3Why is the bias of every symmetric three-point interpolator exactly zero at \(d\) = 0 and \(d\) = 0.5 samples, and largest in between?

\(R(k) = R_{ss}((k-d)T_s)\) with \(R_{ss}\) even. At \(d = 0\) the samples are symmetric about \(k = 0\) (\(R_- = R_+\)), so any symmetric fit returns 0. At \(d = 0.5\) the samples are symmetric about \(k = 0.5\) (\(R(0) = R(1)\), \(R(-1) = R(2)\)), so the fit returns 0.5. Mirroring the whole configuration about ½ gives \(\mathrm{bias}(d) = -\mathrm{bias}(1-d)\), so the bias is odd about ½. A smooth odd function with zeros at 0, ½ and 1 has its extremes in between: near 0.3 and 0.7 for the RF parabola at \(f_s/f_0\) = 8. (In expectation: one finite speckle kernel is not exactly symmetric.)

Q4For broadband RF speckle at \(f_s/f_0\) = 8, rank parabolic, Gaussian, cosine, upsample ×8 and phase-zero estimators by worst-case bias.

From Demo 11.2 at 5 MHz, 60 %, 40 MHz: phase-zero (0, if \(f_0\) is exact) < upsample ×8 + parabolic (≈ \(4\times10^{-4}\) sample) < cosine (≈ \(1.4\times10^{-3}\)) < parabolic (≈ 0.012) < Gaussian (≈ 0.025). The Gaussian fit models an envelope peak, not an RF cosine peak, so it is the worst choice on RF and the right one on envelope or \(\lvert\mathrm{IQ}\rvert\) correlation. Céspedes et al. (1995) reached the same ordering for parabolic vs cosine on RF echoes. With an \(f_0\) error the phase estimator loses its advantage (5 % error gives about 0.025 sample).

Q5Evaluate the Walker–Trahey bound for \(f_0\) = 5 MHz, \(B\) = 0.6, \(T\) = 2 µs, SNR = 30 dB, \(\rho\) = 1, then for \(\rho\) = 0.99. What does doubling \(T\) do in each case?

\(3/(2 f_0^3 \pi^2 T (B^3+12B))\) = 3/(2 · 1.25·10²⁰ · 9.87 · 2·10⁻⁶ · 7.416) = \(8.2\times10^{-17}\) s². With \(\mathrm{SNR}_p\) = 1000 the bracket is \((1.001)^2 - 1\) = 0.002, so \(\sigma \ge \sqrt{1.64\times10^{-19}}\) = 0.41 ns. With \(\rho\) = 0.99 the bracket is 1.002/0.9801 − 1 = 0.0223, so \(\sigma \ge\) 1.35 ns: decorrelation dominates. Doubling \(T\) divides both by \(\sqrt{2}\) (0.29 ns, 0.96 ns), but only if \(\rho\) does not fall with \(T\), which it does under strain (Section 11.5).

Q6Monte Carlo at 5 dB SNR gives \(\sigma\) = 100 ns while the CRLB is 8 ns. Is the estimator broken?

No. This is the threshold region: some trials pick a side peak one or more periods (200 ns at 5 MHz) away, so the error distribution is a mixture and its variance is dominated by those outliers (e.g. a quarter of trials hopping one period: \(0.25 \times 200^2 \approx (100\ \mathrm{ns})^2\); m11_jitter_vs_crlb.m sees 36 % outliers, some multi-period, and 202 ns at 5 dB). The CRLB only describes local errors. Lengthen the kernel, widen the bandwidth, shrink the search range, use envelope correlation for the coarse search, or reject low-NCC kernels.

Q7Adjacent-element delay estimates have \(\sigma_\Delta\) = 3 ns. After integrating across 64 elements, what is the expected error at the far end, and how would you reduce it?

A random walk: \(\sigma_{64} \approx \sqrt{63} \times 3 \approx\) 24 ns, about 43° of phase at 5 MHz, comparable to a moderate aberrator. Remedies: correlate each element against a beamsum (or a running corrected sum) reference so errors do not accumulate; solve a least-squares problem using lags 1, 2, 3 (overdetermined, which damps the drift); weight each \(\Delta\tau\) by its NCC; iterate the correction, since a better focus raises \(\rho\).

Q8Why does FFT-based correlation of two 256-sample records without zero padding give wrong delays for large shifts?

The inverse DFT of \(X^*Y\) is the circular cross-correlation (Module 04): the lag-\(k\) value includes products of samples that wrapped around from the other end. For large \(k\) most products are wrapped, unrelated content, and the peak can move. Zero-padding to ≥ 511 samples (use 512) makes circular equal linear. Normalizing by \(\sqrt{\text{energy}}\) of the actually overlapping segments gives true NCC.

Inter-element coherence versus lag: theory against in vivo tissue

M. A. Lediju, G. E. Trahey, B. C. Byram, J. J. Dahl, "Short-lag spatial coherence of backscattered echoes: imaging characteristics," IEEE Trans. Ultrason. Ferroelectr. Freq. Control 58(7):1377–1388, 2011. doi:10.1109/TUFFC.2011.1957 (author copy: PULSE Lab). Fig. 1.

Fig. 1 plots the correlation of RF echoes between receive elements against their separation (lag) for a point target, for diffuse speckle and for in vivo thyroid tissue. Diffuse scatter gives the van Cittert–Zernike triangle falling from 1 at zero lag to 0 at lag \(N-1\); the thyroid curve falls below it, which the authors attribute to clutter, phase aberration and noise, with the largest coherence losses at short lags. That short-lag value is exactly the adjacent-element \(\rho\) that sets the decorrelation floor of (11.13) in phase-screen estimation: the theoretical \(1 - 1/N\) is a best case that real tissue does not reach.

Subpixel localization errors of seven ULM algorithms, benchmarked

B. Heiles, A. Chavignon, V. Hingot et al., "Performance benchmarking of microbubble-localization algorithms for ultrasound localization microscopy," Nature Biomedical Engineering 6:605–616, 2022. doi:10.1038/s41551-021-00824-8. Fig. 4 and Fig. 5.

Fig. 4 shows the directional localization errors and statistical indices of each algorithm on the "in silico flow" dataset at 30 dB; Fig. 5 studies how a non-uniform PSF in different directions affects localization. Read them as the 2D, single-frame version of Section 11.2: a localizer is a subsample peak estimator on an envelope image, its errors depend on the assumed peak shape (weighted average, interpolation, Gaussian fit), and directional bias toward pixel centres is the localization analogue of pixel locking.

Localization and frame-to-frame tracking of bubbles in the rat brain

C. Errico, J. Pierre, S. Pezet et al., "Ultrafast ultrasound localization microscopy for deep super-resolution vascular imaging," Nature 527:499–502, 2015. doi:10.1038/nature16066. Fig. 1 and Fig. 2.

Fig. 1 gives the principle of ultrafast ULM (plane-wave acquisition, clutter rejection, localization, tracking); Fig. 2 quantifies spatial resolution and flow in the rat cortex through a thinned skull. The pairing step of Section 11.6(d) is what turns localizations into the velocities of Fig. 2: at kilohertz frame rates bubbles move a small fraction of a wavelength per frame, so the search radius, not the localization precision, decides whether neighbours are mispaired.

Phase-shift tracking between transmit angles for sound-speed maps

P. Stähli, M. Kuriakose, M. Frenz, M. Jaeger, "Forward model for quantitative pulse-echo speed-of-sound imaging," arXiv:1902.10639v2 (2019); published as "Improved forward model for quantitative pulse-echo speed-of-sound imaging," Ultrasonics 108, 2020. Fig. 2 and Fig. 7 (arXiv numbering).

The delay estimator here is the phase of the kernel-averaged Hermitian product of complex images from two transmit angles (2 × 2 mm kernel), tracked in 2° steps and accumulated over 10° to stay inside the \(\pm\pi\) wrap; Fig. 2 relates the measured phase shift to the shift of the echo-envelope centroid, and Fig. 7 shows the resulting B-mode and speed-of-sound images of a volunteer's abdominal wall and liver.
