Matrices as operators

Linear algebra rebuilt as geometry. A matrix is a machine that moves vectors, and a beamformer, a phase-screen correction, a clutter filter and a sound-speed tomogram are all such machines. This module sets the vocabulary (subspaces, projections, conditioning, unitary bases) that Modules 06 and 07 and the three targets rely on.

You will be able to

  • Read \(A\mathbf{x}\) as a weighted sum of columns and predict what a 2×2 matrix does to the plane from det, trace and rank.
  • Name the four fundamental subspaces and decide whether \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) has zero, one or infinitely many solutions.
  • Derive least squares from orthogonality, build the projector \(P = A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}\) and use the pseudo-inverse.
  • Compute κ, predict noise amplification and say what Tikhonov regularization trades.
  • Write DAS, apodization, phase-screen correction and SoS tomography as matrices, and move between element and plane-wave bases with a unitary DFT.

In 60 seconds
  1. Complex inner products conjugate: Similarity between complex vectors needs one of them conjugated, \(\langle x, y\rangle = y^Hx\), with H the conjugate transpose; otherwise the nonzero vector (1, i) gets length zero. The normalized product is a cosine of similarity. The coherence factor, a focusing-quality metric, is this cosine squared between the delayed channel data and an all-ones vector. §05.1
  2. Structure decides cost: How a matrix is built matters more than its size. A diagonal operator costs n operations, a circulant one n log n via the FFT, a sparse one only its nonzeros. Time-gain compensation and apodization, per-sample and per-channel weights, are diagonal matrices on channel data, so spotting structure first makes imaging operators tractable. §05.2
  3. Null spaces hide parts of the unknown: In Ax = b, with A the measurement matrix, x the unknown and b the data, x is unique only if no nonzero x maps to zero. Whatever part of x lies in that null space, such as parts of a limited-angle sound-speed map, comes from the regularizer or prior, not the data. §05.4
  4. Least squares is an orthogonal projection: The best fit leaves a residual perpendicular to every column of A, which gives the normal equations \(A^H A\hat x = A^H b\), with \(\hat x\) the estimate and \(A^H\) the conjugate transpose (\(A^T\) for real A). Forming \(A^HA\) squares the condition number, the error amplification, so solve with QR or SVD factorizations instead. A squared-error fit is also pulled hard by one outlier. §05.5
  5. Conditioning sets the digits you lose: The condition number κ, the ratio of a matrix’s strongest to weakest gain (largest to smallest singular value), bounds error growth: about \(\log_{10}\kappa\) digits are lost. Near-parallel columns are the usual cause; two unit columns 10° apart give κ = 11.4. Regularization damps the weak directions, trading noise for bias. §05.6
  6. Unitary maps change basis without cost: A unitary matrix Q, one with \(Q^HQ = I\), rotates coordinates without changing energy or white-noise statistics, so its condition number is 1. The DFT across an array is one: a plane wave lands in one spatial-frequency bin. A thin aberrating layer is diagonal across elements but circulant across plane-wave angles. §05.7
  7. Imaging is a linear operator: Under single scattering, channel data equal a matrix H times the reflectivity map plus noise; column j of H is the data from a unit scatterer at pixel j. Delay-and-sum beamforming applies the adjoint \(H^H\), not the inverse, so its image is reflectivity blurred by the point-spread function. §05.8
From the field
  • Write the whole chain as y = Ax once, then examine A before choosing an algorithm. Rank and conditioning of A, the matrix mapping unknown x to data y, decide what no reconstruction recovers: anything A maps to zero is lost. Two unit columns 10° apart give a condition number (error amplification) of 11.4. §05.4 §05.6
  • Never invert and never form AᵀA (AᴴA for complex data): solve with backslash, QR or LSQR (an iterative least-squares solver). Explicit inversion and the normal equations compound rounding error. On the 10 × 10 Hilbert matrix the residual is 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁶ with backslash and 4.4 × 10⁻⁵ with inv(A)*b. §05.5
  • Exploit structure in imaging-sized operators: sparsity, FFTs, matrix-free products. Dense matrices are impossible at this size, but iterative solvers need only products with A and its adjoint. Delay-and-sum from 2048 × 128 samples to a 256 × 256 image is a 65 536 × 262 144 matrix with 16.8 M nonzeros, about 0.1 % dense. §05.2 §05.8
  • For a large ill-posed problem, treat the LSQR iteration count as a regularization parameter. Stopping early acts like discarding the small singular values, the weak directions; running to convergence amplifies noise as one over the singular value. §05.12
  • Plot the residuals of every least-squares slope fit before quoting the slope. Squared-error fitting gives one bad point leverage that grows with its distance from the data centre. In the module’s attenuation fit, one +6 dB outlier at 7.75 MHz moves the slope by +0.20 dB/MHz, about three standard errors. §05.5
  • Centre the regressors before fitting a line or polynomial in frequency. Raw frequency columns are nearly parallel to the constant column, which ill-conditions the fit. For the module’s fit the condition number of [1 f] drops from 16.2 to 1.66 after centring; a raw order-7 polynomial reaches 2.6 × 10⁹. §05.6

05.1Vectors, inner products and the conjugate

A vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\) or \(\mathbb{C}^{n}\) is an ordered list of numbers and, geometrically, an arrow. You already handle such vectors every day: one RF line (n = number of samples), one snapshot across the array at fixed time (n = 128 element samples), or a whole IQ frame stacked into one long column (\(n = N_{z}N_{x}\)). Everything below treats these lists as points in a space where length and angle are defined, so that "similar", "orthogonal" and "closest" become computable.

\[ \langle \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = \mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x} = \sum_i x_i\, y_i^{*}, \qquad \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert = \sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle} = \Big(\sum_i \lvert x_i\rvert^{2}\Big)^{1/2} \]

\((\cdot)^{H}\) is the conjugate (Hermitian) transpose. For real vectors \((\cdot)^{H} = (\cdot)^{T}\) and the conjugate does nothing. This lab conjugates the second argument; physics texts often conjugate the first. Magnitudes agree, phases flip sign.

Why the conjugate. A norm must be real and positive for every nonzero vector. Take \(\mathbf{x} = (1, i)\): without conjugation \(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x} = 1 + i^{2} = 0\), a nonzero vector of "length" zero. With it, \(\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{x}\) \(= \lvert 1\rvert^{2} + \lvert i\rvert^{2}\) \(= 2\). IQ samples are complex, so every energy, correlation, projection and covariance on IQ data uses \((\cdot)^{H}\). The conjugate also removes the common carrier phase: if both vectors pick up the same phase \(e^{i\varphi}, \mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}\) is unchanged.

Angle and orthogonality. Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\lvert \langle \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle \rvert \le \lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert \lVert \mathbf{y}\rVert\), with equality only for parallel vectors, so for real vectors \(\cos \theta\) \(= \mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{x} / (\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert \lVert \mathbf{y}\rVert)\) is a valid angle. For complex vectors the magnitude \(\lvert \mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}\rvert /(\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert \lVert \mathbf{y}\rVert) \in [0,1]\) plays the same role. Orthogonal means \(\langle \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle = 0\); an orthonormal set satisfies \(\mathbf{q}_{j}^{H}\mathbf{q}_{i} = \delta_{ij}\).

The bracket is a rank-one projector matrix. Pythagoras holds because \(\mathbf{p} \perp (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p})\). Every projector in this module is a sum of such pieces over an orthonormal basis.

Cosine similarity is the correlation coefficient. Subtract the means, \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x} - \bar{x}\mathbf{1}\), and the Pearson coefficient is exactly the cosine of the angle between the centered vectors: \(\rho\) \(= \tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}} / (\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\rVert \lVert \tilde{\mathbf{y}}\rVert)\). Module 07 builds covariance and spatial coherence on this; Module 11 uses normalized cross-correlation (a cosine between a kernel and a shifted window) for speckle tracking and delay estimation.

The coherence factor is a squared cosine. With \(\mathbf{s}\) the delayed aperture vector for one pixel, \(\mathrm{CF}\) \(= \lvert \mathbf{1}^{H}\mathbf{s}\rvert^{2} / (N\lVert \mathbf{s}\rVert^{2})\) \(= \cos^{2}\angle (\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{1})\), since \(\lVert \mathbf{1}\rVert^{2} = N\). Perfect focusing makes \(\mathbf{s}\) parallel to the all-ones vector; aberration rotates it away, which is why sound-speed estimation uses CF and its relatives as focus-quality metrics.

Pitfall

numpy.dot(x, y) on complex arrays does not conjugate; numpy.vdot(y, x) conjugates its first argument and returns \(\mathbf{y}^{H}\mathbf{x}\). In MATLAB x' is \((\cdot)^{H}\) and x.' is \((\cdot)^{T}\). Using the wrong one on IQ data gives energies that can be negative or complex, and correlations whose magnitude depends on the carrier phase.

Key takeaways
  • Complex inner products conjugate one argument, ⟨x, y⟩ = yᴴx; without it x = (1, i) has xᵀx = 0. The conjugate also cancels a carrier phase common to both vectors.
  • |yᴴx|/(‖x‖‖y‖) ∈ [0, 1] measures similarity; the Pearson coefficient is the cosine between the mean-removed vectors.
  • Projection onto a line: c = aᴴx/(aᴴa), residual ⟂ a, and ‖x‖² = ‖p‖² + ‖x − p‖².
  • CF = |1ᴴs|²/(N‖s‖²) = cos²∠(s, 1): aberration rotates s away from the all-ones vector.

05.3A matrix is a linear map

A map is linear if \(A(\alpha \mathbf{x} + \beta \mathbf{y})\) \(= \alpha A\mathbf{x} + \beta A\mathbf{y}\). Every linear map from \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) to \(\mathbb{R}^{m}\) is a matrix, and the matrix is fully determined by where it sends the basis vectors: column j of A is \(A\mathbf{e}_{j}\). Writing any input as \(\mathbf{x} = \sum x_{j}\mathbf{e}_{j}\) and applying linearity gives the two readings of a matrix–vector product:

\[ \text{column picture: } A\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\mathbf{a}_2 + \dots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n, \qquad \text{row picture: } (A\mathbf{x})_i = \mathbf{r}_i^{T}\mathbf{x} \]

Beamforming reads naturally both ways. Row picture: a pixel value is a dot product between that pixel's delay-and-apodization pattern and the channel data. Column picture of the forward model: the channel data are a sum over scatterers of amplitude × that scatterer's echo hyperbola. Section 05.8 makes both exact.

Composition is multiplication. \((AB)\mathbf{x} = A(B\mathbf{x})\): the matrix written last acts first. Shapes chain as (m×k)(k×n) = m×n, and in general \(AB \ne BA\). With shear \(S = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) and 90° rotation \(R = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\): \(SR\mathbf{e}_{1} = (1, 1)\) but \(RS\mathbf{e}_{1} = (0, 1)\).

Ultrasound link

The receive chain up to the envelope is a product of matrices: IQ demodulation D, decimation S, beamforming B, angle compounding C, so \(\mathbf{i} = C B S D \mathbf{r}\) for raw RF \(\mathbf{r}\). Envelope detection |·| and log compression are the first nonlinear steps. Everything before them obeys superposition, which is why SVD clutter filtering (Module 06) can act on channel data or on beamformed IQ, and why a PSF fully characterizes the linear part of the system.

Pitfall

In code, A @ B @ x applies B first. A chain written as "first weight channels by W, then beamform with B" is \(B W \mathbf{d}\), not \(W B \mathbf{d}\); the second is not even dimensionally valid when B changes the vector length.

Key takeaways
  • Column j of A is Aej; in (AB)x = A(Bx) the matrix written last acts first, and AB ≠ BA in general.
  • det is the signed area scale (det < 0 flips orientation, det = 0 means dependent columns); tr A = λ₁ + λ₂, det A = λ₁λ₂.
  • The unit circle maps to an ellipse with semi-axes σ₁ ≥ σ₂, so |det A| = σ₁σ₂.
  • The receive chain i = CBSDr is linear up to envelope detection and log compression, so a PSF characterizes it.

  • dim C(Aᵀ) + dim N(A) = n and dim C(A) + dim N(Aᵀ) = m, with N(A) ⟂ C(Aᵀ) and N(Aᵀ) ⟂ C(A).

  • Never test rank with det: a perfectly conditioned 128 × 128 matrix with all σ = 0.5 has det ≈ 3 × 10⁻³⁹. Threshold the singular values instead.
  • The part of a sound-speed map in N(A) is set by the regularizer or prior, not by the data.

MATLAB's fft and numpy's default fft are \(\sqrt{N}\) times the unitary DFT: \(\lVert \operatorname{fft} (\mathbf{x})\rVert^{2}\) \(= N\lVert \mathbf{x}\rVert^{2}\). Use fft(x)/sqrt(N) (or norm="ortho") when energy or inner products must be preserved across bases.

Key takeaways

  • Across 128 elements at 0.3 mm pitch and 5 MHz a plane wave sits in bin kθ = Np sin θ/λ, with Δsin θ = 0.0080 per bin and no wrap within ±30.9° (±58.8° at 3 MHz).
  • A thin phase screen D is diagonal in the element basis and circulant (FDFᴴ) in the plane-wave basis; Dᴴ corrects it only if it is the same for all imaging points.
  • fft is √N times the unitary DFT: use fft(x)/sqrt(N) when energy must be preserved across bases.

Ultrasound link

05.10How experts think about it

Experienced researchers see every linear step of the pipeline as one matrix and ask four questions of it: what are its columns (the responses it can produce), what is its structure (diagonal, Toeplitz, sparse, low-rank, unitary), how well conditioned is it, and is the operation in front of them the adjoint, the inverse or a regularized inverse. The rules below are the sentences that carry those answers, each with the number that backs it.

RMB assumes independent, identically distributed Gaussian snapshots that do not contain the desired signal. In ultrasound the signal is in every snapshot, which slows convergence (signal cancellation) and is one more reason for loading; adjacent depth samples and coherent echoes are correlated, so the effective K is smaller, and coherent sources also need spatial smoothing. Fixed loading ignores how many snapshots you have and how mismatched the steering vector is; adaptive and robust (uncertainty-set) MV variants choose ε from the data.

05.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1In MATLAB, what is the difference between ' and .', and which do you use on IQ data? · screen

' is the conjugate (Hermitian) transpose, .' the plain transpose; on real data they agree. For z = (1, j), z.'*z = 1 + \(j^{2} = 0\) while z'*z = 2: only \((\cdot)^{H}\) gives an energy. Every energy, correlation, covariance (\(R = X^{H}X/K\)), projection and adjoint on IQ data uses '. A pure layout change (samples × elements to elements × samples) must use .', otherwise every phase flips sign and Doppler or phase-aberration estimates change sign.

Follow-up: "numpy?" x.T does not conjugate, x.conj().T does, and np.dot does not conjugate while np.vdot conjugates its first argument.

I2Why is DAS not the least-squares inverse of the forward model? · onsite

With \(\mathbf{d} = H\mathbf{\gamma} + \mathbf{n}\), the least-squares estimate is \((H^{H}H)^{-1}H^{H}\mathbf{d}\). DAS applies only \(H^{H}\) (up to apodization and the pulse matched filter): it sums each pixel's data along that pixel's hyperbola, the adjoint, so the image is \(H^{H}H\mathbf{\gamma}\), reflectivity blurred by the PSF matrix. \(H^{H}H\) is far from the identity (finite aperture and bandwidth) and is badly conditioned, so the exact inverse would amplify noise along weak singular vectors; in practice one uses \((H^{H}H + \lambda I)^{-1}H^{H}\), solved iteratively with B and \(B^{H}\) (CG/LSQR), never by forming \(H^{H}H\).

Follow-up: "Then why is DAS so widely used?" It is the matched filter: for white noise and a single scatterer it maximizes SNR at that pixel, it is cheap (one sparse product), and it is robust to model error, which the inverse is not.

I3What does a large condition number mean for pulse-echo sound-speed tomography? · research

In \(A \Delta \mathbf{s} = \Delta \mathbf{t}, \kappa (A)\) is the worst-case amplification of relative delay error into relative slowness error, and \(\log_{10}\kappa\) is the number of digits lost. Limited-angle pulse-echo geometry makes axially adjacent pixels see nearly the same steep paths, so columns are nearly parallel and κ is huge (effectively a null space for axial variations). With delays known to 1% and \(\kappa = 10^{3}\), the unregularized map can be 10× wrong in its weak directions. The cure is regularization (Tikhonov, smoothness or TV priors) and more angular diversity (more transmit/receive angles), with bias as the price: the reconstruction blurs or biases exactly the patterns the geometry cannot see.

Follow-up: "How do you choose λ?" L-curve corner, discrepancy principle (residual ≈ expected delay noise), or GCV; and validate on a phantom with known layers, because the bias is structured.

I4K snapshots across N elements: what is the rank of the sample covariance, and what does that imply for minimum-variance beamforming? · onsite

\(\hat{R} = (1/K)X^{H}X\) with \(X \in \mathbb{C}^{K\times N}\), so \(\operatorname{rank} \hat{R} \le \min (K,N)\). For K < N it is singular and \(\hat{R}^{-1}\) in the MV weights \(w = \hat{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}/(\mathbf{a}^{H}\hat{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a})\) does not exist. Ultrasound MV therefore averages over subarrays of length L < N (and over a few depth samples) to get enough snapshots, and adds diagonal loading \(\hat{R} + \varepsilon I\), which is Tikhonov. The Reed–Mallett–Brennan result says about K = 2N independent snapshots keep the expected SINR loss near 3 dB when the snapshots do not contain the desired signal; in ultrasound they do, so convergence is slower and loading is needed even at K ≥ 2N.

Follow-up: "What does the eigen-spectrum tell you?" The number of large eigenvalues is the number of coherent sources; the rest is the noise floor, the basis of subspace methods (MUSIC, eigenspace MV) and of the clutter-rank discussion in Module 06.

I5How do you compute a pseudo-inverse stably? · onsite

Through the SVD: \(A^{+} = V\Sigma^{+}U^{H}\) with \(1/\sigma_{i}\) only for \(\sigma_{i}\) above a tolerance (MATLAB pinv uses about \(\max (m,n)\cdot \sigma_{\max}\cdot \mathrm{eps}\); pinv(A,tol) raises it, which is truncated-SVD regularization). Never form \((A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}\) for an ill-conditioned A: it squares κ. For full column rank, QR (A\b) gives the same least-squares solution more cheaply; for rank-deficient problems lsqminnorm or a complete orthogonal decomposition gives the minimum-norm solution. For large sparse operators, LSQR with early stopping acts as a regularizer.

Follow-up: "How do you pick the tolerance?" From the noise: drop \(\sigma_{i}\) whose \(1/\sigma_{i}\) amplification pushes the noise above the signal along \(v_{i}\), i.e. the discrete Picard condition (\(\lvert u_{i}^{H}b\rvert\) should decay faster than \(\sigma_{i}\)).

I6Size, nonzeros and memory of the DAS matrix for 128 elements, 2048 samples and a 256 × 256 image? Would you store it? · screen

B is 65 536 × 262 144. With linear interpolation each pixel row has 2 taps per element: 65 536 · 256 = 16.8 M nonzeros, density 0.1%. Sparse double storage ≈ 16 B per nonzero ≈ 270 MB; dense would be 137 GB. A product \(B\mathbf{d}\) is 16.8 M multiply-adds per frame (complex data), the same work as a DAS loop. Store it when it is reused many times (fixed geometry over thousands of frames on a GPU, iterative solvers needing B and \(B^{H}\)); compute on the fly when geometry or sound speed changes per frame.

Follow-up: "And with 8 plane-wave angles compounded?" Stack the 8 operators side by side: 8× the columns and nonzeros (134 M, ≈ 2 GB double), or apply them one at a time and sum.

I7Show that the DFT diagonalizes circular convolution. What goes wrong if you FFT-filter an RF line without zero padding? · screen

A circulant is \(C = \sum c_{k}S^{k}\) with S the cyclic shift. The DFT vectors are eigenvectors of S (a cyclic shift multiplies DFT bin k by the unit-modulus phase \(e^{\pm i2\pi k/N}\), sign set by the DFT convention), hence of every polynomial in S, with eigenvalues fft(c): \(C = F^{H} \operatorname{diag} (F\mathbf{c}) F/N, F\) the unnormalized DFT matrix. Linear convolution of N samples with L taps is circular only if both are zero-padded to \(N_{c} \ge N + L - 1\); with \(N_{c} = N\) the last L − 1 output samples wrap into the first L − 1 (error 0.363 for a 31-tap 5 MHz pulse in m05_convolution_matrix.m).

Follow-up: "When is direct convolution faster?" For short kernels: for a 2048-sample line the crossover is near L ≈ 100 taps, lower with real-input FFTs and overlap-add (that count already assumes a precomputed filter spectrum).

I8Adding a quadratic term to your attenuation fit with raw frequency columns makes the coefficients jump around. Why, and what is the fix? · onsite

The columns 1, \(f, f^{2}\) over 2–8 MHz are nearly parallel (a Vandermonde matrix on an off-center interval), so κ grows fast: cond([1 f]) = 16.2 but order 3, 5, 7 give \(6.4\times 10^{3}, 3.8\times 10^{6}, 2.6\times 10^{9}\). Center and scale, t = (f − 5)/3, and the same orders give 7.6, 39 and 215. If you also form \(A^{T}A\) you square these numbers. Fix: centered/scaled (or orthogonal-polynomial) columns, QR solve, and report the covariance \(\sigma^{2}(A^{T}A)^{-1}\) so the reader sees the coefficient uncertainty.

Follow-up: "Does centering change the fitted curve?" No, the column space is the same, so P and the fitted values are identical; only the coefficients and their conditioning change.

I9Write a phase-screen correction as a matrix. When does it fail? · research

For narrowband IQ with element delays \(\delta \tau_{e}\): \(\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{ab}} = (D \otimes I_{t})\mathbf{d}\), \(D = \operatorname{diag} (e^{-i\omega_{0}\delta \tau_{e}})\); correction is \(D^{H}\) before DAS, N complex multiplies per sample, exact because D is unitary. It fails when the aberrator is not a thin screen at the array: a distributed or deep aberrator gives a field-dependent correction (isoplanatic patches of a few mm), so no single diagonal D works across the image. Broadband data need a delay (phase linear in frequency), not one phase.

Follow-up: "How would you estimate D?" Cross-correlate neighbouring channels on a bright target or speckle (Module 11), or find the phases that maximize coherence or image sharpness; the distortion-matrix approach estimates it per isoplanatic patch.

I10Is \(P = I - \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}/N\) a good clutter filter for Doppler ensembles? What are its eigenvalues? · onsite

P is the orthogonal projector onto the complement of the constant vector: eigenvalues 1 (N − 1 times) and 0, \(\operatorname{rank} N - 1, P^{2} = P = P^{T}\). It removes exactly the slow-time mean, i.e. perfectly stationary tissue. Its frequency response is a narrow notch at 0 Hz of width about PRF/N, so moving tissue (breathing, cardiac motion) leaks through and slow flow near the notch is attenuated. Polynomial regression filters project out 1, \(t, t^{2}, \dots\); the SVD filter \(I - U_{k}U_{k}^{H}\) projects out the dominant spatiotemporal components, which handles moving tissue.

Follow-up: "Why does a projector, not a general high-pass, matter?" A projector has no transient for a finite ensemble and leaves the orthogonal complement untouched; IIR filters on short ensembles need initialization tricks.

05.14Self-check

Q1For x = (1, i) and y = (i, 1), compute \(x^{T}x, x^{H}x, y^{T}x\) and \(y^{H}x\). Are x and y orthogonal?

\(x^{T}x = 1 + i^{2} = 0\) and \(x^{H}x = 1 + 1 = 2\), so only the conjugated version is a norm. \(y^{T}x = i\cdot 1 + 1\cdot i = 2i\), but \(y^{H}x = (-i)(1) + (1)(i) = 0\). Under the correct complex inner product x and y are orthogonal; dropping the conjugate would wrongly report a correlation of magnitude 2.

Q2Project b = (3, 4) onto a = (1, 1). Give p, the residual, its norm, and the projector matrix.

\(c = a^{T}b/a^{T}a = 7/2\), so p = (3.5, 3.5) and r = (−0.5, 0.5), \(\lVert r\rVert = 0.707\). Check \(a^{T}r = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0\) and \(\lVert p\rVert^{2} + \lVert r\rVert^{2}\) \(= 24.5 + 0.5\) \(= 25\) \(= \lVert b\rVert^{2}\). \(P = aa^{T}/a^{T}a = \tfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\); \(P^{2} = P\), eigenvalues 1 (along a) and 0 (along (1, −1)), det P = 0.

Q3Compute κ for A = [1 1; 1 1.01]. If b is known to 0.1% (relative), how large can the relative error in x be?

A is symmetric positive definite, so \(\sigma_{i} = \lambda_{i}\). \(\operatorname{tr} = 2.01\), \(\det = 0.01\), \(\lambda\) \(= 1.005 \pm \sqrt{1.005^{2} - 0.01}\) \(= 1.005 \pm 1.0000125\), giving 2.00501 and 0.0049875. κ = 2.00501/0.0049875 ≈ 402. The bound gives up to 402 × 0.1% ≈ 40% relative error in x. Note det = 0.01 alone says nothing; 0.1·I also has det 0.01 and κ = 1.

Q4Two unit-norm columns are 10° apart. What are \(\sigma_{1}, \sigma_{2}\) and κ, and what is the noise std of \(\hat{x}\) along the weak direction for σ = 0.01 noise on b? What does Tikhonov with \(\lambda = \sigma_{2}^{2}\) do to it?

\(\sigma_{1,2} = \sqrt{1 \pm \cos 10^{\circ}} = \sqrt{1.98481}= 1.4088\) and \(\sqrt{0.015192}= 0.12326\); κ = cot 5° = 11.43. Along \(v_{2}\) the std is \(\sigma /\sigma_{2} = 0.01/0.12326 = 0.081\) (along \(v_{1}\) only 0.0071). With \(\lambda = \sigma_{2}^{2} = 0.015192\): \(f_{2}\) = ½, std \(\sigma \sigma_{2}/(\sigma_{2}^{2} + \lambda)\) = 0.01·0.12326/0.030384 = 0.041 (halved), and the \(v_{2}\) component of x is shrunk by 50% (bias). These follow from (05.14) and (05.16); Demo 05.6 at φ = 10° reproduces them.

Q5Fit \(y = c_{0} + c_{1}x\) to the points (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2) by the normal equations. Verify that the residual is orthogonal to both columns.

\(A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{bmatrix},\ A^{T}A = \begin{bmatrix}3 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{bmatrix},\ A^{T}y = (5, 6)\). det = 6, so \(c_{0} = (5\cdot 5 - 3\cdot 6)/6 = 7/6\) and \(c_{1} = (3\cdot 6 - 3\cdot 5)/6 = 1/2\). Predictions 7/6, 5/3, 13/6, residual r = (−1/6, 1/3, −1/6). Column 1: sum = 0. Column 2: 0 + 1/3 − 2/6 = 0. \(\lVert r\rVert^{2} = 1/6\).

Q6A DAS operator maps 2048 samples × 128 elements to a 256 × 256 image. Bound its rank and its null-space dimension, and describe a vector in the null space.

n = 262 144 inputs, m = 65 536 outputs, so r ≤ 65 536 and dim N(B) ≥ 262 144 − 65 536 = 196 608. At least 75% of the channel-data space is invisible to the beamformer: for example, energy placed at sample–element combinations that lie on no pixel's delay curve, or patterns whose weighted sums along every delay curve cancel. Noise components in N(B) never reach the image, which is one way to see the SNR gain of beamforming.

Q7A 5 MHz plane wave arrives at θ = 10° on a 128-element array with 0.3 mm pitch (c = 1540 m/s). Which DFT bin does it fall in, and what is the largest unaliased steering angle at 5 and 7.5 MHz?

λ = 0.308 mm, \(k_{\theta}\) = Np sin θ/λ = 128 · 0.3 · 0.17365/0.308 = 21.65, between bins 21 and 22, so its energy leaks over neighboring bins. Unaliased range |sin θ| < λ/(2p): arcsin(0.5133) = 30.9° at 5 MHz; at 7.5 MHz λ = 0.2053 mm, arcsin(0.342) = 20.0°. Demo 05.7 shows the peak at k ≈ 22 and the wrap past 30.9°.

Q8A 2×2 system has σ = (2, 0.02). Compare noise amplification along \(v_{2}\) without regularization and with λ = 0.01, and give the filter factors.

Unregularized: std along \(v_{2} = \sigma_{n}/0.02 = 50\sigma_{n}\). Whether that trade is good depends on how large \(v_{2}^{T}x\) is compared with \(50\sigma_{n}\).
