Estimation theory and the Cramér–Rao bound

Every number your pipeline produces is an estimate: a bubble position in ULM, an element-to-element delay, a Doppler frequency, a sound speed, an attenuation slope. This module builds the language for how precise those numbers can be (likelihood, Fisher information, the Cramér–Rao bound) and for the three ways the bound misleads: thresholds, nuisance parameters and biased estimators.

You will be able to

  • Split any error into bias and variance, and say which estimators are efficient for a given noise model
  • Write the Fisher information for a signal in real or complex Gaussian noise, and get the factor 2 right
  • Derive \(\sigma_x \ge w/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}}\) for ULM localization, convert it to µm and λ, and judge a localizer against it
  • Recognize the threshold region, and quantify what unknown nuisance parameters cost (neighbour bubbles, \(t_0\) in sound-speed estimation)
  • Explain when a biased or Bayesian estimator legitimately beats the CRLB in MSE

In 60 seconds
  1. Error splits into bias and variance: Total error has a systematic part, bias, and a random part, variance, and only the variance averages down. Mean squared error is \(b^2 + \mathrm{var}\), with b the bias, so 2 µm of bias and 3 µm of jitter give 3.6 µm RMS. Always report the bias beside an RMS error. §12.1
  2. Gaussian maximum likelihood is least squares: In Gaussian noise the most likely parameter value is the least-squares fit, weighted for coloured noise. With an unknown complex amplitude removed, the criterion becomes the normalized correlation of the data with a model signal: the periodogram for a tone, a scanned matched filter for a bubble, the correlation peak for a delay. §12.2
  3. Fisher information measures sensitivity against noise: Fisher information I measures how strongly the signal changes with the parameter relative to noise, and it adds over independent samples. No unbiased estimator has variance below 1/I, the Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB). Demodulating RF to IQ neither creates nor destroys information; apparent factors of 2 are noise-power conventions. §12.3
  4. Frequency precision grows fast with record length: A tone’s frequency is pinned ever more tightly as the record lengthens: the bound on its standard deviation falls as \(N^{-3/2}\), with N the samples. The periodogram peak reaches the bound only above a threshold in integrated SNR, N times per-sample SNR: 13 dB for N = 32. Below it, errors explode. §12.4
  5. Localization is set by width and energy, not pixels: A microbubble can be located far more finely than the beam is wide: \(\sigma_x \ge \mathrm{FWHM}/(2.355\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}})\), with FWHM the full width at half maximum of the point-spread function and ENR the energy-to-noise ratio. λ/10 with FWHM = λ needs 12.6 dB. Smaller pixels only fake improvement by inflating ENR. §12.5
  6. Every unknown costs: Parameters you must estimate but do not care about (nuisance parameters) inflate the error whenever they mimic the signature of the one you want, so the bound that assumes them known is optimistic. An unknown neighbouring bubble at half the beam width inflates localization error 3.96-fold. More views, calibration or a prior buy precision back. §12.6
  7. Beating the bound requires bias: A biased estimator can beat the bound locally: shrinking the mean by a factor 0.8 toward a target reaches 0.64 of the bound there, breaks even three standard deviations away and loses beyond. Regularized and learned estimators behave the same way, so they must report bias over the whole range of interest. §12.7
From the field
  • Compute the Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB) before building the estimator; an unbiased estimator that beats it is a bug. A result below the bound usually means a factor-2 real-versus-complex error, leaked truth or hidden bias. Far above it at low SNR, the estimator has crossed its threshold: 13 dB integrated SNR for a 32-sample periodogram. §12.3 §12.4
  • Compare regularized or learned estimators with the bound only through their bias across the whole range. The CRLB holds for unbiased estimators, so beating it at one point proves nothing. Shrinkage by 0.8 reaches 0.64 of the bound at its target but breaks even three standard deviations away and loses beyond. §12.7
  • Put every parameter you do not actually know into the bound as a nuisance parameter. The bound that assumes everything else known is optimistic when another parameter mimics yours. Single-target sound speed at 30 mm degrades from 0.0034 to 2.8 m/s once depth and time origin are unknown. §12.6
  • Use the bound to size SNR, bandwidth and ensemble length before committing hardware. It shows which spec limits precision. Microbubble localization at λ/10 with a beam width (FWHM) of λ needs only 12.6 dB energy-to-noise ratio; Doppler velocity reaches 1.49 mm/s with 16 pulses at 10 dB, a 5 kHz pulse rate and 5 MHz. §12.4 §12.5

12.1Estimators, bias, variance and MSE

An estimator \(\hat\theta = g(\mathbf{y})\) is a function of noisy data, so it is a random variable. Three numbers describe it: its bias \(b(\theta) = \mathrm{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta\), its variance \(\operatorname{var}(\hat\theta) = \mathrm{E}[(\hat\theta - \mathrm{E}\hat\theta)^2]\), and their combination, the mean squared error. Adding and subtracting \(\mathrm{E}\hat\theta\) inside the square, the cross term has zero mean:

\[ \mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathrm{E}\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big] = b^2(\theta) + \operatorname{var}(\hat\theta) \]
(12.1)

Bias and variance trade against each other. An RMSE of 3.6 µm can be 2 µm of bias plus 3 µm of jitter: \(\sqrt{2^2 + 3^2} = 3.6\) µm. Report both parts, because only the variance averages down with more data.

An estimator is consistent if \(\hat\theta \to \theta\) as \(N \to \infty\), unbiased if \(b \equiv 0\) for every \(\theta\), and efficient if it is unbiased and its variance equals the Cramér–Rao lower bound (Section 12.3). The bound is a property of the data model, not of any estimator: change the noise law and the best estimator changes.

The simplest case shows this. Estimate a constant \(A\) from \(N\) samples \(y_n = A + w_n\), noise variance \(\sigma^2\):

Key takeaways
  • MSE = b² + var: an RMSE of 3.6 µm can be 2 µm of bias plus 3 µm of jitter; report both, because only the variance averages down.
  • The bound belongs to the noise model: in Gaussian noise the mean is efficient and the median 64 % efficient; in Laplace noise the median is asymptotically efficient and the mean 50 %.
  • The CRLB needs a support that does not move with θ; uniform noise breaks it and the midrange variance falls as 1/N².
  • Speckle intensity: the sample mean is the efficient MLE (var μ²/N); averaging dB is biased by −2.51 dB and 61 % efficient.

12.2Likelihood, the MLE and least squares

The likelihood is the data density read as a function of the parameter: \(L(\theta) = p(\mathbf{y}; \theta)\). The maximum-likelihood estimator is \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta)\). Under regularity conditions it has four properties that make it the default:

  • Invariance: the MLE of \(\alpha = g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}})\), for any \(g\).
  • Consistency, and asymptotic normality: \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{ML}} \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, I^{-1}(\theta))\) as \(N \to \infty\) or as SNR → ∞.
  • Asymptotic efficiency: it reaches the CRLB in that limit.
  • If an efficient estimator exists at finite N, the MLE is it.

None of these promise unbiasedness at finite N or low SNR. Invariance is the classic trap: if \(\hat a\) is the MLE of a complex amplitude, \(\lvert\hat a\rvert^2\) is the MLE of the power, and it is biased upward by the noise term, \(\mathrm{E}\lvert\hat a\rvert^2 = \lvert a\rvert^2 + \sigma^2/\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2\).

Least squares is the MLE in Gaussian noise

For real white Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), and for circular complex Gaussian noise with \(\mathrm{E}\lvert w\rvert^2 = \sigma^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{real:} \\ &\ln p = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{s}(\theta) \rVert^2 + \text{const} \\[4pt] &\text{complex:} \\ &\ln p = -\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\,\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{s}(\theta) \rVert^2 - N \ln(\pi\sigma^2) \end{aligned} \]

Maximizing either is minimizing the squared residual: (nonlinear) least squares. For the linear model \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{H}\theta + \mathbf{w}\) the MLE is the least-squares solution \(\hat\theta = (\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{y}\) (Module 05), with covariance \(\sigma^2(\mathbf{H}^{T}\mathbf{H})^{-1}\). That covariance equals the CRLB exactly, at any N: in the linear Gaussian model least squares is efficient, not just asymptotically.

12.3Fisher information and the Cramér–Rao bound

The score \(\partial \ln p(\mathbf{y}; \theta)/\partial\theta\) has zero mean when the support of \(p\) does not depend on \(\theta\). Its variance is the Fisher information, which also equals the expected curvature of the log-likelihood:

\[ \begin{aligned} I(\theta) &= \mathrm{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \ln p}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2}\right] = -\mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ln p}{\partial\theta^2}\right] \\ \operatorname{var}(\hat\theta) &\ge \frac{1}{I(\theta)} \quad \text{(unbiased)} \\ \operatorname{var}(\hat\theta) &\ge \frac{\big(1 + b'(\theta)\big)^2}{I(\theta)} \quad \text{(bias } b\text{)} \end{aligned} \]
(12.4)

Information is the energy of the signal's derivative, measured against the noise: a parameter is well determined when changing it changes the signal a lot compared with the noise. The bound depends on the noise only through its covariance, and the parameters only through \(\mathbf{J}\).

Where the factor 2 comes from. A complex sample is two real samples. With total noise power \(\sigma^2 = \mathrm{E}\lvert w\rvert^2\), each quadrature has variance \(\sigma^2/2\), so \(I = \sum(\partial\,\mathrm{Re}\,s)^2/(\sigma^2/2)\) \(+ \sum(\partial\,\mathrm{Im}\,s)^2/(\sigma^2/2)\) \(= (2/\sigma^2)\sum\lvert\partial s\rvert^2\). It has three consequences, all checked in m12_crlb_amplitude_mle.m:

  • Real amplitude of a known complex pulse (phase known), complex noise: \(\operatorname{var}(\hat a) \ge \sigma^2/(2\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2)\).
  • Complex amplitude (phase unknown): \(\mathrm{E}\lvert\hat a - a\rvert^2 \ge \sigma^2/\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2\), i.e. \(\sigma^2/(2\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2)\) per real component.
  • Real RF pulse in real noise of variance \(\sigma^2\): \(\operatorname{var}(\hat a) \ge \sigma^2/\lVert\mathbf{s}\rVert^2\).

Demodulation to IQ is an invertible map of the band-limited data, so it neither creates nor destroys information. Factor-2 errors arise when one side of a comparison counts \(\sigma^2\) per quadrature and the other counts it per complex sample. Write down which convention every SNR uses.

Pitfall

The CRLB bounds unbiased estimators only, and only locally. An estimator that clips its output to a search range, or a localizer that returns the pixel centre when the fit fails, is biased; its variance can sit below \(1/I\) and that proves nothing. Always plot bias and RMSE separately against the true parameter.

Key takeaways
  • Fisher information is the variance of the score and the expected curvature of the log-likelihood; for a signal in Gaussian noise it is the energy of ∂s/∂θ against the noise, and it adds over independent samples.
  • With E|w|² = σ², I = (2/σ²)Σ|∂s|²: a phase-known amplitude has var ≥ σ²/(2‖s‖²) in IQ against σ²/‖s‖² for real RF in real noise; demodulation neither creates nor destroys information.
  • The bound covers unbiased estimators only, and only locally: a clipped or fallback estimator can sit below 1/I and prove nothing, so plot bias and RMSE separately.

12.4Frequency estimation, the threshold and global bounds

Slow-time Doppler is a frequency-estimation problem. Model one complex tone in circular complex Gaussian noise, \(y[n] = A\,e^{j(\omega n + \varphi)} + w[n]\), \(n = 0,\dots,N-1\), \(\mathrm{SNR} = A^2/\sigma^2\). With \(\omega\) and \(\varphi\) unknown, the Fisher matrix and its inverse (Schur complement on \(\varphi\)) are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{I} &= \frac{2A^2}{\sigma^2}\begin{bmatrix} \sum n^2 & \sum n \\ \sum n & N \end{bmatrix} \\ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{var}(\hat\omega) &\ge \frac{\sigma^2}{2A^2}\,\frac{N}{N\sum n^2 - \left(\sum n\right)^2} \\ &= \frac{6}{\mathrm{SNR}\cdot N(N^2 - 1)} \\ &\phantom{=}\ \ (\text{rad/sample})^2 \end{aligned} \]

In each case "SNR" is signal power over noise power. At equal SNR the complex tone has half the variance: the factor 2 again. Kay's form is approximate (it neglects the interaction with the negative-frequency image) and holds for \(f\) not near 0 or ½.

Converting to Doppler: \(f_D = \omega\cdot\mathrm{PRF}/(2\pi)\) and \(v = c f_D/(2f_0)\), so \(\sigma_v = c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}\cdot\sigma_\omega/(4\pi f_0)\). With M = 16 pulses at 10 dB per pulse, \(\sigma_\omega \ge 0.0121\) rad/sample; at PRF = 5 kHz and \(f_0\) = 5 MHz that is 9.65 Hz, or 1.49 mm/s.

For the complex tone the MLE is exactly the periodogram peak (the concentrated likelihood of Section 12.2). The lag-one autocorrelation (Kasai, Module 11) is not efficient: at high SNR its phase estimate averages \(N - 1\) phase differences, which telescope to \((\varphi_{N-1} - \varphi_0)/(N - 1)\), so \(\operatorname{var} \to 1/(\mathrm{SNR}\,(N - 1)^2)\), a factor \(N(N + 1)/(6(N - 1))\) above the bound (5.7 at N = 32). It wins on cost and on robustness when the Doppler spectrum is broad.

The threshold effect and global bounds

The CRLB is local: it describes small errors around the true peak. When noise lifts a periodogram bin elsewhere above the signal bin, the estimate jumps to a random frequency, and the MSE rises toward the variance of a uniform guess, \(\pi^2/3\). The MSE curve has three regions: the asymptotic region on the bound, a threshold region where outliers start, and a no-information region. For the periodogram the outlier probability depends on the integrated SNR \(N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}\) (Rife and Boorstyn, 1974): in m12_frequency_crlb.m (1000 trials), N = 32 sits on the bound for \(N\cdot\mathrm{SNR} \ge 13\) dB and is about 180 times above it at 11 dB, where 2 % of trials are outliers. The 160-trial demo below finds the same knee, but at 11 dB it sees only a handful of outliers, so its ratio there swings by an order of magnitude from seed to seed (14× with the default seed). Global bounds that include these ambiguity errors (Ziv–Zakai, Barankin, Weiss–Weinstein) reproduce the knee; the CRLB never does. Module 11 showed the same mechanism for delays, where the false peaks sit one RF period apart.

Precision versus resolution. Precision is not resolution. A λ/10 figure in a ULM paper is the standard deviation of one isolated bubble's position. Two bubbles inside one PSF at the same time are a different estimation problem with more unknowns (Section 12.6), and their positions degrade sharply below about one FWHM of separation. Pixel size. The pixel does not set the bound: \(\sigma_x\) depends on the PSF width and on the noise density. Pixels finer than the Nyquist spacing of the noise band add samples whose noise is correlated, not new information. What the pixel size changes is what a given estimator can extract. A simulation that adds independent noise to every pixel at a fixed per-pixel SNR makes ENR grow as \(1/p^2\), so the "bound" improves with finer pixels: an artefact of the noise model.

How the common localizers compare

  • Maximum likelihood: maximize \(\lvert\langle y,\, g(\cdot - x_0, \cdot - z_0)\rangle\rvert^2\) over \((x_0, z_0)\), i.e. a Gaussian fit with complex amplitude (12.3). Efficient above threshold at any pixel size that samples the PSF.
  • Intensity-weighted centroid of \(\lvert y\rvert^2\) in a box of ±1 FWHM: at high ENR its error is \(\sum x\cdot 2\,\mathrm{Re}(a^* g\, n)\big/\sum \lvert a\rvert^2 g^2\), with variance \(2N_0\int x^2 g^2 \big/ \big(\lvert a\rvert^2 (\int g^2)^2\big)\) \(= w^2/\mathrm{ENR}\). For a Gaussian PSF it is nearly efficient, because \(x\cdot g\) is exactly the derivative template. Background and box truncation bias it.
  • Amplitude-weighted centroid of \(\lvert y\rvert\): weights every pixel in the box with equal noise, so the error variance grows with box area (1.2–3 times the bound in the MATLAB lab).
  • 3-point parabola or Gaussian through the peak pixel and its neighbours: cheap, but they use only three samples. As pixels shrink they see less of the PSF and lose information (about 2.2 times the bound at λ/4 and λ/8); the parabola on |y| also has a deterministic bias at coarse pixels (Module 11 showed the same for delays).

Axial phase. The carrier phase is not free information. After demodulation the axial IQ PSF is the envelope times a phase \(e^{-j2kz_0}\) that also depends on \(z_0\). If that phase were known, \(\lvert\partial s/\partial z_0\rvert^2 = g'^2 + 4k^2 g^2\) (\(k = 2\pi/\lambda\)) and the axial information would be \((1 + 8k^2 w_z^2)\) times larger: 33 times (5.7 times in σ) for an axial FWHM of 0.75λ. Bubble phase depends on bubble size, shell and the exact scatterer position, so localizers treat it as unknown, and with \(a\) unknown the carrier term cancels exactly: only the envelope counts (verified in m12_nuisance_fisher.m, Part 3).

Three ultrasound cases show the cost, all computed in m12_nuisance_fisher.m:

  • Unknown amplitude, width, background for a bubble centred in a window of ±3 FWHM: free (inflation 1.000), because \(g'\) is odd and \(g\), \(\partial g/\partial w\) and a constant are even. Off centre the symmetry breaks: with the bubble on the edge of a ±1.5 FWHM window (1.5 FWHM from its centre), inflation is 1.55 with amplitude unknown and 4.1 with width unknown as well (1.004 and 1.07 at 1 FWHM from the centre).
  • A neighbour bubble at separation \(d\) with unknown position and amplitude: inflation 3.96 at d = 0.5 FWHM, 1.38 at 1 FWHM, 1.04 at 1.5 FWHM for in-phase echoes (1.81, 1.11, 1.01 in quadrature). It does not depend on the neighbour's strength at all, only on its phase; for small \(d\) it grows as \(d^{-2}\) in phase and \(d^{-1}\) in quadrature.
  • Sound speed from delays: receive delays of one point target, \(\tau_e = (z + r_e)/c + t_0\), \(r_e = \sqrt{z^2 + (x_e - x_s)^2}\), 128 elements at 0.3 mm, 1 ns independent jitter (Module 11 sets that number). The intercept \(t_0\) absorbs the mean delay; depth and speed then trade along the moveout curvature, and only the small departure from a parabola separates them.

The same algebra says how to buy the information back: add data whose derivatives point in different directions (targets at several depths, several transmit angles, a calibrated lens delay \(t_0\)), or add prior information (Section 12.7).

12.7Biased estimators, shrinkage and the Bayesian view

The CRLB constrains unbiased estimators. Give up unbiasedness and the MSE can drop below \(1/I\). Shrink an unbiased estimate \(\bar y \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, s^2)\), \(s^2\) = CRLB, toward zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\theta = c\,\bar y:\quad b &= -(1 - c)\,\theta, \quad \operatorname{var} = c^2 s^2 \\ \mathrm{MSE} &= c^2 s^2 + (1 - c)^2\theta^2 \\ \mathrm{MSE} < s^2 &\iff \lvert\theta\rvert < s\sqrt{\frac{1 + c}{1 - c}} \end{aligned} \]

For \(p \ge 3\) independent means the result is stronger. The James–Stein estimator \(\hat\theta = \big(1 - (p - 2)/\lVert\mathbf{y}\rVert^2\big)\mathbf{y}\) has total MSE below \(p\) (the MLE's) for every \(\theta\), with risk exactly 2 at \(\theta = 0\) whatever \(p\) is. Pooling information across parameters is why regularized maps of many pixels can beat pixel-by-pixel estimates everywhere on average.

Bayesian estimation and MAP

Treat \(\theta\) as random with a prior \(p(\theta)\). The posterior mean minimizes the MSE averaged over the prior (MMSE), and the posterior mode is the MAP estimate. For a Gaussian prior \(\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) and data \(\bar y \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, s^2)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{E}[\theta \mid \bar y] &= c\,\bar y, \quad c = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + s^2} \\ \mathrm{E}\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big] &= \frac{1}{1/s^2 + 1/\tau^2} \\ &= \text{Bayesian CRB} \end{aligned} \]

12.9How experts think about it

An expert reads every precision claim as three questions. What is the data model (signal shape, noise law and covariance, real or complex, which parameters are unknown)? What does the Fisher information say for that model, roughly "energy of the signal's derivative over noise density"? And is the estimator in the regime where the local bound means anything (above threshold, unbiased, isolated targets)? Then they compare the claimed number with w/√ENR or its analogue and ask which assumption buys the difference.

Quote RMSE, not just a CRLB: the bound covers only the variance of unbiased estimators, and averaging more data never touches the bias.

Write \(\hat\theta - \theta = (\hat\theta - \mathrm{E}\hat\theta) + b\); the cross term has zero mean (12.1). An estimator with 2 µm bias and 3 µm std has RMSE \(\sqrt{13} = 3.6\) µm. 12.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What is the Cramér–Rao bound, and what does it assume? · screen

A lower bound on the variance of any unbiased estimator: \(\operatorname{var}(\hat\theta) \ge 1/I(\theta)\), \(I = \mathrm{E}[(\partial\ln p/\partial\theta)^2]\). It assumes the support of \(p(y;\theta)\) does not depend on \(\theta\) and the log-likelihood is differentiable; it is local (curvature at the true value) and says nothing about bias. It is reached exactly in the linear Gaussian model and asymptotically by the MLE. Follow-up: "And for biased estimators?" \((1 + b')^2/I\), and the MSE can be lower than \(1/I\).

I2A paper claims λ/20 resolution with ULM. What do you ask? · screen

Whether λ/20 is localization precision (std of an isolated bubble's position) or the separability of two bubbles. Then the ENR or SNR definition and noise model (\(\sigma_x = \mathrm{FWHM}/(2.355\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}})\): λ/20 with FWHM ≈ λ needs only ENR ≈ 18.6 dB), whether outliers were excluded, how motion and tracking errors were handled, and how resolution was measured on the final map (for example a Fourier-ring-correlation split of the localizations, or the smallest separated vessel pair). Follow-up: "Why is precision so cheap?" Because the bound scales as \(1/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}}\) and bubbles are bright.

I3Derive the localization CRLB for a Gaussian PSF in complex white noise. · onsite

\(s = a\,g(x - x_0)\,g(z - z_0)\), noise density \(N_0\). \(I_{x_0x_0} = (2/N_0)\lvert a\rvert^2\iint\lvert\partial g/\partial x\rvert^2\) \(= (2\lvert a\rvert^2/N_0)\cdot\pi w_x w_z/(2w_x^2)\) \(= \mathrm{ENR}/w_x^2\). Cross terms with \(\mathrm{Re}\,a\), \(\mathrm{Im}\,a\) and \(z_0\) vanish (odd × even), so \(\sigma_x \ge w_x/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}}\). Example: FWHM 1λ at 5 MHz, ENR 30 dB: 308/(2.355·31.6) = 4.1 µm. Follow-up: "Real RF instead?" \(\sqrt{2}\,w/\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}_r}\) with \(\mathrm{ENR}_r = \sum s^2/\sigma^2\), and the axial bound changes if the carrier phase is known.

I4Where does the factor 2 between real and complex noise come from? · onsite

With \(\mathrm{E}\lvert w\rvert^2 = \sigma^2\), each quadrature has \(\sigma^2/2\); summing the real-data information of both quadratures gives \(I = (2/\sigma^2)\,\mathrm{Re}\sum\lvert\partial s\rvert^2\). Phase-known amplitude from IQ: \(\sigma^2/(2\lVert s\rVert^2)\); real pulse in real noise \(\sigma^2\): \(\sigma^2/\lVert s\rVert^2\). Same for frequency: complex tone \(6/(\mathrm{SNR}\cdot N(N^2-1))\) vs Kay's real \(12/(\eta N(N^2-1))\). Follow-up: "Does demodulating RF to IQ change the bound?" No; it is an invertible map of band-limited data. Only the bookkeeping of \(\sigma^2\) changes.

I5Why can an intensity-weighted centroid be nearly efficient while an amplitude-weighted centroid is not? · onsite

Linearize at high ENR: \(\lvert y\rvert^2 \approx \lvert a\rvert^2 g^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}(a^* g\, n)\), so the intensity centroid's error is a noise projection onto \(x\cdot g\), which is proportional to \(g'\), the optimal (matched) template: variance \(w^2/\mathrm{ENR}\), the bound. With \(\lvert y\rvert\) weights the error is a projection onto \(x\cdot 1\) over the box: equal-weight noise from every pixel, so variance grows with box area. Follow-up: "What breaks the intensity centroid?" Background (pulls toward the box centre), truncation, neighbours, and the \(\lvert n\rvert^2\) term at low ENR.

I6How does Doppler frequency precision scale with ensemble length, and where is the threshold? · onsite

\(\operatorname{var}(\hat\omega) \ge 6/(\mathrm{SNR}\cdot N(N^2 - 1))\), so \(\sigma \propto N^{-3/2}\); N = 16 → 32 cuts σ by 2.83. The periodogram MLE follows it down to an integrated \(N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}\) of about 13 dB (N = 32); 2 dB lower it is two orders of magnitude worse because noise bins win. Lag-one is 5.7× in variance above the bound at N = 32 even at high SNR. Follow-up: "Convert to velocity": \(\sigma_v = c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}\cdot\sigma_\omega/(4\pi f_0)\): 1.49 mm/s for N = 16, 10 dB, PRF 5 kHz, 5 MHz.

I7How would you bound sound-speed estimation from delay data, and what dominates the bound? · research

Write the delay model \(\tau_e(c, z, x_s, t_0)\), its Jacobian, \(\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{C}_\tau^{-1}\mathbf{J}\) with the delay covariance from the Walker–Trahey/Carter bound (Module 11), and take \([\mathbf{I}^{-1}]_{cc}\). The nuisances dominate: for one target at 30 mm, 128 elements, 1 ns jitter, \(\sigma_c\) goes from 0.065 m/s (\(z\) unknown) to 2.8 m/s (\(z\) and \(t_0\) unknown), because \(t_0\), \(z\) and \(c\) all produce nearly the same bowl-shaped moveout. Break it with several depths and angles, a calibrated lens delay, or a prior. Follow-up: "Correlated delay errors?" Use the full \(\mathbf{C}_\tau\); aberration-like errors are low-order and project onto the same bowl, so they hurt more than white jitter.

I8A learned localizer beats the CRLB on your test set. Is that suspicious? · research

Not necessarily: the CRLB bounds unbiased estimators. A network trained on a distribution of PSFs and positions acts as a Bayesian estimator with that prior, and the relevant bound is the Bayesian CRB \(1/(\mathrm{E}[I] + I_{\mathrm{prior}})\). Check the bias map against true sub-pixel position (pull toward pixel centres or training positions), test out of distribution (other PSF widths, depths, neighbours), and compare the MSE against the Bayesian bound for the same prior. Follow-up: "Can any estimator beat the Bayesian bound?" Not in Bayes MSE under regularity conditions.

I9Why is the MLE of echo power from IQ biased, and how do you correct it? · onsite

Invariance makes \(\lvert\hat a\rvert^2\) the MLE of \(\lvert a\rvert^2\), but \(\mathrm{E}\lvert\hat a\rvert^2 = \lvert a\rvert^2 + \sigma^2/\lVert s\rVert^2\): the estimator variance adds. At ENR = 10 dB the power is 10 % (0.41 dB) high. Subtract an estimate of \(\sigma^2/\lVert s\rVert^2\) from a noise-only region or noise frames. Follow-up: "Where else?" Power Doppler, coherence factor and correlation coefficients at low SNR, all biased by the noise term.

I10Two bubbles are 0.5 FWHM apart. What happens to the precision, and what does it mean for high-concentration ULM? · research

With both positions and amplitudes unknown, the Fisher matrix couples them: std inflation 3.96 for in-phase echoes (1.81 in quadrature), rising as \(d^{-2}\) (\(d^{-1}\)) as they approach. Before that, a single-bubble localizer returns a biased "mid-point". High concentration therefore needs model-order selection (one bubble or two), sparse recovery (Module 13), or temporal separation (separable bubble dynamics). Follow-up: "Does amplitude ratio matter?" Not in the Fisher inflation (only the relative phase), but a weak neighbour is harder to detect, which fails first.

I11To estimate mean backscattered power from speckle, do you average intensity or dB values? · screen

Intensity. The sample mean of exponential intensities is the efficient MLE. Averaging dB values gives a biased result (−2.51 dB, Euler's \(\gamma\) in \(10\log_{10}\)) with variance \(\pi^2/6 = 1.64\) times the bound for \(\ln\mu\): 61 % efficiency, i.e. the same precision needs 64 % more independent samples. Follow-up: "How many independent samples in a gate?" Count speckle cells, not pixels (Module 07).

Q1A tracker has 2 µm bias and 3 µm standard deviation. What is its RMSE, and what happens to it with 4× more independent frames averaged?

\(\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2} = 3.61\) µm. Averaging 4 frames halves the std to 1.5 µm but leaves the bias: \(\sqrt{4 + 2.25} = 2.5\) µm. The bias becomes the floor.

Q2A bubble PSF has a lateral FWHM of 200 µm and ENR = 25 dB. What is the lateral CRLB?

\(w = 200/2.355 = 84.9\) µm; \(\sqrt{\mathrm{ENR}} = \sqrt{316.2} = 17.8\); \(\sigma_x \ge 4.8\) µm (FWHM/41.9).

Q3You resample the same beamformed IQ image from λ/4 to λ/16 pixels. Does the localization CRLB change? Does your localizer's error?

The bound does not change: noise density and PSF are the same, and the extra samples carry correlated noise. A matched-filter MLE or intensity centroid stays near the bound; a 3-point fit gets worse (it now sees a smaller part of the PSF). Only a simulation that adds independent noise per pixel would report an improvement.

Q4Slow-time ensemble of M = 16 at 10 dB per sample, PRF 5 kHz, \(f_0\) = 5 MHz. Lower bound on the velocity std?

\(\sigma_\omega = \sqrt{6/(10\cdot 16\cdot 255)} = 0.0121\) rad/sample → \(\sigma_f = 0.0121\cdot 5000/(2\pi) = 9.65\) Hz → \(\sigma_v = 1540\cdot 9.65/(2\cdot 5\times10^6) = 1.49\) mm/s. Doubling M to 32 divides it by 2.83.

Q5Why does an unknown complex amplitude cost nothing for the lateral position of a centred, symmetric PSF?

The Fisher cross term is \(\propto \mathrm{Re}\sum(a\,g')^*\,g\): \(g'\) is odd, \(g\) is even, so the sum is zero. The position derivative is orthogonal to the amplitude derivatives, and \([\mathbf{I}^{-1}]_{11} = 1/I_{11}\). Off-centre in a finite window, or with an asymmetric PSF, the symmetry breaks and the cost appears.

Q6A frequency estimator's RMSE is on the CRLB at 0 dB per sample but 100× above it at −4 dB, with N = 32. What is happening, and which number predicts it?

The threshold effect: noise peaks in other bins beat the signal bin and the estimate jumps (outliers), which the local CRLB cannot describe. The relevant number is the integrated SNR \(N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}\) (15 dB vs 11 dB here); global bounds such as Ziv–Zakai capture it.

Q7Shrinkage with c = 0.5: below the CRLB for which true values, and which prior makes it optimal?

\(\mathrm{MSE} = 0.25s^2 + 0.25\theta^2 < s^2\) for \(\lvert\theta\rvert < \sqrt{3}\,s = 1.73s\). It is the posterior mean for a prior std \(\tau = s\sqrt{c/(1 - c)} = s\), with Bayes MSE \(c\,s^2 = 0.5s^2\).
