Sound-speed estimation (capstone)

Every beamformer assumes a sound speed, and every quantitative ultrasound number inherits the error of that assumption. Estimating the speed from the echoes is a delay-estimation problem (Module 11) sitting inside an inverse problem (Module 13), solved with the beamformer itself as the forward model (Modules 07, 14, 25). This capstone builds the estimators from the global focus-optimal speed, through the average-to-local inversion of layered media and echo-mode tomography (CUTE), to differentiable beamforming, and closes with the error budget that decides whether a liver measurement can separate healthy from steatotic tissue.

You will be able to

  • Compute the focusing tolerance on sound speed for a given F-number, depth and frequency, and say what a 1 % error does to phase, depth and coherence
  • Explain why a focus-quality maximizer returns an average speed (the RMS speed of seismology, not the harmonic mean), and invert averages to local speeds with Dix's formula, straight-ray differencing or regularized least squares, with their noise amplification
  • Derive the CUTE measurement (echo phase shifts between steered acquisitions), its wrapping limit, the phase jitter of correlated speckle, and the difference between transmit-only and common-mid-angle data
  • Set up limited-angle sound-speed tomography as \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{A}\,\Delta\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{n}\), read its resolution matrix, and separate noise, regularization and forward-model errors
  • Recognize differentiable-beamforming autofocus as nonlinear tomography, predict where its losses have local minima, and place deep-learning and full-wave methods on the same map
  • Write an error budget (CRLB, calibration, layer bias, model error) for a clinical liver sound-speed measurement and compare it with the 10–40 m/s effects that matter

32.1Why sound speed matters: focus, geometry and a biomarker

Soft tissues span roughly 1450 m/s (fat) to 1600 m/s (muscle, healthy liver), a −6 % to +4 % band around the 1540 m/s that every scanner assumes. A wrong speed does three separate things. It defocuses: the receive delays have the wrong curvature, so the aperture sums out of phase. It misplaces: depth is computed as \(\hat c\,t/2\). And it hides a biomarker: fat lowers the sound speed of liver, so the speed itself is a quantitative measure of steatosis, and every downstream QUS estimate (attenuation, backscatter, Module 27) is computed from images formed with the assumed value.

Start with the defocus. Place the pixel by its two-way time, as every scanner does, so the on-axis delay is always right. A receive element at lateral offset \(x\) then sees a residual delay set by the error in the wavefront curvature. With \(\hat c = c + \Delta c\), depth \(\hat z = \hat c t_a\) (\(t_a\) the one-way apex time) and \(x \ll z\):

\[ \delta\tau(x) = \frac{\sqrt{z^{2}+x^{2}}-z}{c} - \frac{\sqrt{\hat z^{2}+x^{2}}-\hat z}{\hat c} \;\approx\; \frac{\Delta c}{c}\,\frac{x^{2}}{z\,c}, \qquad \left\lvert\frac{\Delta c}{c}\right\rvert < \frac{\lambda F^{2}}{2z} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \lvert\Delta c\rvert < \frac{c^{2}F^{2}}{2 f_0 z} \]
(32.1)
\(\delta\tau(x)\)

\(c,\ \hat c,\ \Delta c\)

\(z,\ \hat z\)

The tolerance is the λ/8 (π/4 phase) criterion at the aperture edge \(x = X\). At 5 MHz and 30 mm it is 7.9 m/s at F/1, 32 m/s at F/2 and 71 m/s at F/3: a large aperture is both what needs the right speed and what can measure it. The curvature error is twice that of a pixel held at fixed depth, because the time-tied pixel moves by \(\Delta c/c\) as well.

The geometry and the parameterization follow from the same travel times. Displayed depth scales as \(\hat z = (\hat c/c)\,z\): 1 % of speed is 0.3 mm at 30 mm, larger than the axial resolution at 5 MHz. And every travel time is linear in slowness \(s = 1/c\), not in speed, which is why all the inversions below are written for the slowness deviation:

\[ \Delta s = \frac{1}{c} - \frac{1}{c_0}, \qquad \Delta c \approx -c_0^{2}\,\Delta s, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{ns/mm} \;\leftrightarrow\; 2.37\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \text{at}\ c_0 = 1540\ \mathrm{m/s} \]
(32.2)
\(\Delta s\)

\(c_0\)

A +40 m/s liver region is −16.4 ns/mm; 10 mm of it advances a one-way path by 164 ns, 0.8 of a period at 5 MHz (329 ns for the round trip of an echo from below it). The linearization error is \(\Delta c^{2}/c\): 1.0 m/s at 40 m/s, 2.5 % of the correction.

The biomarker. Imbault et al. (Phys. Med. Biol., 2017) estimated local liver speed in 17 patients; the estimates correlated with MRI proton-density fat fraction (R² = 0.69) and separated biopsy-graded steatosis with an area under the ROC curve of 0.952. The working numbers used in this module (patient speeds of about 1490–1600 m/s, steatosis cut-offs near 1541 and 1555 m/s) are taken from that paper's results and should be checked against it before reuse. Those numbers set the scale for everything in this module: a useful estimator must resolve about 10 m/s in a liver that sits under 10–30 mm of fat and muscle at 1450–1580 m/s. The overlying layers bias any average by tens of m/s (Section 32.3), so the hard part of liver sound speed is not precision, it is locality.

32.2Global sound speed by focus-quality maximization, and which average it returns

The oldest estimators sweep \(\hat c\), beamform, and keep the speed that maximizes a focus metric. Module 07 showed why that works: the van Cittert–Zernike theorem makes the aperture-domain coherence of speckle peak when the delays are right, and Module 14 turned the sweep into gradient ascent. The usual metrics, all evaluated on delayed channel signals \(s_i\) of a pixel or a region tied to two-way time:

\[ \mathrm{CF}(\hat c) = \frac{\left\lvert \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i(\hat c) \right\rvert^{2}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lvert s_i(\hat c)\rvert^{2}}, \qquad R_1(\hat c) = \frac{\mathrm{Re}\sum_i s_i\, s_{i+1}^{*}}{\sqrt{\sum_i \lvert s_i\rvert^{2} \sum_i \lvert s_{i+1}\rvert^{2}}}, \qquad B(\hat c) = \sum_{\mathrm{ROI}} \Bigl\lvert \sum_i s_i \Bigr\rvert^{2} \]
(32.3)

\(\mathrm{CF}\)

\(R_1\)

For a quadratic residual phase with edge value \(\Phi\) across a uniform aperture, \(\mathrm{CF} = \lvert\langle e^{j\Phi u^{2}}\rangle\rvert^{2}\): 0.95 at \(\Phi = \pi/4\), 0.5 at 2.73 rad, 0.39 at π. The CF peak is therefore broad (half-width about 67 m/s for a 19.2 mm aperture at 30 mm, F/1.6), and its centre, not its width, carries the estimate.

Anderson and Trahey (J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 1998) estimated the speed more directly from the arrival-time profile of the echoes across the aperture: in a homogeneous medium the profile is a hyperbola whose curvature fixes \(\hat c\) once the apex time is known. Both families answer the same question, "which homogeneous speed best explains the wavefront?", and both are exact only for a homogeneous medium. Under a fat layer they return an average. Which one?

Write the vertical one-way time through layers of thickness \(d_i\) and speed \(c_i\) as \(T = \sum_i \Delta T_i\), \(\Delta T_i = d_i/c_i\), and the time weights \(w_i = \Delta T_i/T\). Two averages are natural:

\[ \begin{gathered} V_h = \frac{z}{T} = \sum_i w_i\, c_i, \qquad V_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2} = \sum_i w_i\, c_i^{2} \\ V_{\mathrm{rms}} - V_h \approx \frac{\operatorname{Var}_w(c)}{2V_h} \;\overset{\text{two layers}}{=}\; \frac{w_1 w_2\,(c_2 - c_1)^{2}}{2V_h} \end{gathered} \]
(32.4)

\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\)

\(z,\ T\)

\(c_i\)

For small offsets the moveout of a reflection under flat layers is \(t^{2}(x) \approx t_0^{2} + x^{2}/V_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}\) (Dix, Geophysics, 1955), so a curvature fit or a focus maximizer returns \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\), not \(z/T\). For 10 mm of fat (1450) over liver (1580) at 40 mm: \(V_h\) = 1545.4, \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\) = 1546.4 m/s. The difference (1.1 m/s) is small; the difference from the liver itself (33 m/s) is not.

m32_global_sos_focus.m checks this on simulated channel data with Snell refraction: the CF-optimal speed lands 0.6–1.0 m/s above \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\) at every depth from 15 to 50 mm (1547.1 m/s at 40 mm), while the liver below is at 1580. Demo 32.1 runs the same physics on a point target and shows the residual phase across the aperture: at the optimum the quadratic part vanishes and only the non-hyperbolic (quartic) moveout of the layered medium is left.

32.3From average to local: layered media, Dix and the price of differentiation

A focus maximizer applied at many depths returns an average profile \(\bar c(z)\). The local speed is hidden in how that average changes with depth. In the vertical straight-ray model the one-way time to depth \(z\) is the integral of slowness, and the measured average is \(\bar c(z) = z/T(z)\). Written in slowness, the relation is linear, and inverting it is a derivative:

\[ \begin{gathered} z\,\bar s(z) = \int_0^{z} s(z')\,dz' \;\Longrightarrow\; s(z) = \frac{d}{dz}\bigl[z\,\bar s(z)\bigr] \\ s_k \approx \frac{z_k \bar s_k - z_{k-1}\bar s_{k-1}}{\Delta z}, \qquad \sigma_{c,\mathrm{loc}} \approx \frac{\sqrt{z_k^{2} + z_{k-1}^{2}}}{\Delta z}\,\sigma_{\bar c} \approx \sqrt2\,\frac{z}{\Delta z}\,\sigma_{\bar c} \end{gathered} \]
(32.5)
\(\bar s(z) = 1/\bar c(z)\)

\(s(z)\)

\(\Delta z\)

\(\sigma_{\bar c},\ \sigma_{c,\mathrm{loc}}\)

\(z\prime\)

\(k\)

\(z_k,\ \bar s_k\)

Differencing two averages taken 1 mm apart at 30 mm multiplies their noise by 42: 2 m/s on each average becomes about 85 m/s on the local speed (83.5 from the formula; m32_layered_inversion.m predicts 85.8 because the slowness-to-speed conversion adds the factor \((c_{\mathrm{loc}}/\bar c)^{2}\), and finds 87.1 by Monte Carlo; 142 and 148 at 50 mm). Locality costs \(z/\Delta z\) in precision, and no estimator escapes that factor without a prior.

\[ c_{\mathrm{int},k}^{2} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{rms},k}^{2}\,T_k - V_{\mathrm{rms},k-1}^{2}\,T_{k-1}}{T_k - T_{k-1}}, \qquad \text{straight-ray:}\quad c_{\mathrm{int},k} = \frac{z_k - z_{k-1}}{T_k - T_{k-1}},\ \ z_k = \hat c_k T_k \]
(32.6)

On the CF-optimal speeds of m32_global_sos_focus.m the liver interval speed comes out at 1580.1–1580.6 m/s by Dix and 1580.5–1582.5 m/s by the straight-ray rule (true 1580): the right formula matters at the 1–2 m/s level, the noise amplification of (32.5) at the 10–100 m/s level.

Two ways out of the noise. Regularization (Module 13) trades resolution for precision: penalize the first difference of the local slowness and pick \(\lambda\) by the discrepancy principle, \(\lVert \mathbf{W}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} - \mathbf{b})\rVert^{2} \approx m\), the number of averages. A layer prior uses the interfaces visible in the B-mode image and estimates one speed per layer, which turns the ill-posed derivative into a well-posed least-squares problem with three unknowns:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{s}}_\lambda = \arg\min_{\mathbf{s}} \lVert \mathbf{W}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} - \mathbf{b})\rVert^{2} + \lambda^{2}\lVert \mathbf{D}\mathbf{s}\rVert^{2}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}}_{\mathrm{layer}} = \mathbf{P}\,(\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{b},\ \ \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P} \]
(32.7)

\(\mathbf{b}\)

m32_layered_inversion.m, 2 m/s noise per average: Tikhonov (λ = 1.5×10⁵) gives the liver at 1548.6 m/s with 6.8 m/s RMSE but rounds the interfaces; the layer fit gives 1449.3 ± 0.8, 1587.6 ± 2.5 and 1548.9 ± 0.7 m/s for fat, muscle and liver (true 1450, 1585, 1550). The average at 60 mm is 1539.2 m/s, 10.8 m/s below the liver.

This is the logic of Jakovljevic et al. (J. Acoust. Soc. Am., 2018): Anderson-type averages first, then a model relating local speeds to path averages, solved by gradient descent. In two-layer phantoms and simulations their local estimates had biases below 6.2 m/s where the matched averages were biased by up to 66 m/s, at the cost of a larger standard deviation that a 5 × 5 mm region brought below 7 m/s. Ali and Dahl later moved the same idea to travel-time tomography from average speeds (IEEE IUS 2019), and Brevett, Sanabria, Ali and Dahl to common-midpoint gathers of non-beamformed data (IEEE IUS 2022).

Pitfall

Reporting the focus-optimal speed of a deep liver ROI as "the liver speed". Under 10 mm of fat (1450 over 1580 m/s) it reads about 45, 34, 22 and 17 m/s low (\(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\)) at 30, 40, 60 and 80 mm, more than the 14 m/s between the two Imbault cut-offs (working values, unverified; Section 32.1). A global estimate is a property of the path, not of the tissue at the end of it.

32.4Echo-mode tomography I: what CUTE measures

Averages along vertical paths cannot see lateral structure. Jaeger and colleagues (Ultrasound Med. Biol., 2015) added angle diversity with ordinary pulse-echo hardware: beamform the same region with \(c_0\) from several steered plane-wave transmits, and compare the phase of the complex images pixel by pixel. If the tissue is homogeneous at \(c_0\), every steered image of a given speckle pixel has the same phase. If not, each transmit direction accumulates a different travel-time error on its way to the pixel, and the echo phase shifts. In the straight-ray, first-order model (Section 13.8 built its linear algebra):

\[ L_\theta(x,z) = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}\int_0^{z} \Delta s\bigl(x - (z - z')\tan\theta,\ z'\bigr)\,dz', \qquad \Delta\tau_{\theta}(x,z) = L_\theta - L_0, \qquad \Delta\phi = 2\pi f_0\,\Delta\tau \]
(32.8)

\(\Delta s\)

\(\Delta\tau_\theta\)

\(\Delta\phi\)

\(x,\ z,\ z\prime\)

\(\theta\)

A +40 m/s inclusion of radius 5 mm (\(\Delta s\) = −16.4 ns/mm) gives up to 169 ns of shift at 15° below its centre, where the 0° path crosses the whole inclusion and the 15° path misses it (m32_cute_phase_shift.m; 164 ns from the inclusion chord, 5.7 ns from the 4 mm fat layer of the script), 5.3 rad at 5 MHz. The measurement is linear in \(\Delta s\) and differential: only differences between paths are seen.

Two practical limits follow at once. Wrapping: a phase is known modulo 2π, so \(\lvert\Delta\tau\rvert\) must stay below \(1/(2f_0)\) = 100 ns at 5 MHz. CUTE therefore measures between small angle steps and accumulates: a 2° step reaches only 56 ns (1.8 rad) for the same inclusion. Decorrelation: the phase of a speckle pixel is random, and only its change is meaningful if the two images contain the same speckle. Steering the transmit alone changes the speckle pattern. Stähli et al. \[ \begin{gathered} d_{\gamma}(x,z) = \bigl[L_{\varphi_n} + L_{\psi_m}\bigr] - \bigl[L_{\varphi_{n'}} + L_{\psi_{m'}}\bigr], \qquad \tfrac12(\varphi_n + \psi_m) = \tfrac12(\varphi_{n'} + \psi_{m'}) = \gamma \\ \text{flat layer, } (\theta,-\theta)\ \text{vs}\ (0,0)\text{:}\quad d = 2(\sec\theta - 1)\,S \end{gathered} \]

\[ \hat\phi = \arg\sum_{k=1}^{N} a_{2,k}\,a_{1,k}^{*}, \qquad \sigma_{\phi} \approx \frac{\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}}{\rho\sqrt{2N}}, \qquad \sigma_\tau = \frac{\sigma_\phi}{2\pi f_0} \]

Monte Carlo in m32_cute_phase_shift.m: N = 64, ρ = 0.95 gives 0.0295 rad (formula 0.0291), 0.94 ns at 5 MHz; ρ = 0.99 gives 0.41 ns. The formula fails at low ρ and small N (0.355 versus 0.306 rad at ρ = 0.5, N = 16) where the phase distribution grows tails. Correlation is worth more than kernel size: going from ρ = 0.9 to 0.99 is worth a factor 3.4 in σ, the same as 12 times more samples.

Put the pieces together for the hardest case: the speed of a 5 mm layer from common-mid-angle data at ±20° with 0.92 ns jitter (ρ = 0.95, N = 64) is uncertain by 2.0 ns/mm, i.e. 5 m/s, from a single pair of pixel data (the layer delay \(S\) is the datum just below the layer minus the datum just above it, so \(\sqrt2 \times 0.92\) ns divided by \(2(\sec 20° - 1) \times 5\) mm). Averaging laterally helps; the axial blur of Section 32.5 does not. Stähli et al. also showed that a quantitative forward model needs a second correction: the misfocused echo is reconstructed at a shifted position, which adds a phase shift of its own. With both features their liver-phantom reconstructions came within a few m/s of the through-transmission values (standard error 2.5–10 m/s) and a volunteer liver read 1564 ± 4 m/s.

\(\mathbf{R}\) does not depend on the data, only on the acquisition design and \(\lambda\). Compute it before acquiring anything: it is the design tool of SoS tomography, as the beam pattern is of an array.

Limited angle. Rays within ±θmax of vertical measure, by the Fourier-slice theorem, only a wedge of spatial frequencies around the \(k_x\) axis (Section 13.8). Everything near \(k_z\), horizontal edges and the axial profile, sits in the missing cone. The resolution kernels are therefore elongated in depth and get worse with depth: m32_sos_tomography.m (θmax = 25°, 7 angles, 2.4 mm cells) finds axial/lateral FWHM of 2.5/2.4, 2.6/2.4 and 3.4/2.4 mm at 9, 19 and 31 mm for transmit-only data. Layers and the absolute level are in the same blind spot: a constant \(\Delta s_0\) shifts every datum by only \(\Delta s_0 z(\sec\theta - 1)\), so the mean speed must come from somewhere else. That is why CUTE reconstructions are run with an a priori speed and why Stähli et al. (2020, Figs. 4–6 of the preprint) and Jaeger et al. (2022, Figs. 9–10) show their maps for several a priori values.

Forward-model error dominates noise. The same script separates the error sources by running each design twice. With data generated by the cell model itself plus 2 ns noise (noise and regularization only) the RMSE is 3.8 m/s for transmit-only and 1.9 m/s for common mid-angle. With noise-free data from fine straight rays through a smooth truth (discretization and regularization only) it is 22.0 and 30.8 m/s. The model, not the noise, is the error budget, and adding more data rows does not fix it. In the ray model a common-mid-angle row \((W_i + W_j) - (W_{i-1} + W_{j+1})\) is exactly a combination of transmit-only rows of the same pixel (the script verifies a residual of 5×10⁻¹⁵): CMA adds no ray information. Its benefit lives outside this model, in the speckle correlation of (32.10) and the echo-position term that Stähli et al. added. Spatial-domain formulations of the same reconstruction (Sanabria et al., Phys. Med. Biol., 2018) and learned forward models are attempts to shrink exactly this model error.

Intuition

Echo-mode SoS tomography is transmission tomography with the sources and detectors on the same side, so every ray is nearly vertical. It sees lateral changes well, axial changes poorly and uniform offsets hardly at all, which is the opposite of what a liver protocol needs (a layered abdominal wall over a uniform organ). Every successful method adds what the rays cannot give: an a priori mean, interfaces from the image, or a better forward model.

Module 14 climbed a single \(\hat c\) by gradient ascent on a focus metric. Differentiable beamforming does the same for a whole map. Simson et al. Reported errors (arXiv version 3): −11 m/s in a simulated inclusion, 8–14 m/s below calibrated values in layered and commercial phantoms, from 12 m/s below to 42 m/s above in graphite–gelatin phantoms.

Why phase losses have local minima. A phase is periodic in delay with period \(1/f_0\). Once a residual delay difference exceeds half a period the wrapped phase points the wrong way, and gradient descent locks onto a wrong cycle, the cycle skipping of Section 14.4. The ways out are the ones seismology uses: start from a good global estimate (Section 32.2), compare only nearby (lag-1) and well-correlated signals, go from low to high frequency, or use envelope and coherence losses that are smooth but less sharp. Demo 32.5 maps the loss over a two-layer model and lets you start the descent anywhere.

Learned inversion. Feigin, Freedman and Anthony (IEEE Trans. Biomed. Eng., 2020; preprint 2018) trained a fully convolutional network to map raw channel data of three plane waves directly to a sound-speed map. It was trained on 6,026 k-Wave simulations of random elliptical inclusions (1300–1800 m/s) in speckle and reached 20.5 m/s RMSE on simulated test data; on a phantom and in vivo the results were qualitative. The forward model is now implicit in the training set, so the model error of Section 32.5 becomes a domain gap that no regularizer can see.

Full-wave inversion. Straight rays ignore refraction and diffraction, which are not small at fat–muscle interfaces. Geophysics solved the same problem with wave-equation migration velocity analysis; Ali's thesis (Stanford, 2021) laid the groundwork for it in pulse-echo ultrasound. On the IEEE IUS 2026 QUASOS challenge, which scored bias, variance, lesion detectability and robustness to near-field aberration on phantom and simulated RF data, the published final sound-speed scores (lower is better) put R. Ali (Rochester) first at 22.6 and the Bern CUTE group (M. Jaeger) ninth at 72.5 among 11 entries.

Sound speed is upstream of everything quantitative. A 1 % speed error is a 0.3 mm depth error at 30 mm, larger than the localization precision ULM claims.

32.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why does a 1540 m/s assumption hurt a high-frequency, low-F-number image more than a low-frequency, high-F-number one? · screen

The speed error appears as a curvature error, \(\delta\tau(X) = (\Delta c/c)X^{2}/(zc)\); in phase that is \(2\pi f_0\delta\tau\). Requiring less than π/4 at the edge gives \(\lvert\Delta c\rvert < c^{2}F^{2}/(2f_0z)\): inversely proportional to frequency and proportional to F². At 5 MHz and 30 mm: 7.9 m/s at F/1, 71 m/s at F/3. Follow-up: and the depth error? \((\Delta c/c)z\), independent of aperture: 0.3 mm per percent at 30 mm.

I2You sweep the assumed speed and maximize coherence in a liver ROI at 50 mm under 10 mm of subcutaneous fat. What number did you measure? · screen

An average of the path, specifically the RMS speed \(V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\sum w_i c_i^{2}}\) (time weights), because small-offset moveout under flat layers is hyperbolic with \(V_{\mathrm{rms}}\) (Dix). With 1450 over 1580 m/s it reads about 1553, 27 m/s below the liver, and only about 1 m/s above \(z/T\). Follow-up: how do you get the liver itself? Measure at several depths and invert (Dix or regularized), or use a local method (tomography, layer fit with the B-mode interfaces).

I3Derive the noise of a local speed obtained by differencing two average speeds. · onsite

\(z\bar s(z) = \int_0^{z}s\), so \(s_k = (z_k\bar s_k - z_{k-1}\bar s_{k-1})/\Delta z\). With independent errors \(\sigma_{\bar s} = \sigma_{\bar c}/c^{2}\) on each average, \(\sigma_{s} = \sigma_{\bar s}\sqrt{z_k^{2} + z_{k-1}^{2}}/\Delta z\), i.e. \(\sigma_{c,\mathrm{loc}} \approx \sqrt2(z/\Delta z)\sigma_{\bar c}\). 2 m/s averages 1 mm apart at 30 mm give 85 m/s. Follow-up: what fixes it? Larger Δz (resolution), Tikhonov with a discrepancy-chosen λ, or a layer prior from the image, each a different bias.

I4Explain CUTE to a beamforming engineer in two minutes, including what limits it. · onsite

Beamform the same region with \(c_0\) from several steered plane waves (and receive angles); for each pixel the echo phase changes between steering angles in proportion to the difference of the travel-time errors along the two paths, \(\Delta\tau = L_\theta - L_{\theta'}\), \(L_\theta = \sec\theta\int\Delta s\). Stack all pixels and angles into \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{A}\Delta\mathbf{s}\) and invert with regularization. Limits: phase wraps at \(1/(2f_0)\) (small steps, accumulate), speckle decorrelation between angles (common mid-angle pairing), limited angle (axial blur, layers and the mean nearly invisible), and the forward model (straight rays, echo-position shift). Follow-up: what did Stähli et al. change? Common mid-angle pairs and the echo-position term.

I5Why is a laterally uniform fat layer hard for echo-mode tomography? Quantify. · onsite

All steered paths cross it, with lengths \(d/\cos\theta\); the difference to 0° is \((\sec\theta - 1)S\), where \(S\) is the vertical delay. For 4 mm at 1450 m/s, \(S\) = 161 ns, but the 15° datum moves by 5.7 ns (11.4 ns for a symmetric common-mid-angle pair), comparable to a few times the 1 ns jitter of one datum. A constant offset of the mean behaves the same way. Follow-up: so how do you anchor the mean? A global coherence estimate, a calibrated a priori value, or physics beyond straight rays.

I6What is the phase jitter of a CUTE datum, and what is the cheapest way to halve it? · onsite

\(\sigma_\phi \approx \sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}/(\rho\sqrt{2N})\), the interferometric-phase CRLB; at ρ = 0.95, N = 64: 0.029 rad, 0.93 ns at 5 MHz. Halving it by kernel size needs 4N (resolution loss); raising ρ from 0.95 to 0.987 does the same. Hence pairing schemes that keep speckle correlated (common mid-angle, common midpoint), small angle steps and good SNR. Follow-up: when does the formula fail? Low ρ, small N: the distribution grows tails and wraps.

I7Your SoS tomography gives 25 m/s RMSE on a phantom. More angles or a better forward model? · research

Check which term dominates first. Rerun the inversion on data synthesized by your own forward model with your measured noise level: if that RMSE is small (a few m/s, as in m32_sos_tomography.m: 3.8 and 1.9 m/s), the 25 m/s is model error (straight rays, refraction, echo-position shift, discretization, lens) and more angles will not help. Then look at the resolution matrix to see whether the phantom features are resolvable at all. Follow-up: how would you validate the forward model alone? Full-wave simulation of the phantom geometry, compared datum by datum.

I8What is the relationship between differentiable-beamforming autofocus and CUTE? · research

With straight rays, each beamforming delay is \(\sum_j \ell_{ij}s_j\); the chain rule makes the gradient of any delay-based loss \(\mathbf{A}^{T}\nabla_\tau\mathcal{L}\), a back-projection along the same rays CUTE uses. Gradient descent is a nonlinear iterative tomography that recomputes the delays (and could recompute bent rays) at each step; the iteration count and TV play the role of λ. It shares CUTE's missing cone and benefits from the same correlated pairings (common midpoint). Follow-up: what does it buy? Losses evaluated on the actual beamformed data, any differentiable forward model, and easy priors.

I9Where do phase-based autofocus losses get stuck, and how would you initialize? · research

Wherever a residual delay difference exceeds half a period, the wrapped phase reverses and the loss has a secondary minimum: cycle skipping (Module 14). Long subaperture lags and high frequency make it worse. Initialize with a coherence- or CF-based global speed (smooth, wide basin), use lag-1 pairs with a correlation threshold (as in CMPE), and go from low to high frequency. Follow-up: can coherence alone be the loss? Yes, it is smooth but flat near the optimum and biased toward the path average.

I10Write the error budget for a liver sound-speed number reported to a hepatologist. · research

Random: curvature or phase CRLB divided by √M (sub-m/s after averaging). Biases: layer bias if an average is reported (17–45 m/s under 10 mm of fat at 30–80 mm), forward-model error (refraction, straight rays, echo position: tens of m/s in tomography), calibration (≈ 0.8 m/s per ns of edge delay error for 64 elements at 30 mm; lens speed and thickness), regularization and the a priori mean. Compare with the 14 m/s between the Imbault cut-offs (working values, unverified). Follow-up: how do you validate? Phantoms with through-transmission references (±5 m/s), then patients against MRI-PDFF.

I11Would you trust a deep network that outputs a sound-speed map from channel data? · research

Only inside its training distribution. Feigin et al. (2020) trained on 6,026 k-Wave simulations of random ellipses and reported 20.5 m/s RMSE on simulated test data, with qualitative results in vivo: the forward model is now the simulator, and anatomy, lens, attenuation and noise mismatches become an unseen domain gap. I would want physics anchoring (unrolled or model-based inversion), uncertainty estimates and benchmark results on independent data such as QUASOS. Follow-up: what is a good sanity test? Feed a homogeneous phantom with known speed and a layered phantom; check the mean and the layer bias.

Q1At 7.5 MHz, F/1.5 and 25 mm, what speed error puts the edge phase at π/4?

\(\sqrt{45^{2} + 40^{2}}/5\) × 1 = 12.0 m/s.

Q5A 3 mm-radius region at −30 m/s (relative to 1540). Maximum vertical chord delay, and does a 10° transmit-only step wrap at 6 MHz?

\(\Delta s\) = 1/1510 − 1/1540 = 12.9 ns/mm. The longest chord through a circle is its diameter in any direction, 6 mm, so no datum can exceed 77 ns, even if one path crosses the full chord and the other misses the region. Wrapping starts at 1/(2f₀) = 83 ns at 6 MHz: no wrap, whatever the step.

\(\sigma_\phi\) = √0.19 / (0.9 × √200) = 0.0342 rad; \(\sigma_\tau\) = 0.0342/(2π × 5×10⁶) = 1.09 ns.
