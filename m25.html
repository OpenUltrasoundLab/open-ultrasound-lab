Adaptive and coherence-based beamforming: MV, CF, GCF, PCF, DMAS, SLSC, FXPF

After the delays, every pixel is a vector of 64 channel samples. DAS adds them with fixed weights. Every method in this module looks at that vector first and then decides what to output. Here we derive each one, put all of them on one simulated dataset with honest metrics, and show which improvements are real and which come from dynamic-range stretching.

In 60 seconds
  1. One aperture vector: After the focusing delays, an echo from the focal point looks the same on every element, while off-axis echoes, clutter and noise vary across the aperture. Delay-and-sum (DAS) adds the delayed channels with equal weights. Each adaptive beamformer is another rule for rejecting what is not constant across the aperture, so compare methods on this one vector. §25.1
  2. Minimum variance: Minimum variance (MV) picks channel weights that minimize output power with unit gain toward the focus; in white noise it equals DAS. Unit gain on the modelled focus direction holds exactly, but other echoes are coherent with the wanted one, and MV uses that coherent interference to cancel the wanted signal at its output unless the channel covariance is averaged over subarrays and time. A lone MV point narrows as noise falls, so judge resolution with two points. §25.2
  3. Coherence weighting: The coherence factor (CF) is the fraction of channel energy that adds in phase: \(\mathrm{CF} = |\sum_i s_i|^2/(N\sum_i |s_i|^2)\), with \(s_i\) the delayed sample on element i of N. It is near 1 for a point, 1/N for noise, about 2/3 for focused-transmit speckle. As a pixel gain it stretches dynamic range rather than sharpening. §25.3
  4. Nonlinear summation and coherence imaging: Delay-multiply-and-sum (DMAS) combines channel pairs through signed square roots of their products, close to the DAS beam squared: narrower mainlobe, sidelobes doubled in dB. Short-lag spatial coherence (SLSC) images how well nearby elements correlate, so noise maps near zero and clutter fades, but a point stays bright wherever it beats the noise and images wide. §25.4
  5. Channel-data filters: The f-x prediction filter (FXPF) predicts each channel from its neighbours, so it keeps anything predictable, off-axis plane waves included, and removes only spatially incoherent energy. ADMIRE (aperture domain model image reconstruction) fits wavefronts from inside and outside the region of interest and rebuilds from the inside part, so it also removes off-axis clutter. §25.5
  6. Fair comparison: Many adaptive methods act like a pixel weight or power law, which stretches dB contrast without making a lesion easier to detect: \(\mathrm{CR_{dB}}(A^p) = p\,\mathrm{CR_{dB}}(A)\), with CR the contrast ratio and A the amplitude. gCNR, one minus the lesion–background histogram overlap, ignores such maps. Tune every method by one rule; retest under noise and aberration. §25.6 §25.7
From the field
  • Judge adaptive beamformers by histogram overlap (gCNR) and two-point resolution, not contrast ratio. A pixel weight or power law stretches dB contrast without separating lesion and background histograms; gCNR, one minus their overlap, does not move. On the module's data three adaptive methods report 42–71 dB contrast while their gCNR (0.91–0.99) sits near delay-and-sum's 0.96. §25.7
  • Test resolution with two close points, never one bright point. A lone minimum-variance (MV) point narrows as noise falls, so its width reports signal-to-noise ratio, not resolving power. Image a pair at known spacing with fine lateral sampling and read the dip: at 0.5 mm spacing MV dips 12 dB where delay-and-sum shows −1.3 dB. §25.2
  • Add an aberration error before you believe a minimum-variance result. It treats a mis-steered echo as interference and cancels it. With 0.55 rad phase error across 12 elements the echo ends 37.1 dB below delay-and-sum; diagonal loading (adding the mean diagonal value, δ = 1, to the covariance diagonal) restores it to −2.6 dB. §25.2
  • Repeat any ranking at more than one noise level. Adaptive estimates degrade with channel noise at different rates, so a ranking found at one noise level need not hold at another. On the module's data the minimum-variance gCNR (one minus the histogram overlap) falls below delay-and-sum at −5 dB channel noise, 0.83 against 0.91. §25.6
  • Check the real-time compute budget before adopting a paper result. Minimum variance needs a covariance estimate and a matrix solve at every pixel; coherence weights stay close to a plain sum. With 32-element subarrays and ±2 samples of time averaging it costs about 16 000 complex multiply-accumulates per pixel, roughly 250 times delay-and-sum. §25.9

25.1One pixel, one aperture vector: where each method acts

Method map step 1 of 7 · the aperture vector

Fix a pixel \(\mathbf{r} = (x, z)\). For every transmit angle θ and element i, read the IQ channel sample at the round-trip delay, rotate it by the carrier phase of that delay, and sum over angles. The result is the aperture vector \(\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{r}) \in \mathbb{C}^N\), with N = 64 here. Its real part is the delayed RF sample on each element, which is what DMAS and SLSC use.

Intuition

Read the aperture spectrum as a map of arrival directions: bin k is a wave arriving from lateral offset \(\Delta x \approx k\lambda z/(Np)\), 0.48 mm per bin at 30 mm. DAS keeps bin 0 and nothing else. CF is the fraction of energy in bin 0. GCF widens the window to \(\lvert k\rvert \le M_0\). MV and ESBMV build weights that null whichever bins carry interference. FXPF keeps whatever few discrete bins (plane waves) explain the data best.

What you get

One common input for every method, and a language to predict them: a focused echo is a constant aperture vector; clutter is a phase ramp, random phase or amplitude pattern. Cost: the delayed, compounded aperture data, about 60 µs per pixel, shared by all methods.

Key takeaways
  • At each pixel the delayed, compounded aperture vector s ∈ ℂN (N = 64) is the common input; DAS sums it with equal weights.
  • After the delays a focused echo is the same on every element (steering vector a = 1); off-axis echoes, clutter and noise appear as a phase ramp, a random phase or an amplitude pattern.
  • In the aperture spectrum, bin k is a wave from lateral offset Δx ≈ kλz/(Np), 0.48 mm per bin at 30 mm: DAS keeps bin 0 only.

25.2Minimum variance (Capon) and the three fixes ultrasound needs

Method map step 2 of 7 · adaptive aperture weights

Module 07 introduced MVDR and the snapshot problem. This section gives the beamformer as ultrasound actually implements it (Synnevåg, Austeng & Holm, IEEE TUFFC 2007 and 2009). It minimizes the output power subject to unit gain toward the focus:

\[ \begin{gathered} \min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{a} = 1 \\ \Rightarrow\ \ \mathbf{w} = \frac{\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}^{H}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}}, \qquad \mathbf{a} = \mathbf{1} \\ P_{\mathrm{MV}} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^{H}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}} \end{gathered} \]
(25.2)

Derivation of (25.2)

Lagrangian \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w} + \lambda(1 - \mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{a}) + \lambda^*(1 - \mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{w})\); setting the gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{w}^*\) to zero gives \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w} = \lambda\mathbf{a}\) (Capon, Proc. IEEE 1969).

Three properties of echo data break the textbook formula. Each has a standard fix, and the estimator (25.3) combines all three.

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{R}} &= \frac{1}{(2K+1)P}\sum_{k=-K}^{K}\sum_{l=0}^{N-L} \\ &\qquad \mathbf{s}_l(t+k)\,\mathbf{s}_l^{H}(t+k) + \varepsilon\mathbf{I} \\ \varepsilon &= \Delta\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}})/L \\ y_{\mathrm{MV}}(t) &= \frac{1}{P}\sum_l \mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{s}_l(t) \\ P &= N - L + 1 \\ \mathbf{s}_l &= [s_l, \dots, s_{l+L-1}]^{T} \end{aligned} \]

Diagonal loading ε, here \(\Delta = 10^{-2}\) of the mean element power (Cox, Zeskind & Owen, IEEE Trans. ASSP 1987). Our engine uses L = 32, K = 2 samples of 75 µm (±0.19 µs), Δ = 0.01.
Forward-backward averaging (not used on this page)

Forward-backward averaging, \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{FB}} = \tfrac12(\hat{\mathbf{R}} + \mathbf{J}\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{T}\mathbf{J})\) with \(\mathbf{J}\) the exchange (flip) matrix, doubles the effective snapshot count for the symmetric steering vector \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{1}\) and is a common addition (Asl & Mahloojifar, IEEE TUFFC 2011); this page uses forward smoothing only.

Smoothing is not optional. Without it, MV meets its constraint by using a coherent interferer to cancel the signal (Widrow et al., IEEE Trans. Antennas Propag. 1982). Subarray l sees the interferer with an extra phase \(e^{\,j\psi l}\), so averaging over \(P = N - L + 1\) subarrays multiplies the cross term by the Dirichlet factor (25.4).

Why one snapshot cancels the signal

Let the desired echo be s and a coherent interferer be βs with steering \(\mathbf{v}\). With one snapshot model \(\mathbf{x} = s(\mathbf{a} + \beta\mathbf{v})\), \(\mathbf{R} = \sigma^2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^H\) with \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{a} + \beta\mathbf{v}\). Minimizing \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\) drives \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{u} \to 0\), and with \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{a} = 1\) that forces \(\beta\,\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{v} = -1\).

Pitfall: a wrong steering vector makes MV null the echo it should keep

The constraint \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{a} = 1\) protects only the assumed wavefront. If the true echo arrives with a slightly different one (a sound-speed or aberration error), MV sees it as interference and suppresses it while still meeting the constraint. Worked example (exact covariance, no estimation noise): N = 12, true wavefront \(a_m = e^{j0.05m}\) (0.55 rad, 31°, of phase error across the aperture), assumed \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{1}\), echo power 10 per channel, white noise 0.05. DAS keeps 0.985 of the echo amplitude; MV keeps 0.0137, −37.1 dB below DAS. Diagonal loading of δ times the mean diagonal restores it: −27.8 dB at δ = 0.01, −12.8 dB at δ = 0.1, −2.6 dB at δ = 1. High SNR makes this worse, not better, which is why ultrasound MV uses loading, subarray smoothing and temporal averaging even on clean data, and why its resolution collapses quickly under aberration (25.7). An external review reproduced these numbers independently (October 2026).

Pitfall: the MV point is an SNR meter

For one point in white noise the MV output has a closed form whose −6 dB width is \(0.914/\sqrt{N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}}\) times the DAS width: 0.036, 0.011 and 0.0036 of it at 10, 20 and 30 dB. A lone point therefore reports SNR and loading, not resolution: loading caps the effective SNR near 1/Δ. Judge resolution with two points (the pair dip in Demo 25.4) and sample laterally finer than the MV peak, or bright points vanish between pixels (−23 dB at half a 0.15 mm pixel in our dataset).

The closed form and its check

\(\lvert y(x)\rvert = \sigma^2\lvert\mathbf{v}^H\mathbf{a}\rvert/Q\) with \(Q = N(\sigma^2 + N) - \lvert\mathbf{v}^H\mathbf{a}\rvert^2\), for signal power 1 per channel. It matches a direct solve to 1 % at 10, 20 and 30 dB.

Running case

What you get

You get sharper points and a resolved pair from MV, and ESBMV's clean background on well-focused data. You pay about 250 × the DAS sum (ESBMV 2–3 × more), lower speckle SNR, and a steep loss under steering or aberration error unless you load and smooth.

Key takeaways
  • Capon weights minimize output power under unit gain toward the focus; in white noise they reduce to 1/N, so DAS is MV in white noise.
  • Echoes are coherent: without subarray smoothing (L = 64) the desired-signal gain falls to about −66 dB; L = 48 restores −0.3 dB in Demo 25.2.
  • A wrong steering vector is suppressed as interference: N = 12 with 0.55 rad of phase error keeps 0.0137 of the echo amplitude, −37.1 dB below DAS.
  • A lone MV point is 0.914/√(N·SNR) times the DAS width (0.036, 0.011, 0.0036 at 10, 20, 30 dB): judge resolution with two points and fine lateral sampling.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{CF} &= \frac{\lvert\sum_i s_i\rvert^{2}}{N\sum_i \lvert s_i\rvert^{2}} \\ &= \frac{\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^{2} + \hat{\sigma}^{2}} \\ \bar{y} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_i s_i \\ \hat{\sigma}^{2} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \lvert s_i - \bar{y}\rvert^{2} \\ H_{\mathrm{Wiener}} &= \frac{\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^{2} + \hat{\sigma}^{2}/N} \\ &= \frac{N\cdot\mathrm{CF}}{(N - 1)\,\mathrm{CF} + 1} \end{aligned} \]

DMAS (Matrone, Savoia, Caliano & Magenes, IEEE TMI 2015) multiplies every pair of delayed RF channels, restores amplitude units with a signed square root, sums, and band-passes at \(2f_0\) (F-DMAS):

Sampling and the 2f₀ band-pass

\(s_i\) is real RF here, sampled every 18.75 µm ≈ λ/16 in depth (41 MHz equivalent) so that the \(2f_0\) band is not aliased. For an aligned echo every pair term equals \(\lvert \mathrm{rf}(t)\rvert\), whose spectrum has a DC term, a \(2f_0\) band and weaker \(4f_0\): the band-pass (here Gaussian, centred at \(2f_0\) with \(\sigma = \sqrt{2}\,\sigma_f\)) keeps only the \(2f_0\) part.

The beam narrows because, in a narrowband picture, the \(2f_0\) term of the pair (i, j) carries the phase \(\varphi_i + \varphi_j\), as if a virtual element at \((x_i + x_j)/2\) received at twice the frequency. \[ \sum_{i<j} e^{\,j(\varphi_i + \varphi_j)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big[\Big(\sum_i e^{\,j\varphi_i}\Big)^{2} - \sum_i e^{\,j2\varphi_i}\Big] \]

On our broadband plane-wave data the gain is smaller (FWHM 0.43 mm vs 0.48 mm, peak sidelobe −27 vs −19 dB), because the transmit part of the PSF is shared and the square-root compression mixes harmonics.

What you get

F-DMAS buys lower sidelobes and a slightly narrower point at 1–10 × the DAS cost; SLSC buys the best lesion separability and graceful aberration behaviour. You pay a small gain on broadband data (F-DMAS) and an image that is not amplitude, with wide points (SLSC).

Key takeaways
  • F-DMAS multiplies channel pairs, restores amplitude with a signed square root and band-passes at 2f₀; the O(N) form avoids the 2016 pair products of N = 64.
  • Narrowband DMAS ≈ DAS beam squared: 0.74× the −6 dB width and −26.5 dB instead of −13.3 dB sidelobes; on the broadband plane-wave data FWHM 0.43 vs 0.48 mm and PSL −27 vs −19 dB.
  • SLSC sums R(m) for m ≤ M: point ≈ M, speckle ≈ 5.7 at M = 8 (6.0 simulated), noise ≈ 0; clutter-robust, but a point is about 1.9 mm wide.

25.5Channel-data filters: f-x prediction (FXPF) and the ADMIRE idea

Method map step 5 of 7 · channel-data filters, before the delays and sum

FXPF borrows f-x deconvolution from seismic noise attenuation (Canales, SEG Expanded Abstracts, 1984) and applies it to the delayed channel data before DAS (Shin & Huang, Proc. SPIE 9790, 2016; Shin, Huang & Yen, IEEE TUFFC 65(10), 2018). In a short depth window, take the FFT of each channel along time; at each frequency the aperture is a complex sequence \(X_1(f), \dots, X_N(f)\). A sum of q plane waves across the aperture is exactly predictable by a linear recursion of order q (the Prony / linear-prediction property), so the filter fits that recursion by least squares, replaces the data by its prediction, inverts the FFT and beamforms.

\[ \begin{aligned} X_i(f) &\approx \sum_{k=1}^{p} a_k(f)\,X_{i-k}(f) \\ X_i(f) &\approx \sum_{k=1}^{p} b_k(f)\,X_{i+k}(f) \\ \hat{\mathbf{a}} &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{a}} \sum_i \Big\lvert X_i - \sum_k a_k X_{i-k}\Big\rvert^{2} + \mu\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert_2^{2} \\ &= (\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{A} + \mu\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{H}\mathbf{x} \end{aligned} \]

The focused echo is constant across the aperture and survives exactly. An off-axis echo is a linear phase ramp: also predictable, so FXPF keeps coherent off-axis clutter and leaves its suppression to the DAS sum. What it removes is spatially incoherent energy: electronic noise, and reverberation or multipath clutter that does not look like a few plane waves. A second, less advertised effect is that at low channel SNR the fitted coefficients shrink toward zero (regression dilution), so weak echoes are attenuated too. FXPF is therefore an SNR-dependent gain, like the Wiener postfilter, but built from prediction rather than energy ratios.

Model-based suppression: ADMIRE

FXPF asks whether the aperture data are predictable; ADMIRE (aperture domain model image reconstruction; Byram, Dei, Tierney & Dumont, IEEE TUFFC 62(11), 2015) asks where each part of them came from. In every short depth window and at every frequency bin of the pulse band it writes the delayed aperture vector as a sum of modelled wavefronts: echoes of scatterers inside a small region of interest (ROI) around the pixel, and echoes of scatterers outside it, at other lateral positions and with extra path length (off-axis and multipath sources). An elastic-net regression picks a sparse set of those wavefronts; the channel data are rebuilt from the ROI wavefronts only, transformed back to time and summed (DAS). Because the model knows the direction and timing of each wavefront, it can remove coherent off-axis clutter that FXPF keeps.

The third line is one step of cyclic coordinate descent, the solver of the released C code (y is first divided by its standard deviation). After the fit, \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\mathrm{dec}}\) is placed in its bin, all other bins are set to zero, the window is inverse-transformed and summed over elements.

The regression has to be regularized. With the released model space each fit has about 31 000 complex predictors (62 000 real unknowns) for 128 real numbers: hundreds of times underdetermined. The L1 term makes the answer sparse (here 30–200 nonzero coefficients per fit); the small L2 term spreads weight across near-identical neighbouring columns instead of picking one at random. Because λ scales with the rms of each window's data, the method is invariant to gain: what decides the outcome is how the energy divides between ROI and outer wavefronts, not the absolute level.

What survives follows from the geometry. A scatterer inside the resolution cell produces a nearly flat wavefront after focusing; ROI columns match it (model–data correlation ≥ 0.999 for isolated points in our tests) and it survives. An off-axis scatterer produces a tilted, partly truncated wavefront; outer columns take it and it is discarded. In an anechoic cyst the window contains only off-axis clutter and noise, so the fit often uses no ROI column at all and the pixel is exactly zero. Electronic noise matches neither family well: part of it ends up in the ROI part, part in the residual.

All computed on each method's own output, A ≥ 0. Why ADMIRE is computed offline, and how our ADMIRE compares with the authors' study

ADMIRE is one of the eleven methods, not a special case: it is treated as the others (same data, same metrics, same tuning rule). Only its cost differs. Each pixel needs elastic-net fits with about 31 000 predictors, roughly 104–105 times the work of the other methods, so its full-model image is computed offline for 8 noise levels × 3 aberration levels and shown in the eleventh panel, and a reduced model can be run live in the page. Compared with the authors' plane-wave study (Dei, Tierney & Byram 2018, simulated PICMUS cyst, 75 angles), our ADMIRE gains in contrast are smaller: they report ADMIRE contrast more than 30 dB above DAS for channel SNR ≥ 10 dB (at least 10 dB at the lowest SNRs) and no substantial CNR gain over the SNR range. They tuned the model space for each plane-wave count; we use the released model space with one tuned parameter (cλ). They also replaced the RF Hilbert-transform envelope by an FIR Hilbert filter, which added about 7 dB of ADMIRE contrast at channel SNR ≥ 30 dB; our data are complex IQ, so the envelope is the magnitude and that artifact does not arise here. Read our ADMIRE panel as a conservative implementation, not the best ADMIRE can do.

The slider sets the noise per channel, relative to the mean speckle power in one element and one angle; the image sees something else. Why beamformed noise looks like speckle: the noise covariance

The noise never touched the sound field, yet it can look like speckle. Speckle-like texture is not an acoustic phenomenon: it appears whenever a random complex field passes through a band-limited linear system, and the beamformer is one. Line-by-line and per-pixel noise: texture and metrics

The correlated texture holds here because every pixel is beamformed from the same recordings (plane-wave or synthetic-aperture imaging). In conventional line-by-line focused imaging each image line comes from its own transmit and recording, so pixels on different lines share no channel samples and \(R_n\) between lines is zero; along a line the noise stays correlated over about a pulse length. The texture is then fine and nearly salt-and-pepper laterally, streaky along depth (the scan converter's interpolation between lines softens it to the line spacing). The selector shows both, plus the non-physical case. At +30 dB channel noise (image SNR −16.1 dB, background ROI essentially noise) the one-pixel envelope correlations lateral/axial are 0.73/0.70 for plane-wave noise, 0.04/0.71 for the line-by-line emulation and 0.00/−0.01 for per-pixel noise, all with μ/σ of 1.7–1.9. Per-pixel salt and pepper appears in real systems only when the noise enters after the band-limited imaging chain (detector or display electronics, quantization, image-domain processing), or when the pixel grid is much coarser than the PSF.

Nonlinear beamformers change the texture further: CF and MV re-weight noise pixel by pixel. With the line-by-line option for all beamformers at +10 dB every panel turns laterally fine and streaky along depth, but the region metrics barely move for most methods (CR / gCNR shared → line-by-line: CF 14.8 → 13.5 dB / 0.40 → 0.43, PCF 10.0 → 9.3 dB / 0.37 → 0.39, SLSC 15.2 → 15.3 dB / 0.80 → 0.78, MV 4.8 → 4.2 dB / 0.35 → 0.34); ESBMV loses most (14.3 → 10.7 dB, 0.72 → 0.63) because its covariance and subspace now change from line to line, and the profile metrics become noise-limited (each fine profile line has its own noise, so FWHM and the pair dip read jagged noise: CF FWHM 0.28 → 0.07 mm, SLSC 1.31 → 0.03 mm; at −20 dB only the far sidelobes change, MV PSL −42 → −20 dB).

A time test separates the two. In every mode the noise is a new realization in each frame, while the speckle of stationary tissue is the same from frame to frame. Press "New noise frame" at +10 dB: the background grain changes while the point targets, the cyst and much of the speckle pattern stay put. On a scanner, hold the probe still on a phantom and compare consecutive frames, or average N frames: noise power falls by N, stationary speckle does not.

The dark streak beside the bright scatterer in ESBMV

The ESBMV image shows a black horizontal band at the depth of the bright scatterer (4.2, 30.5) mm, reaching from the cyst rim to the scatterer. The full analysis: eigenvalues, numbers versus δ, and remedies

The ESBMV image shows a black horizontal band at the depth of the bright scatterer (4.2, 30.5) mm, reaching from the cyst rim to the scatterer, with a thin bright tail around the scatterer itself. It is not a numerical artefact: recomputing those pixels with a full 32 × 32 eigendecomposition instead of the warm-started subspace iteration gives the same output to 0.1 dB. It is what (25.5) does when one bright off-axis scatterer dominates the local covariance. Within about 2.4 mm of a scatterer that stands some 30 dB above the surrounding speckle in the DAS image, its wavefront (a phase ramp across the subarray) is the largest eigenvector by a wide margin (\(\lambda_2/\lambda_1 \approx 0.1\) at z = 30.5 mm), so with δ = 0.5 the signal subspace \(\mathbf{E}_s\) holds that one vector only. That vector is nearly orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{1}\), and the projection drops the distortionless constraint: \(\lvert\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ESB}}^H\mathbf{a}\rvert \le 0.002\) (−54 dB) across the band. The local speckle, which arrives along \(\mathbf{a}\), is erased; what passes is the scatterer itself, scaled by MV's imperfect null on it, which forms the tail. The band is as tall as the pulse plus the ±K temporal average.

In the default run (band x = 1.8–3.2 mm, z = 30.35–30.8 mm), ESBMV sits 51.8 dB below DAS there against 9.2 dB below DAS in the background speckle ROI; MV in the same band sits only 6.3 dB below DAS. Lowering δ shrinks the hole: −47.6, −35.7, −19.9 and −11.5 dB re DAS at δ = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05 and 0.01. The band touches the rim of the 2 mm cyst ROI, so it also adds a little to ESBMV's CR. Remedies that keep the eigen-idea: a lower or noise-referenced threshold (count eigenvalues above the estimated noise floor rather than above δλ₁), or a subspace that must retain the steering direction; the second is our suggestion and was not run here.

Equal tuning: one rule for every method

Each method has one or two knobs, and the published images are usually at the best setting for the claim being made; the sweep panel of Demo 25.4 recomputes the metrics on the ROI and profile pixels for each value with everything else at default. Defaults are open to the objection that a method was left untuned, so every method is also tuned by one rule, the same for all: maximize gCNR subject to point FWHM ≤ 1.1 × DAS FWHM, at −20 dB channel noise and no phase screen. gCNR is the objective because the display cannot move it (display view of Demo 25.5); the FWHM ceiling stops a method from buying contrast with resolution. The "Settings" selector of Demo 25.4 runs either set, and the methods cards and the summary table give the tuned values.

Key takeaways

25.9How experts think about it

An expert reduces every method to what it does with one aperture vector: re-weight it (MV, ESBMV), score its constancy and scale the DAS output (CF family), combine it nonlinearly (DMAS), report its normalized correlation (SLSC), or clean it before summing (FXPF, ADMIRE). Each choice has a predictable signature in four numbers: the width of a lone point (often an SNR measurement), the valley between two points (actual resolution), gCNR (actual separability) and speckle SNR (what the weights did to tissue). Then they ask two questions of any claimed improvement: would a power law on DAS give the same CR, and does it survive a phase screen.

The \(2f_0\) term of pair (i, j) has phase \(\varphi_i + \varphi_j\): a virtual element at \((x_i + x_j)/2\) receiving at λ/2 with triangular multiplicity. A triangle at λ/2 has −26.5 dB sidelobes and 0.74 times the −6 dB width of a uniform aperture at λ; equivalently, the narrowband DMAS beam is the DAS beam squared (25.10), so for a lone point the gain equals a p = 2 power law. On broadband plane-wave data the measured gains are smaller (FWHM 0.43 vs 0.48 mm) because the transmit PSF is common and the signed square root mixes harmonics. The identity turns 2016 pair products into 64 square roots.

For an aligned echo every pair term is \(\operatorname{sign}(\mathrm{rf}^2)\sqrt{\mathrm{rf}^2} = \lvert\mathrm{rf}(t)\rvert\), a full-wave rectified pulse: a large DC (envelope) component, a band at \(2f_0\) (energy 0.22 of the DC band) and weaker even harmonics. The products need RF sampled well above \(4f_0\) so that nothing folds into the pass band: we form them on an 18.75 µm (≈ λ/16) depth grid, 41 MHz equivalent.

25.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Derive the MV weights. When is MV identical to DAS? · screen

Minimize \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}\) subject to \(\mathbf{w}^H\mathbf{a} = 1\). The Lagrangian gradient gives \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w} \propto \mathbf{a}\), and the constraint fixes the scale: \(\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a}/(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a})\), output power \(1/(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{a})\) (25.2). After the delays \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{1}\), so with \(\mathbf{R} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\) (white noise) the weights are 1/N: DAS. The same holds for L = 1 (scalar covariance) and for Δ → ∞ (then the smoothed MV is DAS with a trapezoidal taper). Follow-up: "What does the steering vector assume?" Correct delays: a sound-speed error makes \(\mathbf{a}\) wrong and MV suppresses the signal itself.

I2Why does textbook MVDR fail on ultrasound echoes, and what are the three standard fixes? · onsite

(1) The interfering echoes are coherent copies of the same transmit pulse, so MV uses them to cancel the desired signal: with an equal-amplitude echo 1.5 mm away the gain is −66 dB at L = N. Forward subarray smoothing (\(L \le N/2\)) decorrelates them through the factor \(D_P(\psi)\); at L = 48 the gain is back to −0.3 dB. (2) One time sample is one snapshot: averaging ±K samples over about a pulse length stabilizes \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) (speckle SNR 1.02 → 1.53 in the lab). (3) Model errors and noise: diagonal loading \(\varepsilon = \Delta\cdot\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{R}})/L\) with \(\Delta \approx 10^{-2}\) to \(10^{-1}\) (25.3). Follow-up: "What is the cost of smoothing?" The adaptive aperture shrinks to L elements, so resolution and null depth drop.

I3A paper shows an MV point 0.03 mm wide against 0.48 mm for DAS. Is the resolution 16 times better? · onsite

No. For a lone point in white noise the MV width is \(0.914/\sqrt{N\cdot\mathrm{SNR}}\) times the DAS width: it measures SNR (capped by loading near 1/Δ), not the ability to separate two scatterers. The honest test is two points closer than the DAS width: in the lab, a 0.5 mm pair shows a −12 dB dip with MV against −1.3 dB for DAS, which is a real gain, but nowhere near 16×. Also ask about lateral sampling: an MV peak narrower than the pixel pitch reads −23 dB half a pixel off the grid. Follow-up: "What would you ask the authors to show?" Pair dip versus separation, loading and subarray sweeps, and gCNR.

I4What CF do you expect for a point, for speckle and for noise? Why is our speckle CF 0.23 and not 2/3? · screen

Point at focus: 1. Noise: 1/N (0.0156 for 64 elements). Speckle: the ratio of means is \(\sum(N - \lvert m\rvert)C(m)/N^2\) with C the VCZ coherence. A fully focused transmit with the full receive aperture gives \(C(m) = 1 - \lvert m\rvert/N\) and CF ≈ 2/3. Nine plane waves at 1° synthesize only a 4.7 mm transmit aperture at 30 mm, so C falls to zero at \(m_{\mathrm{tx}} = 15.7\) lags and the expected CF is 0.225 (simulated 0.23). Follow-up: "So what does CF do to the speckle brightness?" It lowers speckle by about 13 dB and noise by 36 dB here, a 23 dB stretch.

I5How are CF and the Wiener postfilter related? · onsite

Write \((1/N)\sum\lvert s_i\rvert^2 = \lvert\bar{y}\rvert^2 + \hat{\sigma}^2\). Then \(\mathrm{CF} = \lvert\bar{y}\rvert^2/(\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^2 + \hat{\sigma}^2)\): signal over signal plus channel noise. The MMSE gain for the DAS output needs the output noise \(\hat{\sigma}^2/N\), so \(H = N\cdot\mathrm{CF}/((N - 1)\mathrm{CF} + 1)\) (25.6) (Nilsen and Holm, 2010). For noise CF = 1/N (−36 dB) but H ≈ 0.5 (−6 dB). Follow-up: "Is H optimal per pixel?" Only with good estimates; single-sample estimates are noisy, so average over an axial kernel or subarrays.

I6Explain GCF's \(M_0\) physically. How would you choose it? · onsite

\(S(k)\) is the DFT of the aperture vector; bin k is a wave arriving from lateral offset \(\Delta x = k\lambda z/(Np)\), 0.48 mm per bin at 30 mm. GCF keeps \(\lvert k\rvert \le M_0\), so \(M_0\) sets how far from the focus incoming energy still counts as signal. Speckle occupies \(\lvert k\rvert \lesssim N/m_{\mathrm{tx}} \approx 4\) here: \(M_0 = 0\) (CF) keeps 22 % of it, \(M_0 = 1\) keeps 60 %. Choose \(M_0\) small enough to exclude the clutter directions you care about and large enough to pass speckle and residual aberration; the lab finds gCNR flat at 0.975 for \(M_0\) = 1–4. Follow-up: "What does aberration do to \(S(k)\)?" It spreads every bin, so GCF degrades more gently than CF but still degrades.

I7DMAS costs \(N(N - 1)/2\) products per sample. Is that true, and where does its gain come from? · onsite

No: \(\sum_{i<j}\hat{s}_i\hat{s}_j = \frac12[(\sum\hat{s})^2 - \sum\hat{s}^2]\) (25.9), so it is O(N) (64 square roots instead of 2016 products). The \(2f_0\) part of each pair carries \(\varphi_i + \varphi_j\), a virtual element at the mid-point receiving at λ/2: the sum coarray, a triangle. In narrowband terms the beam is the DAS beam squared: −26.5 dB sidelobes and 0.74× width, the same as a p = 2 power law for an isolated point. The signed square root keeps amplitude linearity, which is what separates it from DAS². Follow-up: "Why the band-pass?" The pair sum also has a DC (difference-coarray) part that only reproduces the DAS intensity; it must be removed, and the products need RF sampled well above \(4f_0\).

I8Why is a point target wide in SLSC, and what sets the speckle brightness? · research

SLSC normalizes each channel pair, so it reports coherence, not amplitude. Wherever a point dominates the local noise, even far down its DAS sidelobes, the aperture signal is a coherent wavefront and \(R(m) \approx 1\): the point stays bright over about 1.9 mm here (M = 8). Larger M includes lags where the off-focus wavefront decorrelates, so points narrow (5.3 mm at M = 1 to 0.63 mm at M = 24). In speckle \(R(m)\) follows the VCZ curve, the autocorrelation of the transmit aperture, so \(R_{\mathrm{sl}} \approx \sum(1 - m/m_{\mathrm{tx}}) = 5.7\) for M = 8, and noise multiplies every lag ≥ 1 by SNR/(1 + SNR). Follow-up: "Why does SLSC beat DAS in clutter?" Reverberation and off-axis clutter in a dark region are incoherent there and contribute R ≈ 0 regardless of brightness.

I9A reviewer sees CF with CR 52 dB against DAS 25 dB. What do you say? · onsite

CR on the linear output is not a fair comparison for any [0, 1] weight. The weight adds \(20\log_{10}W\) per pixel and is smallest where the signal is incoherent, which stretches the dynamic range: DAS raised to the power p multiplies a dB-domain contrast by p (the power-ratio CR goes from 24.7 to 46.4 dB at p = 2) and leaves gCNR unchanged (25.15). On the same data CF's gCNR is 0.94 against DAS's 0.96, and its speckle SNR falls to 0.91. Report gCNR (Rindal et al., 2019), and CR or CNR only at a matched displayed dynamic range. Follow-up: "Is there any real CF benefit?" Suppression of incoherent noise and clutter where it is the dominant contrast limit, which gCNR will then show.

I10FXPF and ADMIRE both filter channel data before DAS. What can each remove? · research

FXPF fits a linear predictor across the aperture at each temporal frequency. A focused echo and any off-axis plane wave are predictable, so both survive; what goes is spatially incoherent energy (noise, diffuse reverberation): about −14 dB on white noise at p = 4. Its coefficients shrink at low SNR, so weak echoes lose too (−2 dB at 0 dB channel SNR). ADMIRE fits a dictionary of modelled scatterer signatures, inside and outside the region of interest, with elastic-net regularization and reconstructs only the in-region part, so it can remove coherent off-axis and multipath clutter, at a much higher cost. Follow-up: "Why put them before aberration correction?" They clean the data that the coherence or delay estimators use, without changing the delays.

I11You want to estimate sound speed or a phase screen from coherence. Which coherence measure, and where does MV go in the pipeline? · research

Use a region-averaged measure that falls smoothly and monotonically with the residual delay error: mean CF or GCF over a speckle region, or SLSC (here SLSC's gCNR fell only from 0.99 to 0.87 at 60 ns rms). For a point with phase errors the CF-weighted brightness falls three times as fast in dB as DAS (\(\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^3\)), which makes CF sensitive but also noisy as a per-pixel weight. Maximize over the assumed speed or the phase law, as in Module 14. Apply MV or ESBMV after the correction: they amplify residual delay errors into resolution loss (MV's 0.03 mm point widens to 1.1 mm at 50 ns). Follow-up: "Why not use MV's output power as the focus metric?" It depends on loading and SNR as much as on focus.

I12An ESBMV image shows a black horizontal band next to a bright point target that DAS and MV do not show. Explain and fix. · research

Near the bright scatterer its wavefront dominates the subarray covariance, so with a relative threshold \(\lambda_i \ge \delta\lambda_1\) the signal subspace holds only that eigenvector. It is nearly orthogonal to \(\mathbf{a}\), and the projection \(\mathbf{E}_s\mathbf{E}_s^H\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{MV}}\) drops the unit-gain constraint (\(\lvert\mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{ESB}}^H\mathbf{a}\rvert \le 0.002\) in our band): the local tissue vanishes over the pulse length plus the temporal average, 52 dB below DAS in Demo 25.4. Fixes: a lower δ (−36 dB at δ = 0.1, −11.5 dB at 0.01, at the price of less contrast stretch), or a threshold referenced to the noise floor instead of \(\lambda_1\). Follow-up: "Does it affect the metrics?" Only where the band meets a ROI; here it touches the cyst rim and inflates CR slightly.

I13A colleague adds noise at 0 dB SNR to every channel of a 64-element, 9-angle plane-wave dataset and says the noise "did not work" because the DAS image still looks clean. What do you tell them? · onsite

Per-channel SNR is not image SNR. Independent noise adds incoherently, a power gain of \(64 \times 9 = 576\) (27.6 dB), while speckle adds partly coherently; in Demo 25.4 speckle gains about 41 dB, so 0 dB per channel becomes +12 to +14 dB speckle-to-noise in the image, depending on the speckle realization (+13.9 dB in the displayed run). In this plane-wave dataset what remains also looks like speckle: every pixel is a weighted sum of the same band-limited channel samples, so neighbouring pixels share most of their noise (\(R_n(\mathbf{r}_1,\mathbf{r}_2) \propto \sum_e h_e(\mathbf{r}_1)h_e^{*}(\mathbf{r}_2)\)) and the noise becomes a complex Gaussian field with grains of about the PSF size and a Rayleigh envelope. To check, beamform the noise-only data and compare ROI powers, look in an echo-free region, or compare two frames: noise changes from frame to frame, the speckle of stationary tissue does not. Follow-up: "Thermal noise never interacts with the sound field, so how can it look like speckle?" Speckle-like texture comes from any random complex field passing through a band-limited linear system, not from acoustics; the beamformer is that system. "Would a focused line-by-line scan look the same?" No: each line has its own transmit and recording, so noise is independent from line to line and correlated only over about a pulse length along depth, giving a fine, streaky texture that is nearly salt and pepper laterally. True per-pixel salt and pepper needs noise entering after the band-limited chain (post-detection electronics, quantization, image-domain processing) or a pixel grid that undersamples the PSF.

I14A paper reports that ADMIRE improves cyst contrast by 20 dB over DAS. What do you check before you believe it, and what does ADMIRE actually decide per pixel? · research

Per depth window and frequency bin it fits the delayed aperture vector with modelled wavefronts of scatterers inside a small ROI around the line and outside it, by an elastic net (α = 0.9, \(\lambda = 0.0189\,\mathrm{rms}(\mathbf{y})\) in the released code), and keeps only the ROI part (25.14). It is underdetermined by a factor of hundreds (about 31 000 complex predictors for 64 complex data with the released model space), so sparsity decides. Checks: (1) CR on the linear output is meaningless if the cyst is set to exactly zero; in our full-model run 54 % of the cyst pixels are exactly zero at −20 dB channel noise (CR 36.0 dB against 24.7 dB for DAS, set by the remaining pixels), so ask for gCNR and speckle SNR. (2) Is the comparison at matched dynamic range? (3) Was the model calibrated to the system (the released code carries per-probe wavenumber factors)? (4) What happens to speckle near bright scatterers (λ scales with the window's rms, so weak tissue next to a bright target is shrunk away; this motivated iADMIRE, Schlunk et al. 2021)? (5) Cost: about 0.07 s per fit (26 min per 81 × 161 image with 50 % STFT overlap) in our Node.js run, real time only with GPU and ICA-compressed models (Khan et al. 2021). Follow-up: "Why can it remove off-axis clutter when FXPF cannot?" FXPF keeps anything predictable, and an off-axis plane wave is; ADMIRE has an explicit outer model that claims that wavefront, so it is dropped.

25.13Self-check

Q1A point echo sits in white noise at 0 dB channel SNR, N = 64. What are CF and the Wiener gain H?

With signal power 1 and noise power 1 per channel (0 dB SNR), \(\mathrm{E}\lvert\sum s\rvert^2 \approx N^2 + N\) and \(\mathrm{E}\sum\lvert s\rvert^2 = 2N\), so \(\mathrm{CF} = (N + 1)/(2N) = 0.508\). \(H = N\cdot\mathrm{CF}/((N - 1)\mathrm{CF} + 1)\) = 32.5/33.0 = 0.985. CF costs 5.9 dB of output amplitude, H 0.1 dB.

Q2At z = 30 mm, 5 MHz, 0.3 mm pitch and N = 64, which aperture bin does an echo from 3 mm off-axis occupy? Does GCF with \(M_0 = 2\) reject it?

\(k = Np\,\Delta x/(\lambda z)\) = 64 × 0.3 × 3/(0.308 × 30) = 6.2. Yes: it lies outside \(\lvert k\rvert \le 2\) (most of its energy is in bins 6 and 7).

Q3Fifteen plane waves at 0.5° spacing image a speckle region at 40 mm with a 0.3 mm pitch. Where does speckle coherence reach zero, and what is \(R_{\mathrm{sl}}\) for M = 8?

\(m_{\mathrm{tx}} = N_\theta z\,\Delta(\sin\theta)/p\) = 15 × 40 × 0.00873/0.3 = 17.5 lags. \(R_{\mathrm{sl}} = \sum_{m=1}^{8}(1 - m/17.5)\) = 8 − 36/17.5 = 5.9.

Q4How many complex multiply-adds per pixel does MV need for N = 64, L = 16, K = ±1 (covariance plus a Cholesky solve)?

\((2K + 1)(L(N - L + 1) + L^2) + L^3/6\) = 3 × (16 × 49 + 256) + 683 = 3120 + 683 ≈ 3800, about 60× DAS's 64. With L = 32, K = 2 it is about 15 900.

Q5A phase screen adds independent delays of 30 ns rms at 5 MHz. Estimate the point-brightness loss for DAS and for CF-weighted DAS (N = 64).

\(\sigma_\varphi = 2\pi \times 5\ \mathrm{MHz} \times 30\ \mathrm{ns} = 0.94\) rad. \(\mathrm{E}\lvert\bar{y}\rvert^2 = (1 + 63e^{-0.888})/64 = 0.42\), so DAS loses 3.8 dB (\(-4.34\,\sigma_\varphi^2 = -3.9\) dB in the large-N limit). \(\mathrm{CF}\cdot\lvert\bar{y}\rvert = \lvert\bar{y}\rvert^3\) loses three times as much: about 11.3 dB.

Q6In a DAS image the tissue ROI averages −40 dB and the cyst −65 dB (unclipped), and gCNR = 0.958. You display DAS² instead. What are the dB contrast and gCNR, and what does that say about weighted beamformers?

\(A^2\) doubles every dB value: −80 and −130 dB, a 50 dB contrast instead of 25. Pixel ordering is unchanged, so gCNR = 0.958. (A power-ratio CR scales only approximately: 24.7 → 46.4 dB on the lab image.) Any method whose CR gain over DAS can be reproduced by a power law has shown a dynamic-range change, not better separability.

Q7The phases of a pixel's aperture vector have a standard deviation of 0.9 rad. What is PCF for γ = 1 and γ = 1.5?

\(\sigma_0 = \pi/\sqrt{3} = 1.814\) rad. γ = 1: 1 − 0.9/1.814 = 0.50. γ = 1.5: 1 − 1.35/1.814 = 0.26. In dB on the amplitude image, −6.0 and −11.8 dB.

Q8In Demo 25.4 the channel noise is set to +30 dB re speckle. Estimate the image speckle-to-noise ratio for DAS from the readout at 0 dB (+13.9 dB), and predict what the background ROI looks like.

DAS is linear, so with no phase screen the image SNR follows the channel SNR one for one: \(13.9 - 30 = -16.1\) dB, which the readout confirms. Noise then carries about 40 times the speckle power in the ROI, so the background is essentially beamformed noise: the cyst almost filled (DAS CR 0.2 dB, gCNR 0.21) and, because this plane-wave dataset forms every pixel from the same recordings, speckle-like grain at the PSF scale with a near-Rayleigh envelope (DAS speckle SNR 1.69 in the table), not salt and pepper. Two differences remain visible. In a line-by-line focused scan the same noise would be independent from line to line, fine laterally and streaky along depth (selector, line-by-line). And unlike speckle it changes with every frame: press "New noise frame" and the whole background re-draws, which a stationary tissue pattern would not do.

Q9Demo 25.4's image has 81 lines of 161 rows. With 4-row STFT windows, 3 bins and the released ADMIRE model space (≈ 31 000 complex predictors per fit), how many elastic-net fits does one image need, how many real unknowns and equations does each have, and why can the answer still be unique?

With 4-row windows and 50 % overlap (hop 2 rows) each line is covered by 83 windows (we compute 164 rows, starting one hop above the first row), so 81 × 83 × 3 = 20 169 fits per image (plus 416 profile lines × 2 windows × 3 bins for the resolution metrics); without overlap it would be 41 windows and 9963 fits. Each fit has 2 × 31 000 ≈ 62 000 real unknowns and 2 × 64 = 128 real equations, about 480 times underdetermined. Least squares alone would have infinitely many exact solutions; the elastic net's L2 term \(\lambda(1-\alpha)\lVert\mathbf{\beta}\rVert^2/2\) makes the cost strictly convex, so the minimizer is unique, and the L1 term makes it sparse (tens to a few hundred nonzero coefficients). In our offline run a single image with the full model costs about 26 minutes of CPU time in Node.js (22.5 min for the 81 image lines, 3.5 min for the 416 profile lines; 10.4 CPU-hours for the 24 settings of the noise × aberration view of Demo 25.3), which is why Demo 25.4 shows it precomputed.
