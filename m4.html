Sampling, Fourier & LTI systems

The rigorous DSP under every RF line: what sampling does to a spectrum, why a 5 MHz probe can be sampled at 6.75 MHz, what the DFT actually measures, how windows trade mainlobe for sidelobes, why the pulse is the axial point-spread function and the aperture's Fourier transform is the lateral one. Every later step (IQ, delay estimation, ULM localization, sound-speed and phase-screen estimation) assumes these results.

You will be able to

  • Predict the alias of any tone and the legal bandpass sampling rates for an RF band
  • Separate DFT bin spacing from true frequency resolution, and know what zero padding buys
  • Choose a window from its PSL, −3 dB width, ENBW and scalloping loss
  • Model an RF line as pulse ∗ reflectivity and compute axial resolution from \(f_{0}\) and bandwidth
  • Size FFT convolutions, pick FIR vs IIR, and compute grating-lobe angles
  • Treat the beam as the Fourier transform of the aperture: one-way vs two-way, rect vs Hann sidelobes and widths
  • Read the 2D k-space of an RF image: axial band at \(\pm 2f_{0}/c\), lateral extent from the transmit–receive aperture autoconvolution

In 60 seconds
  1. Sampling replicates the spectrum: Sampling copies the spectrum at every multiple of the sample rate, so the copies stay apart only if \(f_s > 2B\), with \(f_s\) the sample rate and B the signal bandwidth. A tone beyond the limit folds back: 7 MHz sampled at 10 MHz reads as 3 MHz. Doppler aliases by the same rule in pulse-to-pulse time. §04.1
  2. An RF band needs only one Nyquist zone: An echo can be sampled below twice its top frequency if its whole band fits inside one Nyquist zone, a slot half the sample rate wide. Legal rates are narrow: 6.5–7.0 MHz for a 3.5–6.5 MHz echo. Systems keep a guard band and record the zone, since even-numbered zones arrive spectrally inverted. §04.2
  3. Resolution is set by observation length: Two tones closer than about 1/T, with T the observation time, merge however much the record is zero-padded. For Doppler, 16 pulses at a pulse repetition frequency of 4 kHz observe 4 ms and resolve about 250 Hz. A shorter range gate buys depth resolution and loses spectral detail. §04.3
  4. A window trades main-lobe width for sidelobe level: A window tapers the record to suppress sidelobes, leakage far from a tone, at the cost of a wider main lobe. Hann gives −31.5 dB sidelobes at 1.44 bins width, against −13.3 dB at 0.89 bins untapered. Low sidelobes reveal a weak tone beside a strong one. §04.4
  5. Axial resolution depends on bandwidth alone: An RF line is the pulse convolved with tissue reflectivity, so depth resolution follows pulse length, which follows bandwidth. Resolution \(\Delta z = c\tau/2\), with c the sound speed and τ the pulse duration, is 0.227 mm for 3 MHz of −6 dB bandwidth at 1540 m/s, at any centre frequency. §04.5
  6. The array samples space: Array elements sample the wavefront, and coarse spatial sampling creates grating lobes, false beams at other angles. Steering to ±θ without them needs element pitch \(d < \lambda/(1 + \sin\theta)\), with λ the wavelength; d ≤ λ/2 is safe at every angle. Check pitch at the highest frequency and widest steering angle in use. §04.7
  7. Focus is a Fourier transform: At the focus the beam profile is the Fourier transform of the aperture weights. Width scales with F#, focal depth over aperture width: a uniform aperture gives a two-way −6 dB width of 0.886 λF#, λ being the wavelength. Depth of field grows as F#², so halving F# sharpens focus but cuts usable depth fourfold. §04.8
From the field
  • Sample to the bandwidth, not to the carrier. Only the occupied band carries information, and data rate times channels and frames drives link, memory and compute cost. A 3–7 MHz band needs 14 MHz sampling as real RF, but only 4 MHz each for I and Q, the two baseband channels after demodulation. §04.2
  • Check element pitch at the band top and widest steering angle. Grating lobes, false beams from coarse spacing, appear once pitch exceeds λ/(1 + sin θ), with λ the wavelength and θ the steering angle. A 0.3 mm pitch is clean unsteered to 5.1 MHz but steerable to 20° only to 3.8 MHz. §04.7
  • Know the Nyquist zone and spectral orientation of bandpass-sampled data before delay processing. Sampling below twice the top frequency folds the band to baseband, inverted in even zones; the wrong zone gives the wrong carrier phase per delay and the wrong flow sign. A 3.5–6.5 MHz echo has a legal window of only 6.5–7.0 MHz. §04.2
  • Interpolate beamforming delays; do not round them to the sample clock. Rounded delays act as random phase errors that raise a diffuse sidelobe floor. With a 40 MHz clock at 5 MHz and 128 elements that floor sits near −34 dB; fourfold interpolation lowers it to about −46 dB. §04.12
  • Apply the identical filter on every channel, and correct for the window’s amplitude gain. A filter delay that differs between channels mimics tissue aberration. Hann’s coherent gain, its average weight, is 0.5, so forgetting it is a 6 dB error in a backscatter coefficient. §04.4 §04.6

If \(f_{0} - k f_{s} < 0\), the positive-frequency content arrives from the negative-frequency image: the spectrum is mirrored and the phase sign flips, \(\cos (2\pi f_{0}t + \varphi )\) \(\to \cos (2\pi f_{\mathrm{alias}}t - \varphi )\).

The sinc sum is not an approximation to the alias: for an infinite sample record it equals it exactly. In a numerical check (40 001 samples, \(f_{0} = 7\) MHz, \(f_{s} = 10\) MHz) the sinc sum and cos(2π·3 MHz·t − \(\varphi\)) agree to within \(2\times 10^{-5}\) near the record center, which is the truncation error. Key takeaways
  • Sampling copies X(f) at every multiple of fs; the copies stay apart only if fs > 2B, strictly (at f0 = fs/2 a sine samples to all zeros).
  • A tone appears at falias = |f0 − fs·round(f0/fs)|: 7 MHz at fs = 10 MHz reads as 3 MHz, mirrored, with the phase sign flipped.
  • Sinc interpolation of an infinite record reproduces the alias exactly; the samples cannot tell the two waves apart.
  • Doppler obeys the same rule in slow time: vNyq = c·PRF/(4f0) = 0.385 m/s at 5 MHz and PRF 5 kHz.

04.2Bandpass sampling for RF ultrasound

Nyquist needs \(f_{s} > 2B\) for a signal confined to \(|f| < B\). An RF echo is not low-pass: it occupies \([f_{L}, f_{H}]\) and its mirror \([-f_{H}, -f_{L}]\). Replication only fails if a copy of one band lands on the other. The band can therefore be sampled below \(2f_{H}\) as long as it fits entirely inside one Nyquist zone \([(n-1)f_{s}/2, n f_{s}/2]\):

\[ (n - 1)\,\frac{f_s}{2} \le f_L \qquad \text{and} \qquad f_H \le n\,\frac{f_s}{2} \]

\(n = 1\) is ordinary Nyquist \((f_{s} \ge 2f_{H})\). The largest n gives the lowest legal rate, approaching 2B only when \(f_{H}\) is an integer multiple of B. For even n, the band arrives spectrally inverted.

Worked example with the band defined at −6 dB: \(f_{0} = 5\) MHz, 60 % fractional bandwidth gives [3.5, 6.5] MHz, \(B = 3\) MHz, \(\lfloor 6.5/3\rfloor = 2\). The legal zones are \(f_{s} \ge 13\) MHz \((n = 1)\) and \(6.5 \le f_{s} \le 7.0\) MHz \((n = 2)\). The second zone is only 0.5 MHz wide. Any clock drift or underestimated bandwidth folds the band onto itself, which is why practical systems leave a guard band.

Quadrature \((4f_{0})\) sampling. At \(f_{s} = 4f_{0}\), consecutive samples of \(\cos (2\pi f_{0}t + \varphi (t))\) fall at carrier phases \(0, \pi /2, \pi ,\ 3\pi /2\), giving I, −Q, −I, Q (sign convention aside). Pairs of samples are already the in-phase and quadrature components, a quarter period apart. Undersampled variants \((f_{s} = 4f_{0}/(2m+1))\) advance the carrier phase by \((2m+1)\cdot \pi /2\) per sample, so they keep the quarter-period interleaving; for odd m the step is \(-\pi /2\) modulo \(2\pi\) and the sign of Q flips (spectral inversion). Module 08 derives the analytic signal and makes this exact.

04.3The DFT as a measurement: record length, bins and resolution

Recap from Module 02. The DFT \(X[k] = \sum_{n} x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\) correlates the record with N rotating probes (Section 02.2, (02.4)). The probes are orthogonal because the roots of unity sum to zero (Section 02.3, (02.5)), so the inverse (02.13) rebuilds x[n], \(\mathbf{F}/\sqrt{N}\) is unitary and Parseval (02.14) holds. Bins above N/2 are negative frequencies (Section 02.5), and the FFT computes all N bins in \((N/2)\log_2 N\) multiplies (Section 02.8). Those derivations are not repeated here. This section treats the DFT as a measurement of a sampled RF or Doppler record and asks what a record of finite length can resolve.

What the DFT samples. X[k] equals the DTFT \(X(e^{j\omega})\) at \(\omega _{k} = 2\pi k/N\), i.e. at frequencies \(f_{k} = k f_{s}/N\). The record length \(T = N/f_{s}\) fixes what can be resolved. Observing N samples multiplies the signal by a rectangular window, whose DTFT is the Dirichlet kernel \(\lvert W_R(e^{j\omega})\rvert = \lvert \sin(N\omega/2)/\sin(\omega/2)\rvert\) (worked for an off-bin tone in Section 02.7, (02.15)). Its first nulls sit at \(\omega = \pm 2\pi/N\), i.e. \(\pm f_{s}/N = \pm 1/T\): two tones closer than ≈ 1/T merge into one lobe.

04.4Windowing and spectral leakage

Multiplying the record by a window w[n] convolves the spectrum with \(W(e^{j\omega})\): the windowing theorem, (03.20) in Section 03.6, whose by-hand leakage example and Hann window are in Section 02.7. A pure tone becomes a copy of W centered on its frequency: a mainlobe plus sidelobes that leak energy into every other bin. Intuition: this is apodization

The array factor of a linear array is the DTFT of the apodization weights, evaluated at \(u = (d/\lambda )(\sin \theta - \sin \theta _{0})\). A rectangular receive aperture is the rectangular window: −13.3 dB first sidelobes, narrowest mainlobe. Hann apodization lowers the sidelobes to −31.5 dB and widens the mainlobe by 1.44/0.89 ≈ 1.6×. That is the same tradeoff you already make between lateral resolution and off-axis clutter, with identical numbers. Sections 04.7 and 04.8 make the mapping explicit.

Pitfall

Windowing changes amplitudes. Divide by CG to read tone amplitudes, and by ENBW when converting a noise floor to a per-Hz density. Forgetting the factor 2 (Hann \(\mathrm{CG} = 0.5\)) is a 6 dB error in a backscatter coefficient.

Key takeaways

  • Pick low sidelobes for a weak tone near a strong one, a narrow mainlobe for close tones of equal level.
  • Divide by CG to read amplitudes and by ENBW for a noise density; forgetting Hann's CG = 0.5 is a 6 dB error in a backscatter coefficient.

04.5LTI systems: the RF line is pulse ∗ reflectivity

Recap from Module 03. An LTI system is fully described by its impulse response h, and its output is the convolution \(y = h \ast x\) (03.4). Intuition

ULM exists because of this section. 04.6FFT filtering of RF data, and the filters on the signal chain

The DFT diagonalizes circular convolution, not linear convolution: the N-point inverse of \(X[k]H[k]\) is the linear result aliased in time with period N ((03.8), Section 03.3). It is the dual of 04.1: sampling the spectrum at N points replicates the time signal with period N, exactly as sampling time at \(f_s\) replicates the spectrum.

With x of length L and h of length M, the linear result has length \(L + M - 1\). The circular result equals it only if no replica overlaps, so \(N \ge L + M - 1\). For long RF lines, overlap-add or overlap-save applies the same rule block by block.

Plane waves sample the transmit k-space. A plane wave at angle \(\theta\) has wave vector \((\sin \theta , \cos \theta )/\lambda\); an echo received along direction \(\varphi\) adds \((\sin \varphi , \cos \varphi )/\lambda\). At 5 MHz, a 1° step gives 17.6 mm, so too coarse an angle set brings back grating-lobe-like artifacts from the side. Pitfall

Every number in the table assumes narrowband, identical transmit and receive apertures and the same focus. A real B-mode line uses a fixed transmit focus, dynamic receive focus and an expanding receive aperture, so the two-way pattern is the product of two different patterns: levels add in dB angle by angle, not peak by peak. A rect receive with a Hann transmit has its first two-way sidelobe at −24.8 dB, not −13.3 − 31.5 = −44.8 dB, because the wide Hann mainlobe still covers the rect's first sidelobe. Outside the transmit focal zone the transmit pattern is not a sinc at all.

Ultrasound link

Aberration correction and sound-speed estimation live in this picture. A wrong sound speed adds a residual quadratic phase across the aperture, i.e. a defocus: the focal-plane pattern is no longer \(A(x/(\lambda F))\), its nulls fill and its mainlobe widens, exactly like the Fresnel term above. A phase screen multiplies \(a(\xi )\) by \(e^{j\varphi (\xi )}\), so the PSF becomes the transform of \(a(\xi )e^{j\varphi (\xi )}\). Coherence-based estimators (Module 07) rest on the same transform: by the van Cittert–Zernike theorem, the spatial coherence of echoes from diffuse scatterers across the receive aperture is the Fourier transform of the transmit beam intensity at the focus, i.e. the autocorrelation of the (possibly aberrated) transmit aperture function.

  • A 60 % band fills the rect two-way first null to −24.0 dB; different Tx and Rx patterns multiply angle by angle (rect receive with Hann transmit: −24.8 dB, not −44.8 dB).
  • In k-space the axial support is kz = 2f/c (6.49 cycles/mm at 5 MHz) and the lateral support |kx| ≤ kz/(2F#).

04.10How experts think about it

Experts carry one picture: every linear stage of the imaging chain is a multiplication in some Fourier domain. The pulse multiplies the reflectivity spectrum in \(k_{z}\), the aperture multiplies it in \(k_{x}\), sampling replicates spectra, and a finite record or aperture convolves them with a sinc-like kernel. Resolution, sidelobes, grating lobes, aliasing and wrap-around are the same few facts seen in time, frequency, space and angle.

The focal-plane beam is the Fourier transform of the aperture, and focusing is what makes that true at any depth, not just in a far field that tissue never reaches.

04.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why is the beam pattern the Fourier transform of the aperture? · onsite

Each aperture point radiates a spherical wave; in the paraxial approximation the path length from \(\xi\) to (x, z) is \(z + (x - \xi )^{2}/(2z)\), so the field is \(\int a(\xi ) e^{j\pi \xi ^{2}/(\lambda z)} e^{-j2\pi \xi x/(\lambda z)} d\xi\) times a phase. When the quadratic term is negligible (\(z \ge 2D^{2}/\lambda\), the Fraunhofer region) it is the FT of \(a(\xi )\) at \(x/(\lambda z) = \sin \theta /\lambda\). Focusing applies \(e^{-j\pi \xi ^{2}/(\lambda F)}\), which cancels the quadratic phase at \(z = F\), so the focal plane is a Fraunhofer plane for any F. Rect aperture → sinc with first null at \(\lambda F\#\).

Follow-up: what happens away from focus? The quadratic phase returns (defocus), nulls fill and the mainlobe widens; the depth of field is ≈ \(7 \lambda F\#^{2}\).

I2Why do two-way sidelobes double in dB, and when is that not true? · screen

By reciprocity the receive sensitivity pattern equals the transmit pattern of the same aperture, and the pulse-echo response is their product. With identical apertures and focus the amplitude is squared, so 20 log of the square doubles every level: −13.26 → −26.52 dB for rect, −31.5 → −63 dB for Hann. It fails when Tx and Rx differ (fixed transmit focus with dynamic receive, different apodizations or aperture sizes): levels add in dB angle by angle, and a rect receive with Hann transmit has its first sidelobe at −24.8 dB. It also assumes narrowband; a broadband pulse fills the nulls.

Follow-up: what is the two-way −6 dB width? \(0.886 \lambda F\#\), equal to the one-way −3 dB width.

I3Where do grating lobes appear for pitch = \(\lambda\) at 20° steering? · screen

\(\sin \theta _{g} = \sin \theta _{0} \pm \lambda /d\) \(= 0.342 \pm 1\). The + sign gives 1.342 (invisible); the − sign gives \(-0.658, \theta _{g}\) = −41.1°, at full height for point elements. With element width \(0.9\lambda\), the element factor puts it at −4.4 dB one-way, −8.7 dB two-way relative to the main lobe. To remove it for \(\pm 20^{\circ}: d\) < λ/(1 + sin 20°) = \(0.745\lambda\) (0.230 mm at 5 MHz).

Follow-up: what does a broadband pulse do to it? The grating-lobe angle depends on \(\lambda\), so the lobe spreads over angles and its peak drops, but its energy stays.

I4What sets axial versus lateral resolution? · screen

Axial: the pulse length, i.e. bandwidth: \(\Delta z = c\tau /2\) with \(\tau \approx 0.883/B\) (Gaussian, −6 dB). 5 MHz, 60 %: \(B = 3\) MHz, \(\Delta z = 0.227\) mm, independent of \(f_{0}\). Lateral: diffraction, i.e. aperture and wavelength: −6 dB two-way width \(0.886 \lambda F\# = 0.546\) mm at 5 MHz, F# 2. Raising \(f_{0}\) at fixed bandwidth improves lateral but not axial; widening the band improves axial only; elevation is set by the lens and the elevation aperture.

Follow-up: why does ULM beat both? It localizes isolated sources, which is estimation (precision scales with SNR and PSF width), not resolution.

I5A 7 MHz tone is sampled at 10 MHz. What do you see? · screen

\(k = \operatorname{round} (7/10)\) \(= 1, f_{0} - k f_{s} = -3\) MHz, so a 3 MHz tone. The negative sign means it comes from the −7 MHz image: the spectrum is mirrored and the phase conjugated, \(\cos (2\pi \cdot 7t + \varphi )\) \(\equiv \cos (2\pi \cdot 3t - \varphi )\) at the sample instants. The samples cannot distinguish them; only an anti-alias filter before the ADC can.

Follow-up: the Doppler analogue? \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = c\cdot \mathrm{PRF}/(4f_{0})\); flow faster than that wraps to the opposite sign.

I6Why does zero padding not improve frequency resolution? · screen

Resolution is the width of the kernel the spectrum is convolved with, set by the record length T (\(\approx 1/T\) for rect, 1.44/T −3 dB for Hann). Zero padding appends samples that carry no information, so the DTFT of the padded record is identical; the DFT just samples it more densely (\(f_{s}/(PN)\) instead of \(f_{s}/N\)). Two equal, in-phase tones 0.6/T apart stay one lobe at any padding (other relative phases can split them, Demo 04.4); a 4× longer record separates them.

Follow-up: what does padding buy? Interpolated peak location, less scalloping in display, and the length needed to avoid circular wrap in FFT convolution.

I7What bandpass sampling rate works for a 3–7 MHz band? · onsite

\(B = 4\) MHz, \(\lfloor f_{H}/B\rfloor = \lfloor 7/4\rfloor = 1\), so only the ordinary Nyquist zone exists: \(f_{s} \ge 14\) MHz. Undersampling needs \(f_{L} \ge B\) (fractional bandwidth ≤ 2/3); this band is 80 %. Alternatives: demodulate to baseband IQ and sample I and Q at ≥ 4 MHz each, or narrow the band with a filter first. For [3.5, 6.5] MHz, \(6.5 \le f_{s} \le 7.0\) MHz works, spectrally inverted \((n = 2)\), with only 0.5 MHz of margin.

Follow-up: why \(4f_{0}?\) Consecutive samples fall at carrier phases \(0, \pi /2, \pi ,\ 3\pi /2\) and give I, −Q, −I, Q directly.

I8You filter RF data by multiplying FFTs and see artifacts at the start of each line. Why? · screen

The DFT diagonalizes circular convolution. With an N-point FFT, the output is the linear result folded with period N; if \(N < L + M - 1\), the filter tail past sample N wraps onto the first samples, so late echoes contaminate shallow depths. Fix: \(N \ge L + M - 1\) \((2048 + 63 - 1 = 2110 \to 4096)\), or overlap-add/save. In 2D image filtering the same thing makes edges bleed across the image; pad both dimensions.

Follow-up: and the 63-tap filter's delay? 31 samples = 0.775 µs = 0.60 mm at 40 MHz; compensate it identically on every channel.

I9How does F-number trade lateral resolution against depth of field? · onsite

Lateral −6 dB width (two-way) ≈ \(0.886 \lambda F\#\), linear in F#; depth of field ≈ \(7 \lambda F\#^{2}\), quadratic. At 5 MHz: F# 2 gives 0.55 mm and 8.7 mm; F# 4 gives 1.09 mm and 35 mm. Halving F# doubles resolution but cuts depth of field by 4, which is why systems use multiple transmit foci or dynamic receive focusing with an expanding aperture (constant receive F#).

Follow-up: what limits the smallest F#? Element directivity (acceptance angle) and the number of channels; below \(F\# \approx 1\) the paraxial formulas also degrade.

I10Sketch the 2D spectrum of an RF image and explain how plane-wave compounding fills it. · research

Two lobes at \(k_{z} = \pm 2f/c\) (\(\pm 6.49\) cycles/mm at 5 MHz, width set by the pulse band) forming a bow-tie: at each frequency \(|k_{x}| \le D/(\lambda z) = 1/(\lambda F\#)\), the convolution of Tx and Rx aperture spectra. A single plane wave has no transmit aperture, so the lateral extent comes from receive only; each angle \(\theta\) shifts it by \(\sin \theta /\lambda\). Compounding angles lays these regions side by side and synthesizes a transmit aperture. A uniform step \(\Delta (\sin \theta )\) samples that synthetic aperture, so the focus is replicated every \(\lambda /\Delta (\sin \theta )\) laterally (17.6 mm for 1° at 5 MHz).

Follow-up: how does a sound-speed error show up? The k-space mapping \(k_{z} = 2f/c\) is scaled and the regions from different angles no longer add coherently: defocus, which is what coherence- and phase-based sound-speed estimators detect.

I11For a QUS power-spectrum estimate from a 3 mm gate, which window and which corrections? · onsite

The gate is 3.9 µs, so spectral resolution is ≈ 0.26 MHz with rect and ≈ 0.37 MHz (−3 dB) with Hann. Backscatter spectra are smooth and span tens of dB, so Hann is the usual choice: its −31.5 dB sidelobes and −18 dB/octave roll-off limit leakage from the passband peak into the band edges. Corrections: divide by \(\mathrm{CG}^{2}\) (Hann 0.25) for coherent levels, or by \(\sum w^{2} (\mathrm{ENBW})\) for noise-like power; the same window on the sample and the reference phantom cancels most of it in the ratio. Average over independent gates to reduce the variance of the estimate.

Follow-up: longer gate? Better spectral resolution and lower variance, worse depth resolution, and attenuation within the gate biases the estimate.

I12Your simulated narrowband beam shows deep nulls, the measured beam does not. Why? · research

A real pulse contains many frequencies, and each puts its nulls at \(x \propto 1/f\), so the energy pattern has none: with a 60 % band the rect two-way first null fills to −24 dB while the sidelobe level drops from −26.5 to −28.8 dB. Other contributors: element-to-element gain and delay errors (random phase errors of variance \(\sigma ^{2} \mathrm{rad}^{2}\) add a diffuse sidelobe floor of about \(\sigma ^{2}/N\) relative to the peak, one-way, uniform weights), quantized delays, noise, and sound-speed or aberration-induced defocus. Compare against a broadband simulation before blaming hardware.

Follow-up: how would you measure the beam? Scan a wire or small sphere laterally in a water tank in the focal plane and record the peak or energy of the echo versus offset.

04.14Self-check

Q1A 7 MHz tone is sampled at 10 MHz. What frequency do the samples represent, and is anything else changed?

\(k = \operatorname{round} (7/10) = 1\), so \(f_{0} - k f_{s} = -3\) MHz and \(f_{\mathrm{alias}} = 3\) MHz. The negative sign means the content comes from the −7 MHz image: the spectrum is mirrored and the phase is conjugated, so \(\cos (2\pi \cdot 7t + \varphi )\) samples identically to \(\cos (2\pi \cdot 3t - \varphi )\). Demo 04.1 confirms that the FFT peak sits at 3.000 MHz.

Q2Which sampling rates are legal for an RF band spanning 3–7 MHz? Compare with a 5 MHz, 60 % band.

\(B = 4\) MHz and \(\lfloor f_{H}/B\rfloor = \lfloor 7/4\rfloor = 1\), so only \(n = 1\) exists: \(f_{s} \ge 14\) MHz. An 80 % fractional band cannot be undersampled. For [3.5, 6.5] MHz, \(B = 3\) and \(\lfloor 6.5/3\rfloor = 2\), so \(f_{s} \ge 13\) MHz or \(6.5 \le f_{s} \le 7.0\) MHz. The second window is narrow, and the band arrives inverted there (n even).

Q3\(N = 1024\) samples at 40 MHz. What are the bin spacing, the observation time and the frequency resolution? What changes with 4× zero padding?

Bin spacing \(f_{s}/N = 39.06\) kHz, \(T = 25.6\) µs, rectangular-window resolution ≈ \(1/T = 39.06\) kHz (Hann −3 dB width 1.44 bins = 56.3 kHz). Padding to 4096 gives 9.77 kHz bins on the same DTFT. Resolution is unchanged, and only peak interpolation improves.

Q4What is the −6 dB axial resolution of a 5 MHz Gaussian pulse with 60 % fractional bandwidth? What happens at 7.5 MHz with the same 3 MHz absolute bandwidth?

\(B = 0.6 \times 5 = 3\) MHz, \(\tau = 4 \ln 2/(\pi B) = 0.883/3\) MHz = 0.294 µs, \(\Delta z = c\tau /2 = 1.540 \times 0.294/2\) \(= 0.227\) mm. At 7.5 MHz with B still 3 MHz, \(\tau\) and \(\Delta z\) are identical: axial resolution depends on bandwidth, not center frequency. The higher \(f_{0}\) only adds more carrier cycles under the same envelope, which helps phase-based delay estimation (Module 11), not resolution.

Q5A second tone at −40 dB sits 6.5 bins from a strong tone. Is it visible with a rectangular window? With Hann?

The rectangular sidelobe envelope falls as ≈ \(1/(\pi \cdot \Delta k)\), and near 6.5 bins the sidelobes peak at −26 dB (\(1/(64 \sin (6.5\pi /64))\) for \(N = 64\)), so a −40 dB tone is buried. Hann sidelobes there are ≈ −58 dB, so the tone stands about 18–19 dB above the leakage. Demo 04.5 reproduces this case with its default settings.

Q6You filter a 2048-sample RF line with a 63-tap FIR by FFT. What is the minimum FFT size, and what delay must you remove?

\(N \ge L + M - 1\) \(= 2048 + 63 - 1 = 2110\), so the next power of two is 4096 (or use overlap-save with smaller blocks). A symmetric 63-tap filter has group delay (M−1)/2 = 31 samples. At 40 MHz that is 0.775 µs, or 0.60 mm of depth after the c/2 conversion.

Q7A 0.3 mm pitch array runs at 7.5 MHz. Where are the grating lobes unsteered, and what pitch would allow \(\pm 20^{\circ}\) steering?

\(\lambda = 1.540/7.5 = 0.205\) mm, \(\lambda /d = 0.684\), so \(\sin \theta _{g} = \pm 0.684\) and \(\theta _{g} = \pm 43.2^{\circ}\), inside the visible region. Grating-lobe-free steering to 20° needs d ≤ λ/(1 + sin 20°) = \(0.205/1.342 = 0.153\) mm (about \(\lambda /1.34\)).

Q8Zero padding a 16-pulse Doppler ensemble to 256 points makes the spectrum look smooth. Can it now separate two flows whose Doppler shifts differ by 0.5/T?

No. The ensemble's DTFT is the true spectrum convolved with a kernel of width ≈ 1/T (wider with a window), and padding only samples that DTFT more densely. Two components 0.5/T apart form a single lobe in the DTFT itself for most relative phases. Near 90° (relative phase at the first sample) a dip can appear, but it comes from interference, and the two peaks sit far from the true frequencies (Demo 04.4, phase slider). Padding changes neither case. Separating them takes a longer ensemble, or a parametric or high-resolution estimator with its own model assumptions.
