Inverse problems and regularization

Sound-speed maps, deconvolved RF lines and sparse-recovery beamformers are all solutions of \(\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{n}\) with an operator that throws information away. This module shows where the information goes, how Tikhonov, iteration count, L1 and total variation put prior knowledge back, how to set their strength without the truth, and what the answer can and cannot resolve. It ends with a limited-angle echo-mode sound-speed tomography you can break.

You will be able to

  • Diagnose ill-posedness from the singular spectrum and name the null space of an ultrasound operator.
  • Write Tikhonov in filter-factor form, convert between the λ and λ² conventions, and predict its noise gain and bias.
  • Choose λ by the discrepancy principle, GCV or the L-curve, and say when each fails.
  • Use iteration count, L1 (ISTA/FISTA) and total variation as regularizers and state the bias each introduces.
  • Read a model-resolution matrix and explain the axial smearing of limited-angle echo-mode sound-speed images.

In 60 seconds
  1. Ill-posed inversion: an imaging operator can be invertible yet useless to invert, because some image components barely reach the data. The singular value \(\sigma_i\) measures how strongly component i reaches the data, and noise in that component is multiplied by \(1/\sigma_i\). Averaging frames lowers noise but cannot recover what was never measured; only new data or a prior can. §13.1
  2. Regularization as a filter: Tikhonov regularization fades weak components instead of inverting them. Component i keeps weight \(f_i=\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)\), with \(\sigma_i\) its singular value and λ the chosen regularization strength; the weight is ½ at \(\sigma_i=\lambda\). Noise gain never exceeds \(1/(2\lambda)\), but the image becomes the truth blurred by a known resolution kernel, worth reporting. §13.2
  3. The penalty states the prior: penalizing size pulls toward a reference value, penalizing neighbour-to-neighbour change toward a constant, change of slope toward a straight line. Converting average to local sound speed differentiates, so noise grows with depth: about 71 m/s at 20 mm per 1 m/s error in the average (0.4 mm steps), so regularize more deeply. §13.3
  4. Choosing λ: every rule for setting the regularization strength λ assumes something about the noise. The discrepancy principle needs the noise level, generalized cross-validation (GCV) assumes white noise, and the L-curve needs a visible corner. With strongly correlated noise GCV gave 31 times the best achievable error, so whiten correlated data or measure the noise. §13.4
  5. Iteration count as regularization: iterative solvers recover strong components first and weak, noisy ones later, so stopping early regularizes and running to convergence does not. Conjugate-gradient least squares (CGLS) needs roughly the square root of the steps plain gradient iteration needs. Stop when the residual reaches the noise level, and report the step count with the rule. §13.5
  6. Sparse and edge-preserving priors: penalizing absolute values (the L1 norm) instead of squares sets most coefficients to exactly zero, so it locates the few reflectors but pulls surviving amplitudes toward zero; refit them by least squares. Total variation applies the same idea to the image gradient: edges survive, but small plateaus lose height and ramps return as staircases. §13.6 §13.7
  7. Limited angles leave a gap: echo-mode sound-speed imaging sees each point only through rays within a small angle \(\theta_{\max}\) of vertical, so depth variation is poorly measured and layers are nearly invisible. A prior sharpens only what the data partly constrain: total variation halved the error at \(\theta_{\max}\) = 30° and did nothing at 10°. Widen angles before tuning priors. §13.8
From the field
  • Pick the regularization strength λ by a stated rule; test its assumption. The discrepancy principle needs the noise level, GCV (generalized cross-validation) assumes white noise, the L-curve a corner. With correlated noise GCV gave 31 times the best achievable error, so cross-check two rules or held-out data before fixing λ. §13.4
  • Fix missing angles or bandwidth in the acquisition before changing the regularizer. A penalty cannot restore what the data never sampled. The symptom is depth smearing that no regularization strength cures: in the lab total variation halved the sound-speed error with rays up to 30° from vertical and did nothing at 10°. §13.8
  • Test on data simulated with a finer, different model than the one you invert. Inverting data made on your own grid (the "inverse crime") rewards too little regularization with near-perfect images that fail on real data. The lab simulates with 0.1 mm ray steps through a smooth-edged truth and inverts with 0.4 mm steps. §13.8
  • Choose an iterative solver's stopping rule as carefully as λ. The step count is the regularization: error falls, then rises, so running to convergence removes it. Stop when the residual norm reaches \(\tau\sqrt m\,\sigma_n\) (m data samples, noise standard deviation \(\sigma_n\), τ a little above 1) and report the count with the rule. §13.5
  • Use L1 to find the support, then refit amplitudes by least squares. The L1 penalty shrinks every surviving amplitude toward zero, so amplitudes read off the sparse solution are biased low. Start the penalty weight near the universal threshold \(\sigma_n\|a\|\sqrt{2\ln N}\) (noise level, column norm, N unknowns) and refit on the selected support. §13.6
Convention used in this module

The Tikhonov penalty is written \(\lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\), so the filter factor is \(f_i = \sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2)\) and \(\lambda\) has the units of a singular value (Hansen 2010; Module 06 uses the same form with α). Module 05 wrote the penalty as \(\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\), whose λ is our \(\lambda^2\). Ridge regression, many ML texts and most "diagonal loading" values also quote the squared quantity. Before you copy a regularization value from a paper, find out which one it is: the two differ by a square, which is a factor 100 for λ = 0.01.

Hadamard's three conditions. A problem is well posed if a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data. Discretized imaging problems usually satisfy the first two formally (least squares exists; add a minimum-norm condition for uniqueness) and fail the third in practice: the inverse exists, but its amplification is so large that the answer is noise. The SVD shows it in one line (Module 06):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{naive}} = \mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{y} &= \sum_{i:\sigma_i>0} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{T}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i \\ &= \mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \sum_{i:\sigma_i>0} \frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{T}\mathbf{n}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i \\ \operatorname{std}(\text{noise term } i) &= \sigma_n/\sigma_i \end{aligned} \]

The three operators above fail differently. The pulse has an exact null space (frequencies it never transmits), the running mean has a smooth decay, and the limited-angle tomography operator loses a whole cone of spatial frequencies. Averaging more frames lowers \(\sigma_n\) but cannot recover a null space: only a new measurement (more bandwidth, more angles) or a prior can.

Pitfall

A small residual is not evidence of a good solution. The naive inverse fits the data exactly, noise included. Judge an inversion by its error against a known truth in simulation, by its resolution matrix, and by its stability to a new noise draw.

Key takeaways
  • Discretized imaging problems fail Hadamard's third condition: the inverse exists, but white noise in component i is amplified by 1/σi.
  • Each operator fails differently: the pulse has an exact null space outside its band (|H| below −40 dB outside about 1.1–8.9 MHz), the running mean decays smoothly, limited-angle tomography loses a cone of k-space.
  • Averaging lowers σn but cannot fill a null space; only a new measurement (bandwidth, angles) or a prior can.
  • A small residual is not evidence: the naive inverse fits the noise exactly. Judge by error against a known truth, the resolution matrix and stability to a new noise draw.

13.2Tikhonov regularization: spectral filtering, bias and resolution

Add a penalty on the size of the solution and minimize both:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_\lambda &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 + \lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 \\ &= (\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{y} \\ &= \sum_i f_i\,\frac{\mathbf{u}_i^{T}\mathbf{y}}{\sigma_i}\,\mathbf{v}_i \\ f_i &= \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2} \end{aligned} \]

Equivalent stacked least squares: \(\min \lVert[\mathbf{A};\ \lambda\mathbf{I}]\,\mathbf{x} - [\mathbf{y};\ 0]\rVert\), which you solve by QR without forming \(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}\) (Module 05). \(f_i \approx 1\) for \(\sigma_i \gg \lambda\), \(f_i \approx \sigma_i^2/\lambda^2\) for \(\sigma_i \ll \lambda\), and exactly ½ at \(\sigma_i = \lambda\): λ is a soft singular-value threshold. Truncated SVD is the hard version.

Split the error into what the filter removes from the truth and what it lets through from the noise. With \(\mathbf{A}^{\#} = (\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\) and the model-resolution matrix \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{A}^{\#}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V}\operatorname{diag}(f)\,\mathbf{V}^{T}\):

The first term is the regularization error (a bias: \(\mathrm{E}[\mathbf{x}_\lambda] = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}\), not \(\mathbf{x}\)); the second is the perturbation error. Raising λ trades the second for the first. The cap \(1/(2\lambda)\), reached at \(\sigma_i = \lambda\), is the most any component of the noise can be amplified.

Deconvolution as the circulant case. For a pulse \(h\) and a periodic (or padded) line, the SVD is the DFT and \(\sigma = \lvert H(f)\rvert\). Intuition

Read the filter-factor panel as "which frequencies of the reflectivity do I believe?" Where the pulse is stronger than λ the estimate takes the data at face value; where it is weaker the estimate returns roughly zero. The passband edges where \(\lvert H\rvert = \lambda\) set the new axial resolution, so deconvolution buys resolution only up to the frequency where the pulse sinks to the noise.

Pitfall

"Super-resolved" deconvolution with a quadratic penalty is band widening up to the \(\lvert H\rvert = \lambda\) crossing, never beyond. Recovering detail outside the band needs a prior that extrapolates (sparsity, positivity, piecewise constancy; 13.6–13.7), and then the result is only as right as the prior.

Key takeaways
  • fi = σi²/(σi² + λ²): ≈ 1 for σ ≫ λ, ≈ σ²/λ² for σ ≪ λ, exactly ½ at σ = λ; truncated SVD is the hard version.
  • Tikhonov never amplifies noise by more than 1/(2λ); the price is a bias, E[xλ] = R x, whose rows are the reconstruction's point-spread functions.
  • For deconvolution the SVD is the DFT: Tikhonov is the Wiener filter with λ² = σn²/σx², and the result is the truth convolved with a new pulse whose spectrum is the filter factor.
  • A quadratic penalty widens the band only up to the |H| = λ crossing; check whether a quoted λ is our λ or λ² before copying it (a factor 100 at 0.01).

13.3General-form Tikhonov: priors on shape

Penalize a derivative instead of the size, and the regularizer prefers a shape instead of zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}_\lambda &= \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}}\ \lVert \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 + \lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 \\ &= (\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^{T}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{y} \\ &\text{unique iff } \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{A}) \cap \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{L}) = \{0\} \end{aligned} \]
(13.6)
\(\mathbf{L}\)
penalty operator: \(\mathbf{I}\), first difference \(\mathbf{D}_1\), second difference \(\mathbf{D}_2\), or a 2D gradient
\(\mathcal{N}(\cdot)\)
null space
\(\gamma_i\)

\(\mathbf{x}_\lambda,\ \mathbf{A},\ \mathbf{y},\ \lambda\)
as in (13.3)
\(\{0\}\)

\(\cap\)

\(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I}\) pulls toward \(\mathbf{x} = 0\) (here: toward the reference \(c_0\)); \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{D}_1\) (first difference) leaves constants free and pulls toward flat profiles; \(\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{D}_2\) leaves straight lines free. The generalized SVD of \((\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{L})\) gives filter factors \(\gamma_i^2/(\gamma_i^2 + \lambda^2)\), so everything in 13.2 carries over.

A one-dimensional sound-speed example. Depth-resolved autofocus gives an average sound speed \(\bar c(z)\). In the vertical-path approximation the average is a travel-time average, which is linear in slowness \(s = 1/c\):

When you read an echo-mode sound-speed paper, look for four things: the angle span (it sets the missing cone), what is differenced (it sets the blind spots, such as layers), the regularizer and how its weight was chosen, and whether accuracy is reported for inclusions large compared with the axial resolution. Mean values inside a 10 mm inclusion can be accurate while a 3 mm inclusion is smeared to half its contrast; the resolution row tells you which regime you are in.

Pitfall

The inverse crime: simulating data with the same discretization you invert with. The data then lie exactly in the range of A, and reconstructions look far better than they will on phantoms. Generate data with a finer grid, a different ray model (or a full-wave simulator), and noise with realistic correlation.

Key takeaways
  • The datum is a common-midangle delay difference, linear in slowness: 1 ns/mm ≈ 2.37 m/s at 1540 m/s, and +50 m/s is −20.4 ns/mm.
  • Rays within ±θmax of vertical sample only a k-space wedge around kx: resolution-row axial FWHM 9.4, 4.6, 3.3 mm at 10°, 20°, 30°, lateral about 1.3 mm.
  • Layers are nearly invisible: sensitivity 2(sec θ − 1) ≈ θ², 0.031, 0.128, 0.309 at 10°, 20°, 30°.
  • TV halves the RMSE at 30° (4.8 → 2.4 m/s) and does nothing at 10°: a prior only sharpens what the data partly constrain. Simulate with a finer, different model than you invert.

13.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why is deconvolution of an RF line ill-posed, and what is in its null space? · screen

Convolution with the pulse is diagonal in frequency with singular values \(\lvert H(f)\rvert\). A transducer passes a band around \(f_0\) and nothing at DC or far above, so those frequencies of the reflectivity are (numerically) in the null space: for a 5 MHz, 60 % pulse, \(\lvert H\rvert < 0.01\) outside about 1.1–8.9 MHz. Dividing by H amplifies noise there by \(1/\lvert H\rvert\), over 100 dB at 12 MHz. Tikhonov returns the minimum-norm, band-limited estimate; anything outside the band must come from a prior.

Follow-up: "Then how do sparse deconvolution methods claim higher resolution?" They assume few reflectors; the prior extrapolates the spectrum, and it fails when the assumption does (for example two same-sign reflectors λ/4 apart, which nearly cancel in band).

I2Write the Tikhonov filter factor. A colleague says "we used λ = 0.01": what do you ask? · screen

\(f_i = \sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda^2)\) for the penalty \(\lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\). I ask whether the penalty was \(\lambda\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\) or \(\lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\) (a factor 100 apart at 0.01), whether A was normalized (λ is meaningful only relative to \(\sigma_1\)), what L was, and how λ was chosen. With λ relative to \(\sigma_1\) and the penalty \(\lambda^2\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2\) (this module's convention), components below 1 % of \(\sigma_1\) are suppressed and no noise component is amplified more than \(1/(2\lambda)\) = 50 times.

Follow-up: "Which components are amplified most?" Those with σ = λ, not the smallest ones.

I3How would you choose λ on in-vivo data, and how can each method fail? · onsite

Discrepancy principle if I can measure the noise level (repeat acquisitions, or a noise-only region): \(\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert = \tau\sqrt{m}\,\sigma_n\); it is robust but over-smooths a little and is wrong if \(\sigma_n\) is wrong. GCV needs no \(\sigma_n\) but assumes white noise: with correlated noise (delay estimates correlated over the kernel, speckle-induced errors) it picks a tiny λ; in the RF demo with AR(1) noise its error is 31 times the oracle's. The L-curve corner is a heuristic that can over-smooth for smooth solutions and has no corner at very low noise. I would cross-check two rules, test stability to a new noise draw, and in simulation report the oracle too.

Follow-up: "Can you fix GCV for correlated noise?" Whiten with an estimate of the noise covariance first, then apply GCV to the whitened problem.

I4Your CG reconstruction gets worse after 40 iterations. Is that a bug? · onsite

No, it is semiconvergence. CGLS first fits the large singular components (signal) and then the small ones (noise), so the error has a minimum. The iteration count plays the role of λ: for Landweber with \(\omega = 1/\sigma_1^2\), \(k \approx \ln 2\,(\sigma_1/\lambda)^2\); CGLS gets there in roughly the square root of that (35 versus 1823 in the RF example). Stop with the discrepancy principle on \(\lVert\mathbf{r}_k\rVert\), or add an explicit penalty and iterate to convergence.

Follow-up: "Which is better, early stopping or explicit Tikhonov?" Equivalent in error in this example; explicit penalties make the result independent of the solver and let you compute R, early stopping is cheaper for very large problems.

I5Compare L2 and L1 regularization for point reflectors. Why soft thresholding, why FISTA, and what about the amplitudes? · onsite

L2 spreads energy over all components and returns band-limited blobs; L1 prefers few nonzeros and can resolve reflectors closer than the pulse length if their echoes stay distinguishable in band. Soft thresholding is the proximal map of \(\mu\lvert x\rvert\), so ISTA alternates a gradient step on the data term with thresholding; FISTA adds Nesterov momentum and improves the worst-case gap from \(O(1/k)\) to \(O(1/k^2)\). L1 shrinks surviving amplitudes by about \(\mu/\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2\), so I would debias by least squares on the recovered support before quantifying intensities, and start μ at \(\sigma_n\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\sqrt{2\ln N}\).

Follow-up: "What breaks L1 recovery?" Highly coherent dictionaries (fine grids, band-limited pulses), off-grid sources, and pairs that cancel in band.

I6When would you use TV for a sound-speed map, and what artifacts would you warn a reviewer about? · onsite

When the tissue is approximately piecewise constant (abdominal wall layers, a lesion in a homogeneous organ) and edges matter. TV preserves edges but lowers small plateaus by about \(2\mu/w\) (1D denoising), turns gradients into staircases, and with the anisotropic form favours axis-aligned edges. In the layered example it roughly halves the RMSE compared with the best quadratic penalty; on a ramp it ties \(\mathbf{D}_2\). I would show a ramp phantom and a small-inclusion phantom to expose both artifacts and quote values from plateau interiors.

Follow-up: "How do you solve it?" ADMM with \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}\): one factorization of \(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \rho\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{D}\), then soft thresholding; ρ affects speed, not the minimizer.

I7Give the Bayesian interpretation of Tikhonov and of L1. What does it tell you about λ? · research

With Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma_n^2\), MAP minimizes \(\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2/(2\sigma_n^2) - \log p(\mathbf{x})\). A zero-mean Gaussian prior with variance \(\sigma_x^2\) gives Tikhonov with \(\lambda^2 = \sigma_n^2/\sigma_x^2\), the noise-to-signal ratio, which is the Wiener filter in the circulant case; a Laplace prior of scale β gives L1 with \(\mu = \sigma_n^2/\beta\). So λ should rise when the noise rises and fall when the expected contrast rises. MAP gives a point estimate; the sparsity comes from the mode, not from the prior, whose samples are not sparse.

Follow-up: "How would you get error bars?" From the posterior: for the Gaussian case its covariance is \(\sigma_n^2(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^{T}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\); for non-Gaussian priors, sampling (MCMC) or approximations.

I8Why do echo-mode sound-speed images smear inclusions in depth, and what would you change in the acquisition? · research

Straight rays from each pixel to a linear array lie within \(\pm\theta_{\max}\) of vertical. By the Fourier-slice theorem, their integrals determine only spatial frequencies in a wedge of half-angle \(\theta_{\max}\) around \(k_x\); the cone around \(k_z\) is missing, so the resolution kernel is elongated axially, roughly by \(1/\sin\theta_{\max}\) (9.4 versus 1.2 mm at 10°, 3.3 versus 1.3 mm at 30° in the toy). Differences between angle pairs also make layered structures nearly invisible (sensitivity \(2(\sec\theta - 1)\)). I would widen the angle span as far as the element directivity allows, add angle pairs with large mid-angle differences, combine with absolute-delay information such as depth-resolved autofocus for layers, and use a prior that matches the anatomy.

Follow-up: "What limits \(\theta_{\max}\)?" Element directivity, grating lobes at large steering, aperture coverage of the oblique rays at depth (fewer valid rows), and speckle decorrelation between widely different angles.

I9A reviewer asks for the resolution of your sound-speed map. What do you report? · research

For a linear (Tikhonov-type) reconstruction, rows of the model-resolution matrix \(\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A} + \lambda^2\mathbf{L}^{T}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}\) at representative pixels: their axial and lateral FWHM, how they vary with depth, and \(\operatorname{trace}\mathbf{R}\) as the effective number of resolved parameters. I would add the noise standard deviation map from the posterior covariance, and for nonlinear regularizers (TV, L1) point-target and edge-response simulations at the same λ. Inclusion-mean accuracy is not resolution.

Follow-up: "Can R be computed for a 3D problem?" Not densely; compute selected rows by solving with unit vectors (one solve each), or estimate the diagonal with randomized probing.

I10How do you validate a regularized reconstruction method without fooling yourself? · research

Avoid the inverse crime: generate data on a finer grid with a different (preferably full-wave) model and realistic, correlated noise. Report the oracle parameter and the parameter from a practical rule. Test priors against phantoms that violate them (gradients for TV, extended targets for L1). Show stability to new noise draws and to model error (wrong c₀, refraction). Then phantoms with independently known sound speed, then in vivo with a reference such as a transmission measurement or a known anatomical value.

Follow-up: "What is the signature of the inverse crime?" Near-perfect reconstructions at unrealistically small λ, and errors that fall without bound as noise goes to zero.

I11Why invert for slowness rather than sound speed, and what does the linearization ignore? · screen

Travel time is the path integral of slowness, so the forward model is linear in \(s = 1/c\) and the Gaussian delay-noise model maps to a quadratic data term. Near 1540 m/s, 1 ns/mm of slowness equals 2.37 m/s (\(\Delta c \approx -c_0^2\,\Delta s\)). The linear model assumes straight rays (no refraction), small contrast and that the misfocused echo stays at the same pixel; larger contrasts call for iterating with ray bending or a wave-based model.

Follow-up: "How big is the delay error from a 50 m/s inclusion?" 20.4 ns/mm, so 204 ns over 10 mm, a full period at 5 MHz.

13.14Self-check

Q1σ = (1, 0.1, 0.01) and λ = 0.03 (penalty \(\lambda^2\lVert x\rVert^2\)). Give the three filter factors. What λ would a code that uses the penalty \(\alpha\lVert x\rVert^2\) need for the same result?

\(f = 1/(1 + 0.0009) = 0.999\); \(0.01/(0.01 + 0.0009) = 0.917\); \(0.0001/(0.0001 + 0.0009) = 0.100\). The other convention needs \(\alpha = \lambda^2 = 9\times10^{-4}\).

Q2With λ = 0.03, what is the largest noise amplification of any component, and at which σ? Compare with the naive gain at σ = 0.01.

\(1/(2\lambda) = 16.7\) at σ = 0.03. At σ = 0.01: Tikhonov \(0.01/(0.0001 + 0.0009) = 10\), naive \(1/0.01 = 100\).

Q3Landweber with \(\omega = 1/\sigma_1^2\) should mimic Tikhonov with \(\lambda = 0.01\,\sigma_1\). About how many iterations? How many would you expect for CGLS?

\(k \approx \ln 2/10^{-4} \approx 6900\). CGLS needs roughly the square root, of order 80 to 100, but check with the discrepancy principle rather than trusting the estimate.

Q4A 10 mm path crosses tissue at 1590 m/s instead of 1540 m/s. What is Δs in ns/mm, the delay change, and the phase change at 5 MHz?

\(\Delta s = (1/1590 - 1/1540)\times10^{6} = -20.4\) ns/mm; delay −204 ns; phase 204 ns × 5 MHz × 360° = 367°, more than a full cycle. Absolute phases wrap; differences between nearby angles do not.

Q5TV denoising (\(\tfrac12\lVert x - y\rVert^2 + \mu\sum\lvert\Delta x\rvert\)) of an isolated plateau 10 samples wide and 1 high with μ = 1.5. What height comes back?

\(1 - 2\mu/w = 1 - 3/10 = 0.7\).

Q6Delay noise \(\sigma_n\) = 2 ns, and the slowness deviation is believed to be zero-mean Gaussian with \(\sigma_x\) = 10 ns/mm per pixel. What λ does the MAP reading give for Tikhonov with L = I?

\(\lambda = \sigma_n/\sigma_x = 2/10 = 0.2\), with units of mm (ns divided by ns/mm), the units of the entries of A. It is meaningful only with A in those units.

Q7Average-to-local inversion with Δz = 0.5 mm and \(\sigma_{\bar c}\) = 0.5 m/s. What noise does the naive inverse have at 40 mm?

\(\sqrt{2}\times(40/0.5)\times 0.5 \approx 57\) m/s (times \((c/\bar c)^2\), close to 1 at that depth).

Q8For a symmetric angle pair at ±15°, what fraction of a layer's vertical delay appears in the common-midangle datum?

\(2(\sec 15° - 1) = 2(1.0353 - 1) = 0.071\), about 7 %.
