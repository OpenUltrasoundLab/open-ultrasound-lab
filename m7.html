Random vectors, covariance & correlation

Second-order statistics rebuilt from the expectation operator up: covariance, the correlation coefficient and how badly it is estimated from few samples, the covariance matrix of a complex IQ vector, correlation functions in time, and finally the channel covariance matrix across an array. That last object, through the van Cittert–Zernike theorem, gives the spatial coherence curve that sound-speed estimators maximize and that aberration flattens.

You will be able to

  • Use the covariance matrix of a complex circular Gaussian vector, its rank when snapshots are scarce, and diagonal loading
  • Disambiguate the six things called "correlation" or "coherence" in the ultrasound literature
  • Derive the VCZ triangle for a focused uniform aperture and predict how noise, aberration and a wrong sound speed change it

07.1Random variables, vectors and the expectation operator

A random variable x is a function that assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment. In ultrasound the experiment is "draw a tissue realization": the positions and reflectivities of the sub-resolution scatterers. One RF sample at a fixed depth is a random variable; the 64 IQ samples across the channels at one depth form a random vector \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{C}^{64}\).

Ensemble versus sample averages; stationarity and ergodicity

You never observe the ensemble. You observe one tissue realization and average over space (pixels in an ROI), time (slow-time frames) or channels. That replacement is legitimate only under two conditions:

  • Stationarity (wide-sense, WSS): the mean is constant and the covariance between two samples depends only on their separation, \(C(t_{1}, t_{2}) = C(t_{1} - t_{2})\). For speckle this means a statistically homogeneous region: same scatterer density, same attenuation-compensated level, same PSF.
  • Ergodicity (in the mean or in correlation): the sample average along one realization converges to the ensemble average. For the mean, a sufficient condition is that the autocovariance decays fast enough to be summable, \(\sum _{k} |C(k)| < \infty\).

The price of correlated samples is quantified by the variance of the sample mean. For N samples of a WSS sequence with correlation coefficient \(\rho (k)\) between samples k apart:

\[ \operatorname{var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N} \sum_{k=-(N-1)}^{N-1} \left(1 - \frac{\lvert k\rvert}{N}\right) \rho(k) \;\equiv\; \frac{\sigma^2}{N_{\mathrm{eff}}} \]

Two different "repeats" are often confused. Re-acquiring a static phantom at the same probe position gives the same speckle realization: frame averaging removes electronic noise but not speckle. Independent realizations come from different scatterer draws: moving the probe by more than a resolution cell, a different phantom region, elevational translation, or tissue motion. A spatial average over an ROI stands in for the ensemble only if the ROI is homogeneous (stationary) and spans many speckle cells. This is the assumption behind every QUS estimate (backscatter coefficient, attenuation, envelope statistics) and behind the spatial-coherence estimates in section 07.6.

\(\rho\) is the cosine of the angle between the centred variables. \(\rho = 0\) means orthogonal, not independent. \(|\rho | = 1\) means an exact affine relation; \(\rho\) says nothing about nonlinear dependence.

Uncorrelated is not independent

Independence means \(p(x, y) = p(x)p(y)\), which forces \(\mathrm{E}[f(x)g(y)] = \mathrm{E}[f(x)]\mathrm{E}[g(y)]\) for every f, g. Uncorrelated asks this only for f, g linear. Example: \(x \sim N(0,\ 1), y = x^{2}\). Then \(\operatorname{cov} (x, y) = \mathrm{E}[x^{3}] = 0\), yet y is a deterministic function of x. The ultrasound version: the in-phase component I and the envelope |z| of circular speckle are uncorrelated (by symmetry) but obviously dependent.

Gaussian exception. If x and y are jointly Gaussian, uncorrelated implies independent, because the joint density factorizes when the off-diagonal of \(\Sigma\) is zero. Two marginally Gaussian variables are not enough; joint Gaussianity is the requirement.

Mean removal

\(\mathrm{E}[xy^{*}]\) (the "correlation" in the signal-processing sense) and cov(x, y) differ by \(\mu _{x}\mu _{y}^{*}\). RF and IQ samples of speckle are zero-mean, so the two coincide. Envelopes, intensities and B-mode pixels are not zero-mean: correlating them without removing the mean gives a large coefficient for two speckle patterns that have nothing to do with each other: \(\mathrm{E}[a]^{2}/\mathrm{E}[a^{2}] = \pi/4 \approx 0.79\) for two independent Rayleigh envelopes, 0.5 for two independent intensities, and close to 1 for log-compressed (dB) B-mode pixels.

Pitfall

A "1σ ellipse" in 2D contains \(1 - e^{-1/2}\) = 39.3% of the probability and the \(2\sigma\) ellipse 86.5%, because the squared Mahalanobis distance is \(\chi ^{2}\) with 2 degrees of freedom: \(P(d \le k) = 1 - e^{-k^{2}/2}\). The 68% / 95% rule is one-dimensional. A 95% confidence region in 2D needs \(k = 2.45\).

Intuition

Fitting a line to a calibration plot (measured vs true attenuation, estimated vs reference sound speed) with ordinary least squares regresses one variable on the other and assumes the regressor is noise-free. If both carry error, the OLS slope is biased toward zero (attenuation bias, or regression dilution); the principal axis (total least squares) treats both symmetrically. Module 05 has the least-squares geometry; here you see it as the difference between the red and teal lines.

Spurious correlation

When \(\rho = 0, \operatorname{std} (\hat{\rho }) \approx 1/\sqrt{N - 1}\). At \(N = 10, a |\hat{\rho }| \ge 0.5\) happens about 14% of the time by chance alone (t-test: \(t = \hat{\rho }\sqrt{N - 2}/\sqrt{1 - \hat{\rho }^{2}}\) with \(N - 2\) degrees of freedom). Worse, all of the above assumes independent pairs. With serially correlated samples, replace N by \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) from section 07.1. Two smooth random curves (or two speckle images over the same ROI) routinely show \(|\hat{\rho }| > 0.5\) with "N = thousands of pixels" because \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is only the number of speckle cells.

Sample covariance with few snapshots: rank deficiency and diagonal loading

With K snapshots stacked as columns of \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{C}^{N\times K}\), the sample covariance is \(\hat{\mathbf{R}} = (1/K)\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{H}\). Its rank is at most min(K, N). With \(K < N\) it is singular, so \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\), which MVDR and whitening need, does not exist. Even for K moderately above N, the eigenvalues of \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) are spread: the large ones biased up, the small ones biased down (Marchenko–Pastur), and the small ones dominate the inverse. Reed, Mallett and Brennan (1974) quantified how many snapshots are enough. For the sample-matrix-inverse (SMI) weights \(\mathbf{w} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\), with K i.i.d. complex Gaussian training snapshots that do not contain the desired signal, the SINR relative to the optimum \(\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{s}\) is a random variable whose distribution depends only on K and N:

Why maximizing coherence estimates sound speed

For diffuse scatterers in a homogeneous medium, the triangle is the upper bound on C(m) for a given aperture: any residual phase across the aperture (wrong \(\hat{c}\), aberration) or noise can only lower it. With \(\hat{c}\) as the only free parameter, the residual phase vanishes at \(\hat{c} = c\), so coherence-based metrics (SLSC, CF, or the full curve) as functions of \(\hat{c}\) peak at the true speed. The sweep panel above computes the expected SLSC and CF analytically for every \(\hat{c}\). The curvature of the peak sets the estimator's sensitivity: with a 19.2 mm aperture focused at 30 mm (F/1.6), a 40 m/s error costs ≈ 4% of SLSC (lags 1–6) but ≈ 28% of CF, which uses every lag. Estimation noise on SLSC from a short axial kernel is of the same order as that 4%, so practical estimators average many pixels. The paraxial edge defocus phase is ≈ \((\pi D^{2}/(2\lambda z))\cdot (\Delta c/c)\), about 1.6 rad here for \(\Delta c = 40\) m/s (1.45 rad with the exact hyperbolic delays): it grows with the aperture squared over \(\lambda z\), so larger apertures and smaller F-numbers sharpen the peak.

Why deviation from the triangle measures aberration

Because the VCZ triangle is known a priori for a given aperture and apodization, the ratio \(C_{\mathrm{measured}}(m)/C_{\mathrm{VCZ}}(m)\) isolates everything that is not ideal: the noise factor at \(m \ge 1\) (a constant), and the two-way aberration factor (decaying with lag, roughly \(\exp (-D_{\varphi}(m))\), half from receive and half from transmit broadening). The lag at which it decays gives the screen's correlation length; its depth gives the rms phase. Correcting the screen (estimating \(\varphi _{i}\) from neighbouring-element cross-correlation, as in Flax and O'Donnell 1988, or from the distortion matrix) restores the curve toward the triangle, and the restored coherence is the standard figure of merit for an aberration-correction method.

Ultrasound link: targets B and C

Target B (sound speed): global estimators sweep \(\hat{c}\) and maximize a focus-quality metric (coherence factor, SLSC, image sharpness); local estimators (tomographic or differentiable-beamforming methods) need the same coherence logic per region plus an inversion. Target C (aberration): the measured coherence deficit is both the diagnostic and the cost function; the distortion-matrix approach (Module 06) can be read as finding the phase law that makes the de-scanned field maximally coherent. ULM (Target A) uses coherence indirectly: microbubbles are point-like, not diffuse, so their channel covariance is rank one (no VCZ triangle), and that contrast is what coherence-based bubble detection exploits.

Pitfall

The coherence factor \(\mathrm{CF} = |\sum _{i}s_{i}|^{2}/(N \sum _{i}|s_{i}|^{2})\) for diffuse speckle with a uniform aperture has an ensemble value (ratio of expectations) of \(\sum _{m}(N - |m|)^{2}/N^{3}\) \(= 2/3 + 1/(3N^{2}) \approx 2/3\). For white noise it is exactly 1/N. The average of the per-snapshot ratio is different (\(\approx 0.57\) for \(N = 64\)), because \(\mathrm{E}[X/Y] \neq \mathrm{E}[X]/\mathrm{E}[Y]\). Papers differ on which they report, and on whether CF is computed per sample or over an axial kernel; the readout shows both.

Pitfall: SLSC lag conventions

Lediju et al. (2011) compute, for each lag m, the normalized correlation of the delayed RF signals of elements i and \(i + m\) over a short axial kernel, average it over the \(N - m\) element pairs, and sum lags 1 to \(M: \mathrm{SLSC} = \sum _{m=1}^{M} \hat{R}(m)\). With IQ data the real part of the complex normalized correlation is used. Check three things before comparing numbers across papers: (1) whether "lag" counts element separation (adjacent elements = lag 1, as here) or is expressed as a fraction of the aperture; (2) whether M is quoted in elements or as \(Q = M/N\), so the same Q on a 64- and a 128-element array means different element counts; (3) whether the curve is averaged with \(1/(N - m)\) and whether the sum is normalized (by M or by the ideal VCZ value, as in the demo's "% of ideal"). The ideal value depends on the transmit aperture through the VCZ triangle, so an SLSC value is meaningful only together with \(N_{t}, M\) and the apodization.

07.8How experts think about it

The expert model has three layers. Speckle is a circular complex Gaussian field, so one covariance matrix is the whole story. That covariance is set by the imaging system, not the tissue: in time by the pulse spectrum (Wiener–Khinchin), across the array by the transmit intensity at the focus (van Cittert–Zernike). Every departure from the system-predicted covariance (noise, aberration, a wrong sound speed, a point target, motion) is a measurable signal, and every estimate of it carries a sampling error set by the number of independent speckle cells, not by the number of samples.

07.10Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Derive the van Cittert–Zernike result for pulse-echo speckle intuitively. What does the coherence curve of a uniform focused aperture look like? · onsite

Diffuse scatterers are independent sources, so their contributions add in power: the focal plane acts as an incoherent source whose brightness is the transmit intensity \(|P_{t}(\xi )|^{2}\). After receive focusing, a source at lateral position \(\xi\) reaches element i with phase \(-kx_{i}\xi /z\), a plane wave across the aperture. The covariance of elements i and j is the sum over sources of \(e^{-jk(x_{i} - x_{j})\xi /z}\) weighted by \(|P_{t}|^{2}\): a Fourier transform of the source intensity evaluated at the separation. Since \(|P_{t}|^{2}\) is the transform of the transmit-aperture autocorrelation, \(C(m) = (t \star t)(m)/(t \star t)(0)\). Uniform: a triangle \(1 - m/N_{t}\), zero at the transmit width; \(N_{t} = 64\) gives \(C(32) = 0.5\).

Follow-up: 32-element transmit, 64-element receive? Zero at lag 32; receive apodization does not change the normalized curve. Away from the focus? The insonified width grows to ≈ \(D|1 - z/z_{f}|\) and the first null moves to a separation ≈ \(\lambda z/w\): at 27 mm with a 30 mm focus, C(16) drops from 0.73 to 0.18.

I2Why does short-lag spatial coherence suppress noise and clutter while keeping speckle? · screen

Channel noise is uncorrelated between elements, so it adds only to the diagonal of \(\mathbf{R}\): it raises lag 0 and scales every lag ≥ 1 by \(\mathrm{SNR}/(1 + \mathrm{SNR})\). SLSC never uses lag 0, so noise does not add to it; at 0 dB SNR the lag-1 value halves (0.98 → 0.49) instead of being swamped. Much reverberation clutter is also nearly incoherent beyond lag 0. Mainlobe speckle, in contrast, keeps \(C(m) \approx 1 - m/N\) at short lags regardless of its brightness, so a clutter-filled anechoic region turns dark. Short lags are used because long lags are both small (triangle) and noisy, and are the first to be destroyed by aberration.

Follow-up: why the real part? The magnitude of a normalized correlation of pure noise is biased up to ≈ \(\sqrt{\pi /(4K)}\) for K snapshots; the real part has zero mean.

I3You sweep the assumed sound speed and plot coherence (CF or SLSC). What do the position, height and width of the peak tell you? · research

Position: in a homogeneous medium the residual focusing phase vanishes only at \(\hat{c} = c\), so the argmax estimates c (the MATLAB lab recovers 1510 m/s to within 1 m/s). In layered media it is a path-averaged speed. Height: compared with the VCZ ideal (SLSC 5.67 for N = 64, M = 6; CF 0.667), a low peak means noise (constant factor at lags ≥ 1) or aberration (lag-dependent loss). Width: set by the edge defocus phase \((\pi D^{2}/(2\lambda z))(\Delta c/c):\ 1.6\) rad at 40 m/s for F/1.6, giving −4% SLSC but about −30% CF. A broad, flat peak means low sensitivity (large F-number, short lags only) or aberration. A shifted, flattened peak with a screen present (+25 m/s for SLSC in the lab) is the signature of aberration bias; noise alone lowers the peak without moving it.

Follow-up: how to make it local? Estimate per region and invert along paths (tomographic or differentiable beamforming), because the focusing delay integrates slowness along the path.

I4How many snapshots do you need for MVDR on a 64-element array, and what do people actually do in ultrasound? · onsite

\(\hat{R} = XX^{H}/K\) has rank ≤ K, so \(K \ge N = 64\) just to invert it. Reed–Mallett–Brennan: with i.i.d. signal-free snapshots the SINR ratio is \(\mathrm{Beta}(K - N + 2, N - 1)\), mean \((K - N + 2)/(K + 1)\), so \(K = 2N - 3 = 125\) for −3 dB and \(K = N\) gives \(2/(N + 1)\) (−15.1 dB for \(N = 64\)). A pixel offers perhaps 1–20 axial samples, so ultrasound MVDR uses sub-aperture averaging (\(L \le N/2\), multiplying snapshots by \(N - L + 1\)) plus diagonal loading \(\delta = \Delta \cdot \operatorname{tr} (\hat{R})/L\) with \(\Delta \approx 10^{-2}\).

Follow-up: why is it worse than RMB in practice? The snapshots contain the desired echo, so errors in steering or delays make the adaptive weights cancel signal; loading bounds the white-noise gain and restores robustness.

I5Distinguish correlation coefficient, correlation function, covariance matrix and coherence. · screen

The correlation coefficient is one number, \(\operatorname{cov} (x, y)/(\sigma _{x}\sigma _{y})\), mean and scale removed, \(|\rho | \le 1\) by Cauchy–Schwarz (NCC peak in speckle tracking). The correlation function \(R_{xy}(\tau )\) is the same inner product as a function of lag (delay estimation, Kasai's arg R(1)). The covariance matrix collects all pairwise covariances of a vector; it is Hermitian PSD, and \(w^{H}Rw\) is beamformer output power. Spatial coherence C(m) is the per-pair normalized covariance averaged along a diagonal of that matrix, a function of element lag. Spectral coherence \(\gamma ^{2}(f)\) normalizes the cross-spectrum per frequency. All coherences are averages: from one segment or snapshot they are identically 1.

Follow-up: is "correlation matrix" the normalized one? It depends on the field: statistics normalizes to unit diagonal; array processing means \(\mathrm{E}[xx^{H}]\), identical to R for zero-mean IQ.

I6State and sketch the Wiener–Khinchin theorem. Why does it matter for speckle and QUS? · onsite

For a WSS process the PSD is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation, \(S(f) = \int R(\tau )e^{-j2\pi f\tau}d\tau\). Sketch: \(\mathrm{E}|X_{T}(f)|^{2}/T\) for a record of length T is the transform of the triangularly weighted \(R(\tau )(1 - |\tau |/T)\), which tends to S(f) as \(T \to \infty\). Finite-record version: the periodogram is exactly the DFT of the biased \(\hat{R}(k)\). For speckle, white reflectivity through the pulse gives \(S = N_{0}|P(f)|^{2}\) and \(R = N_{0}R_{pp}\): the speckle autocorrelation is the pulse autocorrelation (FWHM 1.25/B for a Gaussian pulse), which is why spectral QUS divides by a reference-phantom spectrum.

Follow-up: why average periodograms? Each bin of a raw periodogram is exponential with std/mean = 1 whatever the record length; Welch averaging of K segments cuts that by about \(\sqrt{K}\) at the cost of resolution.

I7What is the coherence factor for a point target, for diffuse speckle and for noise, with a uniform 64-element aperture? · onsite

\(\mathrm{CF} = |\sum s_{i}|^{2}/(N\sum |s_{i}|^{2})\). Point at focus: identical channels, \(\mathrm{CF} = 1\). Diffuse speckle (ratio of expectations): \(\sum _{m}(N - |m|)C(m)/N^{2}\) with C the triangle, = \(2/3 + 1/(3N^{2}) = 0.667\). Noise: only lag 0 contributes, \(\mathrm{CF} = 1/N = 0.016\). Speckle at 0 dB channel SNR: (N·0.667 + 1)/(2N) = 0.34.

Follow-up: the per-pixel average of CF in speckle is ≈ 0.57, not 0.667, because \(\mathrm{E}[X/Y] \neq \mathrm{E}[X]/\mathrm{E}[Y]\); check which a paper reports, and whether CF was computed per sample or over an axial kernel.

I8Two speckle kernels have true correlation 0.95, each with 10 dB SNR of independent noise. What correlation do you measure, and how would you get channel SNR from coherence? · screen

Noise adds to the variances but not the covariance: \(\rho _{\mathrm{meas}} = \rho /\sqrt{(1 + 1/\mathrm{SNR}_{1})(1 + 1/\mathrm{SNR}_{2})}\) \(= 0.95 \times 10/11\) = 0.864. The same factor scales spatial coherence at lags ≥ 1, so with the VCZ value known, \(\mathrm{SNR} = C(1)/(C_{\mathrm{VCZ}}(1) - C(1)):\) \(N = 128, C(1) = 0.80\) gives \(\mathrm{SNR} = 4.2\) (6.2 dB). This is lag-one coherence (Long, Bottenus and Trahey 2018).

Follow-up: what inflates C(1) artificially? Crosstalk or common-mode electronic noise, which is correlated between neighbouring channels.

I9A QUS parameter correlates with fat fraction at \(\hat{\rho } = 0.6\) in 12 patients. Give a 95% interval. What changes if each patient contributes 50 correlated ROIs? · screen

Fisher \(z: \operatorname{atanh} 0.6 = 0.693\), se = \(1/\sqrt{N - 3} = 1/3\), interval 0.693 ± 0.653 = [0.040, 1.347] in \(z, \rho\) ∈ [0.04, 0.87]. Barely excludes zero. Adding 50 ROIs per patient does not give \(N = 600\): the ROIs within a patient share the same fat fraction and are strongly correlated, so the effective N stays near 12 (use a mixed model or per-patient averages).

Follow-up: what is \(\operatorname{std} (\hat{\rho })\) at \(\rho = 0.9\) with \(N = 16\) independent pairs? \((1 - 0.81)/\sqrt{15} = 0.049\).

I10Why is speckle IQ circular complex Gaussian, and what are the envelope statistics? Are I and |z| independent? · screen

Each IQ sample sums many phasors with independent, uniform phases: the central limit theorem gives a Gaussian, uniform phases give rotation invariance, so \(\mathrm{E}[z^{2}] = 0, I\) and Q uncorrelated with equal variance, and (jointly Gaussian) independent. Then |z| is Rayleigh with mean/std = \(\sqrt{\pi /(4 - \pi )} = 1.91, |z|^{2}\) is exponential with mean/std = 1, the phase is uniform. I and |z| are uncorrelated (symmetry under I → −I) but dependent \((|z| \ge |I|);\) \(\operatorname{corr} (I^{2}, |z|^{2}) = 1/\sqrt{2}\).

Follow-up: what breaks circularity, and would the envelope show it? I/Q gain or phase imbalance gives \(|P|/R \approx 0.42\) for 20% gain error and 10° skew, but the envelope SNR only moves from 1.91 to 1.82: the envelope is a poor detector.

I11Your measured coherence curve falls below the VCZ triangle. How do you tell noise from aberration? · research

Divide by the known triangle. Noise gives a ratio that is flat for all \(m \ge 1\) (equal to \(\mathrm{SNR}/(1 + \mathrm{SNR})\)) with a discontinuity at lag 0. Aberration gives a ratio that decays with lag, ≈ \(e^{-D_{\varphi}(m)}\) two-way, where \(D_{\varphi}\) is the phase structure function; the lag where it falls gives the screen correlation length and the depth gives the rms phase (one screen realization in the MATLAB lab, \(\sigma = 0.7\) rad, \(\ell = 4\) elements: \(C(4) = 0.57\) against 0.94; the ensemble value \(e^{-D_{\varphi}(4)}\cdot 0.94\) is 0.64). A wrong sound speed gives a similar lag-dependent loss, so sweep \(\hat{c}\) first. A linear phase error gives no loss at all.

Follow-up: what figure of merit would you report for an aberration-correction method? The restored coherence curve (or SLSC, CF) relative to the triangle, at matched SNR, plus point-target resolution.

I12An RF kernel is 5 mm long at 5 MHz, 60% bandwidth, 50 MHz sampling. How many independent samples does it contain? · research

It holds 325 samples but about 16 speckle cells of length c·1.25/(2B) = 0.32 mm. The effective N depends on the statistic: \(N_{\mathrm{eff}} = T/\int \rho _{q}(\tau )d\tau\) with \(\rho _{q}\) the correlation of the averaged quantity. For an intensity mean, \(\int |r_{\mathrm{env}}|^{2} = 0.94/B\) gives 20.8; for a correlation coefficient between two RF kernels, \(\int r^{2} \approx 0.47/B\) gives 41.5 (Monte Carlo 40). So \(\operatorname{std} (\hat{\rho })\) at \(\rho = 0.9\) is ≈ 0.03, between the cell-count estimate 0.05 and the naive sample-count estimate 0.011.

Follow-up: why does the RF correlation coefficient get twice the intensity count? The carrier makes \(r(\tau )^{2}\) oscillate as \(\cos ^{2}\), halving its integral.

Q5In circular speckle, are I and Q independent? Are I and the envelope |z| independent? Correlated?

I and Q: jointly Gaussian with \(\mathrm{E}[IQ] = 0\) (circularity), therefore independent. Q6MVDR on a 64-element array with \(K = 40\) axial snapshots per pixel. What is the rank of \(\hat{R}?\) With subarrays of \(L = 32\), how many effective snapshots, and is it enough by the RMB rule?

With \(L = 32\) there are \(N - L + 1 = 33\) subarrays, so 40 × 33 = 1320 (correlated) snapshots for a 32×32 matrix, well above \(2L = 64\). Subarray snapshots are not independent, so add diagonal loading (e.g. \(\Delta = 0.01\)) for robustness to steering errors.

Q75 MHz probe, 60% bandwidth, fs = 50 MHz. How many independent samples are in a 5 mm axial window, and what spread do you expect for a \(\hat{\rho }\) computed over it when \(\rho\) = 0.9?

\(B = 3\) MHz, \(\tau _{\mathrm{FWHM}} \approx 1.25/B = 0.42\) µs, axial cell \(c\cdot \tau /2 = 0.32\) mm. 5 mm / 0.32 mm ≈ 16 speckle cells, versus 2·5 mm/1540 m/s·50 MHz ≈ 325 samples. Treating the cells as independent pairs gives \(\operatorname{std} (\hat{\rho }) \approx (1 - 0.81)/\sqrt{15}\) \(\approx 0.05\), a conservative estimate. The exact effective N for a correlation coefficient between RF kernels is \(T/\int r^{2}(\tau )d\tau\) ≈ T·B/0.47 ≈ 41, so \(\operatorname{std} (\hat{\rho }) \approx 0.19/\sqrt{41}\) ≈ 0.03 (Monte Carlo in m07_speckle_autocorr_psd.m: 0.030). Using the sample count, \((1 - 0.81)/\sqrt{324} \approx 0.011\), is wrong by a factor of 3.

Q8A linear phase ramp across the aperture leaves the coherence curve unchanged; a quadratic one lowers it. Why?

A linear phase \(\varphi _{i} = \alpha i\) applied two-way steers the transmit beam and the receive focus by the same angle: \(R_{ij} = e^{j\alpha (i-j)}\cdot g(i - j)\) with g unchanged except for a shift of \(|P_{t}|^{2}\), which only multiplies g by \(e^{-j\alpha (i-j)}\). The two phases cancel, so C(m) is the same triangle; the image shifts laterally instead. A quadratic phase defocuses: the transmit intensity broadens (its FT, the coherence, narrows) and the receive phase difference \(\varphi _{i} - \varphi _{i+m}\) varies along the diagonal, so averaging it reduces |C(m)|. This is why a wrong sound speed, which produces a quadratic residual, is detectable by coherence while a pure steering error is not.
