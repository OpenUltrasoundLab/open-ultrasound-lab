Matched filtering & pulse compression

Coded excitation as an SNR budget tool. The matched filter makes output SNR depend on energy alone, so a long, low-amplitude code can carry the energy of a pulse the mechanical index would forbid and still compress to the resolution of its bandwidth. This module derives that result with its factor-2 conventions, prices every sidelobe fix in dB, shows what attenuation, motion and Doppler do to chirps and Golay pairs, and ends where a 510(k) reviewer starts: at fixed MI, coding raises ISPTA and TI by exactly the compression gain.

You will be able to

  • Derive the matched filter from Cauchy–Schwarz and state its output SNR in the real (\(2E/N_0\)) and complex (\(E/N_0\)) conventions
  • Predict the −6 dB width, peak sidelobe and SNR cost of a tapered chirp from \(T\), \(B\) and the window
  • Use Barker and Golay codes, size a least-squares mismatched filter, and compute the Golay residual for a given motion between firings
  • Explain range–Doppler coupling of an LFM and why it is a micron-scale bias in ultrasound
  • Quantify what frequency-dependent attenuation does to compression, and choose fixed, depth-adapted or equalizing filters
  • Convert a compression gain into penetration depth, and into MI, ISPTA.3 and TI consequences for an acoustic-output submission

10.1The matched filter: SNR depends only on energy

An echo \(s(t - t_0)\) of known shape arrives in additive white Gaussian noise \(n(t)\). We pass the record \(x(t)\) through a linear filter \(h(t)\) and look at the output at \(t_0\). Which \(h\) maximizes the output SNR? Write the signal output and noise power in the frequency domain:

The matched filter is the time-reversed, conjugated echo. Its output is the cross-correlation of the record with the template: \(y(t) = \int x(u)\, s^*(u - t + t_0)\,du\), the correlation receiver.

Two consequences drive the rest of the module. First, the maximum SNR contains only \(E\): the shape of \(s\) is free. A 0.3 µs pulse and a 20 µs chirp with the same energy give the same peak SNR, so duration can buy energy while bandwidth buys resolution. Second, the optimum is a ratio of inner products, so it is flat near its peak: a moderately wrong filter costs little SNR. For a Gaussian echo spectrum of std \(\sigma_s\) (amplitude) and a Gaussian filter of std \(\sigma_h\) offset by \(\Delta f\),

\[ \eta = \frac{\left\lvert\int H S\right\rvert^{2}}{\int\lvert H\rvert^{2}\int\lvert S\rvert^{2}} = \frac{2\sigma_s\sigma_h}{\sigma_s^{2} + \sigma_h^{2}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta f^{2}}{\sigma_s^{2} + \sigma_h^{2}}\right) \]

Colored noise. If the noise PSD is \(N(f)\) rather than flat, the same argument with \(u = H\sqrt{N}\) gives \(H = S^* e^{-j2\pi f t_0}/N(f)\): whiten, then match. The maximum becomes \(\int \lvert S(f)\rvert^2/N(f)\,df\). This is also the reason a filter matched to the electrical drive waveform is wrong: the template must be the received echo, after both passes through the transducer.

Ultrasound link

You already use matched filters. DAS is the spatial matched filter of a point target (Module 05: the adjoint, not the inverse). Correlation-based delay estimation is matched filtering against a reference A-line (Module 11), and its peak is the maximum-likelihood delay in white noise. The receive band-pass filter on any scanner approximates a matched filter to the transducer's two-way response; the result above says that getting it within a factor 2 in bandwidth is good enough for SNR. TGC multiplies signal and noise alike and changes no SNR.

Factor-2 traps

Most "3 dB discrepancies" between a simulation and \(2E/N_0\) are convention, not physics: complex noise counted in both quadratures, one-sided versus two-sided \(N_0\), or energy of the analytic signal (\(2E\)) used with the noise of the real one. State the convention next to every SNR you report, and measure SNR as mean²/variance of the filter output at the peak sample over many noise realizations.

10.2Time–bandwidth product and compression gain

A conventional pulse couples energy to resolution. Its energy is about \(p_{\mathrm{peak}}^2\tau/(\rho c)\) per unit area and its axial resolution is \(c\tau/2\) with \(\tau \approx 1/B\). The mechanical index caps \(p_{\mathrm{peak}}\), so with a short pulse the only way to more energy is a longer pulse, which destroys resolution. Pulse compression breaks the coupling: transmit a code of duration \(T \gg 1/B\) with bandwidth \(B\), then compress it with the matched filter to a mainlobe of width \(\approx 1/B\).

\[ \text{compression ratio} = \frac{T}{1/B} = TB, \qquad G = \frac{E_{\mathrm{code}}}{E_{\mathrm{ref}}} \ \text{ at equal peak amplitude} \]
(10.5)

Constant-envelope code of amplitude \(A\): energy \(A^2T\) (complex envelope). Gaussian envelope \(A\,e^{-t^2/2\sigma_t^2}\): energy \(\sqrt\pi\,\sigma_t A^2\). TB = 50 gives 18.77 dB; a Monte Carlo with \(10^4\) trials gives 18.70 dB (m10_chirp_compression.m).

The mechanism is phase alignment. A chirp delays each frequency by a different group delay, \(\tau_g(f) = (f - f_0)T/B\) for an up-chirp. The matched filter has the opposite group delay, so after filtering all frequencies arrive at the same instant: the output spectrum \(\lvert S(f)\rvert^2\) is real and zero-phase, and its transform is as short as the bandwidth allows. A phase code does the same thing with a random-looking phase spectrum that the filter conjugates away.

Intuition

Think of the code as a deliberately defocused pulse in time, exactly like a defocused aperture in space. The matched filter applies the conjugate delay law, the way a focusing law cancels the quadratic phase across the aperture (Module 04). An LFM is literally a quadratic phase in time, and compression is focusing in range.

10.4Phase codes: Barker, mismatched filters and Golay pairs

A binary phase code transmits \(N\) chips of duration \(T_c\) with phases 0 or \(\pi\) (amplitudes \(c_k = \pm 1\)). In ultrasound a chip is typically one or a few carrier cycles. The matched filter output, sampled at chip spacing, is the aperiodic autocorrelation

\[ R(k) = \sum_i c_i\, c_{i+k}, \qquad R(0) = N, \qquad G = N \ \text{ at equal peak amplitude} \]

Golay pairs suit slow targets. Coherently summing \(K\) matched-filtered pulses multiplies the signal amplitude by \(K\) and the noise variance by \(K\), so the SNR grows by \(K\): exactly what one code with \(K\) times the energy gives. Coding therefore buys time (frame rate, no motion during the average) and lower peak pressure, not energy. That is the right way to explain it to a regulator or a reviewer.

Separate transmit and receive apertures reduce the problem; partial compression degrades gradually rather than as a hard edge.

Quadratic nonlinearity produces \(a^2(t)\cos(2\omega t + 2\phi(t))/2\) at the second harmonic. For a biphase code \(\phi \in \{0, \pi\}\), so \(2\phi \equiv 0\): the harmonic is an uncoded pulse of length \(T\) with no compression. For a chirp \(2\phi(t)\) is again quadratic, a chirp centred at \(2f_0\) sweeping \(2B\), compressible with a filter matched to it. That is why harmonic coded imaging uses chirps.

10.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Derive the matched filter and its output SNR. · screen

Signal output at \(t_0\) is \(\int HS\,e^{j2\pi f t_0}df\), noise variance \((N_0/2)\int\lvert H\rvert^2 df\) for real white noise of two-sided PSD \(N_0/2\). Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the ratio by \(\int\lvert S\rvert^2/(N_0/2) = 2E/N_0\), with equality for \(H = \kappa S^* e^{-j2\pi f t_0}\), i.e. \(h(t) = \kappa s^*(t_0 - t)\). The output is the cross-correlation of the record with the template; only \(E\) appears, not the shape.

Follow-up: what if the noise is colored? Whiten and match: \(H = S^* e^{-j2\pi f t_0}/N(f)\), \(\mathrm{SNR} = \int\lvert S\rvert^2/N(f)\,df\).

I2Your simulation shows matched-filter SNR exactly 3 dB below 2E/N₀. Bug? · screen

Probably a convention. If the data are analytic or IQ and SNR is defined as \(\lvert y\rvert^2/\mathrm{E}\lvert n_{\mathrm{out}}\rvert^2\), both noise quadratures are counted and the result is \(E/N_0\). Taking \(\mathrm{Re}\{y\}\) at the peak restores \(2E/N_0\) (the script measures 6.95 vs 9.94 dB). Other usual causes: one-sided \(N_0\), or the analytic signal's energy \(2E\) paired with the real signal's noise.

Follow-up: does envelope detection really lose 3 dB? Not at high SNR, where the quadrature noise adds only a second-order term to the envelope; the loss appears as a detection-threshold effect at low SNR.

I3Why use a chirp instead of simply driving the probe harder? · onsite

Peak rarefactional pressure is capped by the MI limit (1.9, Track 3), by pulser voltage and by nonlinear distortion at high pressure. SNR depends on energy, so at a fixed peak the only lever is duration; compression restores the \(1/B\) resolution. A TB-50 chirp gives 18.8 dB against a Gaussian pulse of the same band, about 3.8 cm more depth at 5 MHz and 0.5 dB/cm/MHz.

Follow-up: what limits \(T\)? Blind zone \(cT/2\), \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA}}\)/TI (energy scales with \(T\)), frame rate in multi-line or plane-wave sequences, attenuation mismatch, and transmit/receive switching.

I4Your chirp shows −13 dB range sidelobes. How do you get to −50 dB, and what does it cost? · onsite

Taper. On receive alone, Hann gives −31.3 dB for 1.76 dB and a 1.65× wider mainlobe, but Hamming and Blackman stall near −36 dB at TB = 50 because of Fresnel ripple. On transmit the PSL drops to −46 (Hann) or −81 dB (Blackman) at a 4–5 dB energy cost at fixed peak; split tapering sits between. Alternatives: nonlinear FM (spectrum shaped by sweep rate, no mismatch loss), least-squares or Wiener mismatched filters, and larger \(TB\).

Follow-up: why does the transmit taper beat the ideal-window number for Hann? Because the output spectrum is then closer to the window squared, and the smooth envelope suppresses the band-edge ripple.

I5Golay pair or chirp for deep plane-wave ULM at 3 MHz? · research

Golay gives exactly zero static sidelobes but needs two firings per angle, halving the compounded frame rate. With \(N\) = 16 and 5 cm/s axial motion at a 5 kHz PRF, \(d\) = 10 µm, \(\varphi = 4\pi \cdot 3\ \mathrm{MHz} \cdot 10\ \mu\mathrm{m}/1540\ \mathrm{m/s}\) = 0.245 rad and the residual is −28.3 dB; tissue and bubble motion at higher speed degrade it further, and the nonlinear bubble echo differs between firings. A tapered chirp compresses in one firing, survives motion (about 12 ns of Doppler bias per m/s at 5 MHz, \(B\) = 5 MHz, \(T\) = 10 µs), and its harmonic remains compressible. For ULM I would choose a tapered chirp and budget its sidelobes against the bubble-to-tissue ratio after SVD filtering.

Follow-up: how would you rescue Golay? Estimate the inter-firing displacement (e.g. Kasai phase, Module 11) and rotate echo B before summing.

I6What does frequency-dependent attenuation do to a compressed chirp, and how do you fix it? · research

It tapers the received spectrum toward low frequencies: centre 5.00 → 3.68 MHz at 6 cm for 0.5 dB/cm/MHz with an 80 % transducer. With the surface filter the peak does not move (zero-phase model), but the mainlobe widens (338 → 374 ns), the PSL rises (−29.8 → −26.1 dB) and the mismatch loss is 2.0 dB. A depth-adapted filter recovers the 2 dB but widens to 475 ns; an equalizer keeps 376 ns and costs 5.8 dB. Depth-dependent filter banks interpolate between them.

Follow-up: why does the raw chirp envelope move by −2.5 µs while the compressed peak stays? The LFM maps frequency to time, so a spectral tilt shifts the envelope centroid by \((f_c - f_0)T/B\); the matched filter removes the quadratic phase first.

I7Is range–Doppler coupling of an LFM a concern in ultrasound? · research

Not for SNR, sometimes for position. \(\Delta\tau \approx \nu T/B\) with \(\nu = 2vf_0/c\): at 1 m/s, 5 MHz, \(T\) = 10 µs, \(B\) = 5 MHz the ridge gives 13.0 ns and the exact peak 11.8 ns (finite-\(TB\) correction \(3/(\pi^2B^2T)\)), i.e. 9 µm of apparent depth for 0.01 dB of loss. That is small against the 0.19 mm resolution but not against ULM localization precision or µm-scale elastography displacements, and it depends on velocity. Alternate up- and down-chirps, or average them, to cancel it.

Follow-up: when does the narrowband ambiguity function fail? When \((2v/c)\,TB\) approaches 1, i.e. when the echo's time compression over the code length is comparable to the resolution: never at tissue speeds with clinical codes.

I8You add a coded B-mode to a cleared system. What changes in the 510(k) acoustic-output section? · onsite

It must be measured in the new mode at worst-case settings. At the same peak pressure MI is unchanged, but PII, \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA.3}}\) and power scale by the energy ratio, so a TB-50 chirp at MI 1.9 and 200 pulses/s at a point goes from 27 to 2003 mW/cm², over the 720 mW/cm² Track 3 limit; TIs rise by the same factor. You would cap MI (×0.60 to 1.14 here) or the pulse rate for the coded mode, define \(f_{\mathrm{awf}}\) for a swept waveform, and check derated values with nonlinear propagation in water.

Follow-up: can you claim the SNR gain if \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA}}\) already binds? No: at a binding energy limit the gain against white noise is zero; coding helps where MI, not energy, was the binding constraint.

I9How much depth does 15 dB of compression gain buy? · screen

\(\Delta z = G/(2\alpha f_0)\): 3.0 cm at 0.5 dB/cm/MHz and 5 MHz, 5.0 cm at 3 MHz. For a broadband pulse with an adapted filter it is more, 4.9 cm at 5 MHz with a 60 % Gaussian band cut at its −24 dB points, because the deep spectrum downshifts to where attenuation per cm is lower.

Follow-up: and in a clutter-limited region? Nothing: clutter scales with the transmit.

I10Will coded excitation improve the contrast of a liver cyst at 6 cm in an obese patient? · onsite

Only to the extent the cyst is noise-limited. Reverberation from the abdominal wall and off-axis clutter are copies of the transmitted code and compress with the same gain, and range sidelobes of bright interfaces add axial fill-in. If the noise floor sits above the clutter at that depth, coding lowers it by up to \(G\) and contrast improves; otherwise harmonic imaging or better aberration and clutter suppression matter more.

Follow-up: how would you tell which regime you are in? Turn the transmit off (or receive with transmit muted) and compare the noise floor with the signal level inside the cyst at the same gain.

I11What is the PSL of Barker-13, and how would you improve it without a longer code? · screen

Sidelobes are 0 or 1 against a peak of 13: −22.28 dB, with an 11.14 dB gain. A least-squares mismatched filter of length 39 gives −38.5 dB for 0.20 dB of SNR loss (65 taps: −58.6 dB). Concatenating or nesting codes, or switching to Golay pairs, are the other options.

Follow-up: why not design the filter on the ±1 chip sequence directly? Because the received chips are band-limited by the transducer; the mismatched filter must be designed on the received waveform.

10.13Self-check

Q1A real RF echo has Σs² = 100 (sampled) in white noise of variance σ² = 4. What is the matched-filter peak SNR, and what does a complex |y|² definition report on the analytic signal?

\(\sum s^2/\sigma^2\) = 25 = 14.0 dB (\(= 2E/N_0\)). The analytic \(\lvert y\rvert^2/\mathrm{E}\lvert n\rvert^2\) definition reports 12.5 = 11.0 dB (\(E/N_0\)). Same data, same detectability at high SNR.

Q2A chirp has T = 20 µs and B = 4 MHz. Give TB, the compressed −6 dB width and axial resolution, the gain against a Gaussian pulse of the same band, and the blind zone.

\(TB\) = 80. Width \(1.207/4\ \mathrm{MHz}\) = 302 ns, \(\Delta z = c \cdot 302\ \mathrm{ns}/2\) = 0.232 mm. \(G = 10\log_{10}(1.505 \times 80)\) = 20.8 dB. Blind zone \(cT/2\) = 15.4 mm.

Q3What does a Hann receive taper do to a TB-50 chirp?

PSL −13.5 → −31.3 dB, −6 dB width \(1.21/B \to 2.00/B\) (×1.65), SNR loss \((\sum w)^2/(N\sum w^2)\) = 0.667 = −1.76 dB.

Q4A Golay pair with N = 16 at 5 MHz sees 5 µm of axial motion between firings. What is the residual PSL, and what velocity is that at a 5 kHz PRF?

\(\varphi = 4\pi \cdot 5\ \mathrm{MHz} \cdot 5\ \mu\mathrm{m}/1540\ \mathrm{m/s}\) = 0.204 rad; \(\mathrm{PSL} = (5/16)\tan(0.102)\) = 0.032 → −29.9 dB. Velocity 5 µm × 5 kHz = 25 mm/s.

Q5How much depth does an 18.8 dB compression gain buy at 0.5 dB/cm/MHz at 3 MHz and at 7.5 MHz?

\(18.77/(2 \times 0.5 \times 3)\) = 6.3 cm; \(18.77/(2 \times 0.5 \times 7.5)\) = 2.5 cm (narrowband rule, noise-limited region).

Q6MI 1.9 at 5 MHz, 200 pulses per second at a point. Compare ISPTA.3 for a 60 % Gaussian pulse and a TB-50 chirp of the same band and peak pressure.

\(p_{r.3} = 1.9\sqrt5\) = 4.25 MPa. Pulse: \(\mathrm{PII} = p^2\sqrt\pi\,\sigma_t/(2\rho c)\) = 1.33 J/m² → 27 mW/cm². Chirp: \(\mathrm{PII} = p^2T/(2\rho c)\) with \(T\) = 16.7 µs = 100 J/m² → about 2000 mW/cm², ratio 75 = \(1.505\,TB\), above the 720 mW/cm² limit. MI is the same for both.

Q7Why does the compressed chirp peak stay put under attenuation while the raw echo moves 2.5 µs earlier at 6 cm?

Linear-in-f attenuation without dispersion is a real, non-negative spectral weighting, so \(E_zE_0^*\) is real and ≥ 0 and the compressed envelope peaks at lag 0. The raw up-chirp emits low frequencies first; weighting them up moves its energy centroid earlier by \(\approx (f_c - f_0)T/B\).

Q8Why can a biphase code not be used directly for second-harmonic imaging, while a chirp can?

The second harmonic carries twice the phase: 0 and \(\pi\) both map to 0 (mod \(2\pi\)), so the code disappears and the harmonic is an uncompressible long pulse. A chirp's doubled phase is still quadratic: a chirp at \(2f_0\) sweeping \(2B\), compressible with its own matched filter.
