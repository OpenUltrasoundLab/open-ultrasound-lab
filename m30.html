Elastography: strain, ARFI and shear-wave imaging

Elastography images a mechanical property that B-mode cannot see: the shear modulus, which spans five orders of magnitude across soft tissues while the bulk modulus barely changes. Every method has two halves: a way to deform the tissue (hand compression, acoustic radiation force, an external vibrator) and a way to measure the resulting displacement (the speckle-tracking estimators of Module 11, run at kHz frame rates). Shear-wave imaging adds a third half that decides the accuracy: the estimator that turns a space–time displacement field into a speed, and the assumptions (elastic, isotropic, bulk, non-dispersive) that turn the speed into kPa.

You will be able to

  • Convert between \(E\), \(\mu\) and \(c_s\) with \(\mu = \rho c_s^{2}\) and \(E \approx 3\mu\), and state every assumption that conversion makes
  • Predict strain-image SNR, contrast and resolution from kernel length, LSQ span and applied strain (the strain filter), and explain why strain ratios underestimate modulus ratios
  • Compute the radiation force \(F = 2\alpha I/c\), the resulting ARFI displacement in µm, its recovery time and the heating per push
  • Design and critique shear-wave speed estimators: time-to-peak and cross-correlation regression, k–f phase velocity, local wavenumber, directional filtering, and their resolution–precision trade-offs
  • Separate phase from group velocity in viscoelastic tissue (Kelvin–Voigt, power law) and recognize guided-wave (Lamb) dispersion in arteries, cornea and thin layers

In 60 seconds
  1. Speed to stiffness: Elastography measures shear waves, which travel on tissue's shear modulus (kilopascals, against gigapascals for B-mode waves). Because tissue is nearly incompressible, Young's modulus is \(E \approx 3\rho c_s^2\), with ρ the density and \(c_s\) the shear-wave speed, so a speed error doubles in kPa. The conversion assumes elastic, isotropic, unbounded tissue; report m/s. §30.1
  2. Strain imaging: Strain imaging tracks echo shifts across a gentle compression; strain is the depth slope of displacement, so its noise falls steeply with kernel length. Strain is not modulus: a circular inclusion four times stiffer than its surroundings shows a strain ratio of only 2.5. Only layers under uniform stress give the true modulus ratio. §30.2
  3. Radiation force: Absorbed ultrasound pushes tissue with a force proportional to absorption times intensity. A short push ends at a displacement that barely depends on stiffness: quadrupling the shear modulus lowers the peak of a 200 µs push only 1.77 times. Radiation-force (ARFI) displacement therefore compresses stiffness contrast and is a relative measure, and push trains heat tissue. §30.3
  4. Making and timing shear waves: A push that sweeps faster than the shear wave launches tilted fronts, so a lateral time-of-flight reads high: 15.5 % when the sweep is twice the shear speed. Fitting arrival times over a longer distance L improves precision as \(L^{3/2}\) (fixed spacing) but coarsens resolution as L, the core resolution–precision trade-off of shear-wave elastography (SWE). §30.4 §30.5
  5. Phase-velocity estimators: Frequency–wavenumber (k–f) analysis reads speed per frequency from a 2D Fourier peak, so it needs several wavelengths in the window. The local phase gradient needs only two points but wraps unless their spacing is under a quarter wavelength. A directional filter separates waves travelling each way, removing reflections but smearing edges over about a wavelength. §30.6
  6. Dispersion: In viscous tissue shear speed rises with frequency, so a pulse's group velocity exceeds the phase velocity at one frequency, and each estimator samples a different point. For a spring-and-dashpot (Kelvin–Voigt) medium with 1.5 m/s elastic speed and 1 Pa·s viscosity, group velocity is 2.01 m/s against 1.66 m/s phase velocity at 200 Hz. §30.7
  7. Guided waves and anisotropy: In a layer thinner than a shear wavelength the wave becomes a slow, dispersive plate mode: a 1 mm plate with 3 m/s bulk speed at 200 Hz makes a bulk formula report 21 % of the modulus. In muscle speed depends on fibre angle, so an isotropic estimator returns an in-between value. §30.8
From the field
  • Report shear-wave speed in m/s with its frequency band, estimator and depth. Each estimator samples a different point of the speed-versus-frequency curve, and kPa doubles any speed error. For a Kelvin–Voigt medium (1.5 m/s elastic speed, 1 Pa·s viscosity) group and phase velocity at 200 Hz differ by 21 %, 47 % in kPa. §30.7
  • Keep the region away from boundaries and thin layers, and filter by direction first. Reflected waves bias single-frequency estimators and guided modes in thin layers run slow. In a 3 m/s inclusion the phase gradient reads 2.51 ± 0.99 m/s unfiltered and 3.02 ± 0.29 m/s with a directional mask. §30.6 §30.9
  • Budget the push train against thermal index and time-averaged intensity, not mechanical index. One push heats little, but 2D shear-wave imaging fires tens of pushes per image and the heat adds up: a single 200 µs push heats about 12 mK, and 50 pushes reach 0.6 K, which caps push rate and length. §30.3 §30.11
  • Report liver stiffness as a median of acquisitions with passing IQR/median (interquartile range over median). 30.1Tissue mechanics: three moduli, two wave speeds and one conversion

Two constants describe a linear, isotropic, elastic solid. Elastography uses three names for them. The Young's modulus \(E\) is the ratio of stress to strain in uniaxial loading with free sides, the shear modulus \(\mu\) the ratio of shear stress to shear angle, and the bulk modulus \(K\) the ratio of pressure to volume change. Each wave sees a different combination:

\[ c_s = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\rho}}, \qquad c_L = \sqrt{\frac{K + \tfrac{4}{3}\mu}{\rho}}, \qquad E = 2\mu(1+\nu) = \frac{9K\mu}{3K+\mu} \;\approx\; 3\mu = 3\rho c_s^{2} \]
(30.1)
\(c_s\)
shear (transverse) wave speed, m/s
\(c_L\)
longitudinal (ultrasound) speed, ≈ 1540 m/s
\(\mu\)
shear modulus, Pa (soft tissue 0.3–30 kPa)
\(K\)
bulk modulus, Pa (≈ 2.2–2.4 GPa, close to water)
\(E\)
Young's modulus, Pa
\(\nu\)
Poisson's ratio, dimensionless
\(\rho\)
mass density, ≈ 1000 kg/m³ (1050–1060 for liver and muscle)
Ultrasound travels on \(K\), elastography on \(\mu\). Because \(K/\mu \sim 10^{6}\), tissue is nearly incompressible and \(E = 3\mu\) to six digits: 1 m/s is 1 kPa of \(\mu\) and 3 kPa of \(E\); 2 m/s is 4 and 12 kPa; 3 m/s is 9 and 27 kPa.

How incompressible? Writing Poisson's ratio in terms of \(K\) and \(\mu\),

\[ \nu = \frac{3K - 2\mu}{2(3K+\mu)}, \qquad \frac12 - \nu = \frac{3\mu}{2(3K+\mu)} \approx \frac{\mu}{2K} \]
(30.2)
\(\nu\)
Poisson’s ratio, dimensionless
\(K,\ \mu\)
bulk and shear modulus, Pa, as in (30.1)
\(\approx\)
valid for \(\mu \ll K\), i.e. \(\nu \to 1/2\) (nearly incompressible tissue)
With \(\rho c_L^{2}\) = 2.37 GPa and \(\mu\) = 3 kPa, \(\tfrac12 - \nu = 6.3\times10^{-7}\) and \(E/\mu = 2.9999987\). Writing "\(\nu\) = 0.49" or "0.495" in a finite-element model of soft tissue is therefore not a small error: it makes the material \(10^{4}\) times too compressible and changes the static displacement field near boundaries.

Three consequences follow. First, shear waves are 300–1500 times slower than ultrasound, so a plane-wave imager at 5–10 kHz can film them: in 100 µs a 2 m/s wave moves 0.2 mm while the ultrasound crosses 15 cm. Second, shear wavelengths are long: \(\lambda_s = c_s/f\) is 4 mm at 500 Hz for 2 m/s, 40 mm at 50 Hz, compared with 0.31 mm for 5 MHz ultrasound. Spatial resolution of any speed map is limited by \(\lambda_s\) and by the estimation kernel, not by the B-mode resolution. Third, the conversion \(E = 3\rho c_s^{2}\) quietly assumes linear elasticity, isotropy, incompressibility, an unbounded medium and no dispersion. Every one of those can fail in tissue, and Sections 30.7–30.8 quantify how. That is why the SRU and QIBA recommend reporting shear-wave speed in m/s as the measured quantity and treating kPa as a derived, model-dependent number.

30.2Quasi-static strain imaging: displacement, its derivative and the strain filter

Ophir and colleagues (1991) compressed tissue by about 1 % with the probe, tracked the axial displacement between a pre- and a post-compression RF frame, and displayed its derivative. The tracking is the delay estimation of Module 11: an RF kernel of duration \(T\) is cross-correlated between frames and the lag of the peak is converted to displacement with the round-trip factor,

\[ u(z) = \frac{c\,\hat\tau(z)}{2}, \qquad \varepsilon(z) = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} \]
(30.3)
\(\hat\tau(z)\)
estimated echo delay change of the kernel centred at depth \(z\), s (negative when the tissue moves toward the probe)
\(u\)
axial displacement, m; one sample at 40 MHz is 19.25 µm
\(\varepsilon\)
axial strain, positive in compression
\(z\)
depth, m
\(c\)
longitudinal sound speed, m/s
\(\partial\)
partial derivative

Differentiation amplifies noise, so strain is never a two-point difference in practice. The standard estimator is a least-squares slope over \(N\) displacement estimates spaced \(\Delta\); for independent displacement errors of standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) its noise is

\[ \hat\varepsilon = -\frac{\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)(\hat u_i - \bar u)}{\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)^{2}}, \qquad \sigma_\varepsilon = \frac{\sigma_u}{\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{12}{N(N^{2}-1)}}, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_e = \frac{\bar\varepsilon}{\sigma_\varepsilon} \]
(30.4)
\(N\)
number of displacement estimates in the LSQ span
\(\Delta\)
spacing of the displacement estimates (kernel step), m
\(\sigma_u\)
displacement jitter, m (Walker–Trahey bound plus decorrelation, Module 11)
\(\mathrm{SNR}_e\)
elastographic SNR: mean strain over its standard deviation in a uniform region
\(z_i,\ \hat u_i\)
depth (m) and estimated displacement (m) of the \(i\)-th kernel
\(\bar z,\ \bar u\)
their means over the \(N\) kernels
\(\hat\varepsilon,\ \bar\varepsilon\)
least-squares strain estimate and its mean in the region
\(\sigma_\varepsilon\)
standard deviation of the strain estimate, dimensionless
\(N\) = 2 gives \(\sqrt2\,\sigma_u/\Delta\), the two-point rule. \(N\) = 9 gives \(\sqrt{12/720}\,\sigma_u/\Delta = 0.129\,\sigma_u/\Delta\); at \(\Delta\) = 0.25 mm: with \(\sigma_u\) = 0.5 µm the strain noise is 0.026 %, an SNR\(_e\) of 39 at 1 % strain. Overlapping kernels make the \(\hat u_i\) correlated, so the real gain is smaller than this formula.

The strain filter. \(\mathrm{SNR}_e\) is not monotonic in the applied strain. At small strain, \(\sigma_u\) is set by electronic noise and the Walker–Trahey floor, so \(\mathrm{SNR}_e\) grows in proportion to \(\varepsilon\). At large strain the kernel is compressed by \(\varepsilon T\) relative to its partner, the echoes decorrelate, \(\sigma_u\) grows and eventually peak-hopping errors appear. Plotting \(\mathrm{SNR}_e\) against \(\varepsilon\) gives a band-pass curve that Varghese and Ophir (1997) called the strain filter: every combination of kernel, bandwidth and SNR has a window of strains, typically 0.1–3 %, inside which the elastogram is usable. Between the two edges there is a ceiling set by speckle itself: a kernel's correlation peak tracks the amplitude-weighted centroid of its scatterers, which sits a random fraction of the kernel away from its centre, so under strain \(\varepsilon\) the displacement error is of order \(\varepsilon\) times that offset and \(\mathrm{SNR}_e\) stops growing. Temporal stretching of the post-compression frame by the mean strain (companding) moves the upper edge of that window up by realigning the echoes before correlation.

Normalization: why strain is not stiffness. The strain image is a stiffness image only if the stress is uniform. In a 1D layered medium under uniform stress the strain ratio equals the modulus ratio exactly. In 2D and 3D, stress concentrates in stiff inclusions and the strain contrast is compressed. For a circular inclusion in an incompressible matrix under plane strain (Eshelby's result for a cylinder),

\[ \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\varepsilon_{\infty}} = \frac{2}{1 + \mu_{\mathrm{inc}}/\mu_{\mathrm{bg}}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\varepsilon_{\infty}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inc}}} = \frac{1 + r}{2}, \qquad r = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{bg}}} \]
(30.5)
\(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{inc}},\ \varepsilon_\infty\)
uniform strain inside the inclusion and the remote background strain
\(r\)
shear-modulus contrast of the inclusion
\(\mu_{\mathrm{inc}},\ \mu_{\mathrm{bg}}\)
shear modulus of the inclusion and of the background, Pa
A lesion 4 times stiffer than its surroundings shows a strain ratio of 2.5, one 10 times stiffer a ratio of 5.5. The strain ratio is a monotonic but compressed, geometry-dependent proxy, which is why breast strain scores use it qualitatively and SWE took over quantification. Boundary conditions (probe size, depth, slip) add stress decay with depth that a strain image shows as apparent softening.

30.3Acoustic radiation force and ARFI imaging

A wave that is absorbed gives up momentum as well as energy. The power absorbed per unit volume from a plane wave of intensity \(I\) is \(2\alpha I\) (intensity decays as \(e^{-2\alpha z}\) with \(\alpha\) the amplitude coefficient), and dividing by \(c\) converts power to momentum flux. The result is a body force along the beam (Nyborg; Torr 1984; the radiation-force balance of Module 22 weighs the same momentum):

\[ F = \frac{2\alpha I}{c} \]
(30.6)
\(F\)
body force per unit volume along the beam, N/m³
\(\alpha\)
amplitude absorption coefficient, Np/m (1 Np = 8.686 dB); usually approximated by the attenuation coefficient, which overestimates \(F\) by the scattering fraction
\(I\)
temporal-average intensity during the push, W/m² (1 W/cm² = 10⁴ W/m²)
\(c\)
longitudinal sound speed, m/s
0.5 dB/cm/MHz at 4 MHz is 2 dB/cm = 23.0 Np/m. With \(I\) = 500 W/cm² in situ, \(F\) = 2 × 23.0 × 5×10⁶ / 1540 = 1.5×10⁵ N/m³. That intensity is about what a linear 4 MHz push at the regulatory MI of 1.9 delivers: \(p\) = 1.9 × √4 = 3.8 MPa, \(I = p^{2}/(2\rho c)\) = 470 W/cm².

The force is enormous per unit volume but acts on a column only about 0.8 mm wide and 5–10 mm long, for 30–1000 µs. What the tissue does with it depends on how the push duration \(T\) compares with the time a shear wave needs to leave the focal column, \(\sigma_r/c_s\) (≈ 0.2 ms for \(\sigma_r\) = 0.35 mm and 1.5 m/s). During a short push the tissue cannot react elastically and simply accelerates; for long pushes it approaches the static solution, in which the column is held by shear stresses:

\[ u(T) \approx \frac{F\,T^{2}}{2\rho}\ \ \left(T \ll \frac{\sigma_r}{c_s}\right), \qquad u_{\mathrm{static}}(\mathbf{0}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\int \frac{F(\mathbf{r}')}{\lvert\mathbf{r}'\rvert}\, d^{3}r' \ \ \left(T \gg \frac{\sigma_r}{c_s}\right) \]
(30.7)

\(\sigma_r\)

Short pushes: the displacement at the end of the push is set by inertia and is independent of stiffness (1.5×10⁵ N/m³ for 200 µs gives 3.0 µm); the column then coasts until shear stresses stop it, and that part does depend on stiffness. Long pushes: displacement \(\propto F\sigma_r\sigma_z/\mu\), inversely proportional to stiffness. In the model of Demo 30.2 (m30_arfi_displacement.m), quadrupling \(\mu\) (1.5 → 3 m/s) lowers the peak by a factor 1.77 for a 200 µs push (3.51 → 1.98 µm) and 2.82 for a 1 ms push (14.1 → 5.0 µm), against 4 in the static limit: peak displacement scales roughly as \(\mu^{-0.4}\) to \(\mu^{-0.75}\) for clinical push lengths, so ARFI displacement is a relative, not an absolute, stiffness measure.

500 W/cm², 23 Np/m, 200 µs: 0.012 K per push. Harmless once, but 2D SWE fires tens of pushes per image and refreshes at about 1 Hz; the time-averaged intensity (ISPTA) and the thermal index, not the MI, usually cap the push rate and the push length.

The ARFI sequence (Nightingale et al. 2002) consists of two or more reference tracking pulses on the push axis, the push (a long, focused, high-voltage burst from the imaging array), then tracking pulses at a PRF of 5–10 kHz for 3–10 ms, each correlated against the reference with the Kasai or Loupas estimator of Module 11. Displacements are 1–20 µm: 3–70 times the jitter floor of ≈ 0.3 µm (≈ 0.1 µm at high SNR), so the measurement itself is easy. The hard parts are elsewhere. The first 0.2–0.5 ms after the push are lost (transducer ring-down, reverberation and, for single-element tracking, the push and track beams share the aperture). The force is unknown in absolute terms, because \(\alpha\) and the in-situ \(I\) are unknown, so ARFI displacement maps are relative, and the depth dependence of \(I\) (focal gain, attenuation) must be normalized out. And the push excites shear waves, which is exactly what Section 30.4 uses.

Choosing the push frequency. For a fixed source intensity and \(\alpha = a f\), the force at depth \(z\) scales as \(f\,e^{-2afz}\), maximal at \(2afz = 1\): with \(a\) = 0.5 dB/cm/MHz (0.0576 Np/cm/MHz) at 4 cm, \(f^{*}\) = 2.2 MHz. A fixed aperture adds focal gain \(\propto f^{2}\), moving the optimum to \(3/(2az)\) = 6.5 MHz. Real systems push at 2.5–5 MHz, where transducer efficiency, heating of the lens and the MI limit also enter.

30.4Making shear waves: supersonic pushes, external vibrators and harmonic fields

A single push launches a shear wave that spreads cylindrically from the focal column (amplitude \(\propto r^{-1/2}\)) and spherically beyond its ends (\(\propto r^{-1}\)), so it is usable only over a few millimetres of depth and 5–10 mm laterally. Supersonic shear imaging (Bercoff, Tanter and Fink 2004) fires pushes at successive depths faster than the shear wave can leave them. The source moves along the beam axis at speed \(V\), the cylindrical wavelets interfere constructively on a Mach cone, and two quasi-plane shear waves leave it, tilted symmetrically about the lateral axis:

\[ M = \frac{V}{c_s} = \frac{\Delta z / T_p}{c_s}, \qquad \sin\varphi = \frac{1}{M}, \qquad c_{x,\mathrm{app}} = \frac{c_s}{\cos\varphi} \]
(30.9)
\(V\)
source (push) speed along the beam: focal step \(\Delta z\) per push duration \(T_p\), m/s
\(M\)
Mach number, must exceed 1
\(\varphi\)
half-angle of the cone; also the tilt of the wavefront normal from the lateral axis
\(c_{x,\mathrm{app}}\)
apparent speed measured by a purely lateral time-of-flight estimator
\(c_s\)
shear speed, as in (30.1)
2 mm steps of 100 µs give \(V\) = 20 m/s; in 2 m/s tissue \(M\) = 10 and \(\varphi\) = 5.7°, so the waves are nearly lateral and the lateral TOF bias \(1/\cos\varphi - 1\) is 0.5 %. An ultrafast plane-wave imager (Module 23) then films the field at 2–20 kHz. One frame of compounding costs only a few tilted transmits, so the whole wavefield \(u(x, z, t)\) is captured from one push sequence, and pushes on the left and right of the region are combined to fill the shadow behind each source. Variants trade the one Mach cone for several simultaneous beams (comb push; Song et al. 2012) or for pushes outside the imaging plane.

Transient elastography (Sandrin et al. 2003, FibroScan) does not use radiation force. A piston on the probe tip delivers one cycle at 50 Hz, a single-element transducer coaxial with it tracks displacement along one A-line at kHz rate, and the slope of the displacement front in the depth–time plane gives the speed, reported as \(E = 3\rho c_s^{2}\) in kPa. The 50 Hz excitation means \(\lambda_s\) = 20–60 mm, comparable to the measurement depth window (a few centimetres, 25–65 mm for the standard adult probe): the measurement happens in the near field of a finite source, where compressional and shear components mix and diffraction biases the apparent speed. Calibration against phantoms and fixed depth windows keep it reproducible; it is not a bulk-wave measurement in the textbook sense, and its kPa are not interchangeable with SWE kPa at 200–500 Hz in dispersive tissue.

\[ E_{\mathrm{TE}} = 3\rho\left(\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}\right)^{2}, \qquad \lambda_s = \frac{c_s}{f} = \frac{2\ \mathrm{m/s}}{50\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 40\ \mathrm{mm} \]
(30.10)

Harmonic (continuous) methods drive the tissue at one or a few frequencies until a steady state forms, then measure the phase of the displacement at that frequency. MR elastography (Muthupillai et al. 1995) and ultrasound reverberant-field and crawling-wave methods are in this family. With a steady state the natural estimator is the local wavenumber \(k = \partial\varphi/\partial x\) or a Helmholtz inversion of the full field (Section 30.6), and the frequency is known by construction, which removes the phase–group ambiguity of Section 30.7. The drawback is standing waves and reflections, which is why directional filtering was adopted early in MRE (Manduca et al. 2003) before transient shear-wave elastography.

30.5Speed estimation I: time of flight, time to peak and cross-correlation

The tracked data are a space–time matrix \(u(x_i, t_n)\) at one depth: \(N\) lateral positions spaced \(\Delta x\), sampled at the tracking PRF or plane-wave frame rate. Which speed? In an elastic, non-dispersive medium every estimator returns \(c_s\). In viscoelastic tissue the wave is dispersive (Section 30.7) and each estimator returns a different weighted average: TTP of a pulse follows the group velocity at the dominant frequency of the trace it is applied to, or more (the peak of particle velocity marks the steepest rise of the displacement, which the faster high-frequency content carries); cross-correlation sits closer to the phase velocity at that frequency; and the dominant frequency of particle velocity is higher than that of displacement (multiplying by \(j\omega\) tilts the spectrum up). So "TTP on displacement" (Palmeri et al. 2008; with RANSAC outlier rejection, Wang et al. 2010) and "TOF on particle velocity" (SSI) disagree in dispersive tissue by a few percent to over ten percent, for reasons that have nothing to do with noise. The QIBA phantom studies found exactly such system-to-system spreads in viscoelastic phantoms. Robust variants (Radon-sum TOF, Rouze et al. 2010; RANSAC line fits; bidirectional fits from left and right pushes) improve precision but do not change which speed is measured.

Practical constraints. The trace must be recorded long enough to contain the whole pulse at the farthest \(x\) (10 mm at 1 m/s is 10 ms), the frame rate must sample the pulse (10 kHz gives 20 samples per period at 500 Hz) and the region next to the push (≈ 1–2 mm) is excluded, because the source is not a line and the near field is not a travelling wave.

30.7Viscoelasticity and dispersion: phase velocity, group velocity and the model behind the kPa

Tissue stores and dissipates energy. In the frequency domain this replaces \(\mu\) by a complex modulus \(\mu^{*}(\omega)\), the wavenumber becomes complex, and speed and attenuation become functions of frequency. \[ \mu^{*} = \mu + j\omega\eta, \quad k = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\mu^{*}}}, \quad c_{\mathrm{ph}}(\omega) = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}\eta^{2})}{\rho\left(\mu + \sqrt{\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}\eta^{2}}\right)}}, \quad \alpha(\omega) = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho\left(\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}\eta^{2}} - \mu\right)}{2\,(\mu^{2}+\omega^{2}\eta^{2})}} \]

30.9From signal to clinical number: protocol, conversion, quality and cut-offs

A liver stiffness result is not one shear-wave measurement. It is the median of several independent acquisitions, each a speed estimated inside a small ROI and screened by a vendor quality metric, converted to kPa with (30.1) and read against cut-offs that belong to one class of technique. Every link of that chain can move the result by as much as the gap between two fibrosis categories, which is why the guidelines regulate the procedure and not only the thresholds. What follows uses the SRU consensus update (Barr et al. 2020) and the WFUMB 2024 update (Ferraioli et al. 2024); Figure 30.C draws the chain.

The patient fasts for at least 4 hours, because a meal raises portal flow and with it the stiffness, and rests for about 10 minutes, because exercise does the same. The patient lies supine or turned slightly to the left (no more than 30°; a full left lateral decubitus reads higher) with the right arm behind the head to open the intercostal spaces. The operator scans the right lobe through an intercostal window whose B-mode image is free of rib and lung shadowing, holds the transducer perpendicular to the capsule, and asks for a short breath-hold at neutral breathing, since deep inspiration also raises the value. The ROI goes 15–20 mm below the capsule, out of the reverberation and guided-wave zone of Section 30.8, and away from large vessels and bile ducts. The push works best about 4–4.5 cm from the transducer, and attenuation makes shear-wave generation unreliable beyond about 6–7 cm, which is the practical limit in obese patients. Each acquisition comes from a fresh image at the same location, so the replicates are independent.

Inside one acquisition the software runs Sections 30.5–30.6: it tracks the push-induced displacement across the ROI, fits arrival times or a phase velocity, and returns one speed together with a quality indicator (a propagation map, a reliability index or the residual of the time-of-flight fit) that the operator uses to reject failed acquisitions. The exam value is the median of the \(N\) accepted ones. The SRU statement asks for 10 with point SWE and accepts fewer with 2D SWE when a quality map is shown; the WFUMB update asks for 5–10 with point SWE (5 for experienced operators), 3–5 with 2D SWE and 10 with transient elastography. The conversion itself carries the assumptions of Section 30.1 (elastic, isotropic, incompressible, unbounded, non-dispersive) plus a density that every clinical system fixes at 1000 kg/m³. Liver is nearer 1050–1060 kg/m³, so the displayed kPa understate \(\rho c^{2}\) by 5–6 %, but the published cut-offs were derived with the same convention, so consistency matters more than physical truth: a system that "corrected" \(\rho\) would move every patient 5–6 % across the thresholds. Relative errors add as \(\delta E/E = \delta\rho/\rho + 2\,\delta c/c\). The guidelines allow either unit as long as it is stated, and QIBA treats m/s as the measured quantity. Two units traps are common. An MR elastography "liver stiffness" in kPa is a shear stiffness, of order \(\rho c^{2}\), so about one third of the ultrasound Young's-modulus number for the same speed before any frequency effect. And a paper that reports \(\mu = \rho c^{2}\) in kPa is on the same one-third scale.

Why two scales? The WFUMB update states that different systems give different values in the same patient, that ARFI-based values are lower than transient-elastography values with a gap that widens at higher stages, and that follow-up must use the same system, named in the report. Dispersion explains part of the spread between ARFI systems. In a power-law liver with \(n\) = 0.2, \(c \propto f^{0.1}\) (30.18), so a system whose shear-wave spectrum centres at 400 Hz reads 10 % faster, 22 % stiffer in kPa, than one centred at 150 Hz, before any estimator difference (Section 30.5). The QIBA phantom study measured this directly: 95 % limits of the between-system speed difference of ±9.6 % in elastic and ±15.3 % in viscoelastic phantoms, and an MR elastography system that matched the ultrasound speeds with a 140 Hz drive but read 12.2 % low at its clinical 60 Hz; system and site mattered more than focal depth or operator (Palmeri et al. 2021). A power law with \(n \approx\) 0.3 reproduces that 60/140 Hz ratio. Dispersion alone would make 50 Hz transient elastography read lower than the ARFI systems, yet it reads higher, so the near-field geometry of Section 30.4, the different depth window, its own calibration and estimator must outweigh it. The two scales are related empirically, not by a formula, and their cut-offs do not transfer. For a pipeline engineer the corollary is an error budget with five lines: estimator bias in dispersive media, guided-wave and boundary effects, reflection bias without directional filtering, noise (30.11), and the conversion itself.

Finally, stiffness is not fibrosis. Anything that raises the volume or pressure of blood or bile in the liver raises the speed without adding collagen: a recent meal, exercise, deep inspiration, congestion from right heart failure (the Fontan circulation is the extreme case), cholestasis, acute hepatitis or a transaminase flare, infiltrative disease and alcohol binges (Ferraioli et al. 2024). The protocol controls the first three; the rest are why the rule-out threshold carries the proviso "without other clinical signs" and why the report is read with the clinical context.

30.11How experts think about it

An elastography expert separates three layers and never lets them blur: the excitation (what force, where, for how long, at what frequency content), the motion estimate (a delay estimator with known bias and jitter, Module 11), and the inversion (which wave model turns arrival times or wavenumbers into a speed, and which material model turns the speed into kPa). Most published disagreements trace to the third layer: dispersion, guided modes, reflections and anisotropy change the speed that a given estimator reports, while the noise budget is usually the smaller problem.

30.13Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why can a scanner whose pulses travel at 1540 m/s measure tissue stiffness at all? · screen

Because it does not measure stiffness with its own pulses: it uses them as a motion sensor. Compressional speed depends on \(K + 4\mu/3\) with \(K/\mu \sim 10^{6}\), so it is insensitive to \(\mu\). Elastography deforms the tissue (compression, radiation force, vibration) and tracks the displacement with speckle tracking at kHz rates; the shear wave moves at 1–10 m/s, about 1000 times slower, so each frame freezes it. Follow-up: what frame rate? Several samples per shear period: 5–10 kHz for 200–800 Hz content.

I2Convert 1.8 m/s to kPa and list every assumption you just made. · screen

\(E = 3\rho c^{2}\) = 3 × 1000 × 3.24 = 9.7 kPa (μ = 3.24 kPa). Assumptions: linear elastic (no dispersion, so phase = group), isotropic, incompressible (ν = ½), unbounded (no guided modes), ρ = 1000 kg/m³, and a plane shear wave measured along its propagation direction. Follow-up: which fails first in liver? Dispersion (viscosity), then near-capsule effects; in muscle, anisotropy; in arteries, guided waves.

I3Derive the radiation force and estimate the displacement of a 200 µs push. · onsite

Power absorbed per volume \(2\alpha I\), divided by \(c\) for momentum flux: \(F = 2\alpha I/c\). At 4 MHz, 0.5 dB/cm/MHz (23 Np/m), 500 W/cm²: 1.5×10⁵ N/m³. During a short push the column accelerates at \(F/\rho\) = 150 m/s², so \(u(T) = FT^{2}/2\rho\) = 3.0 µm; it coasts a little after (3.5 µm peak in the axisymmetric model at 1.5 m/s). Follow-up: heating, 2αIT/ρC = 12 mK per push; and why the in-situ force is unknown (α, derating, focal gain).

I4Your strain images look great at 1 % compression and terrible at 5 %. Why, and what do you change? · onsite

The strain filter: at 5 % the kernel is compressed by εT relative to its partner (0.1 mm over a 2 mm kernel, a third of a wavelength at 5 MHz), the echoes decorrelate and peak hopping starts. Fixes: global or adaptive companding (stretch the post frame by the expected strain), shorter kernels, multi-step (accumulate small inter-frame strains), guided search and outlier rejection. Follow-up: and at 0.05 %? Then jitter dominates; use longer kernels, larger LSQ span or more compression.

I5Explain supersonic shear imaging. Why does the Mach number matter for estimation? · onsite

Pushes are fired at successive depths faster than the shear wave can leave, so the source moves at \(V > c_s\) and the wavelets add on a Mach cone, giving two quasi-plane waves of larger amplitude and depth extent than one push. The fronts are tilted by \(\varphi = \arcsin(1/M)\); a lateral-only TOF reads \(c_s/\cos\varphi\): 0.5 % high at M = 10, 15.5 % at M = 2. Follow-up: how to fix? Higher M, 2D direction estimation, or directional filters.

I6TTP regression, cross-correlation or k–f: which would you put in a product, and why? · onsite

Depends on the claim. For a robust single number in liver (pSWE), TTP or cross-correlation regression with outlier rejection (RANSAC, Radon sum): cheap, short kernels, stable at low SNR; XC is more precise than TTP because it uses the whole trace. For maps, local XC between x ± Δ or local phase gradient with directional filtering. For viscosity or thin structures, k–f phase velocity, because it gives \(c(f)\) and lets you fit a model. And whichever it is, specify the frequency band, because in dispersive tissue they disagree (1.85 / 1.55 / 1.53 m/s in m30_shear_wave_tof_kf.m). Follow-up: how do you validate? Viscoelastic phantoms plus digital phantoms through your exact pipeline.

I7Derive the Kelvin–Voigt phase velocity and explain why group velocity exceeds it. · onsite

\(\mu^{*} = \mu + j\omega\eta\), \(k = \omega\sqrt{\rho/\mu^{*}}\); write \(\mu^{*} = \lvert\mu^{*}\rvert e^{j\theta}\), Re k = \(\omega\sqrt{\rho/\lvert\mu^{*}\rvert}\cos(\theta/2)\), and \(\cos(\theta/2) = \sqrt{(\lvert\mu^{*}\rvert+\mu)/2\lvert\mu^{*}\rvert}\) gives (30.16). Since \(c_{\mathrm{ph}}\) rises with ω, \(d\omega/dk > \omega/k\): \(c_g = c_{\mathrm{ph}}/(1 - (\omega/c)\,dc/d\omega)\). Numbers: 1.5 m/s, 1 Pa·s, 200 Hz: 1.66 and 2.01 m/s. Follow-up: why do people prefer power laws? KV disperses as √ω at high frequency, much faster than tissue.

I8Your 2D SWE map shows a bright halo and stripes upstream of a stiff lesion. Diagnose. · research

Reflections from the lesion boundary: the reflected wave travels back through the background, superposes on the incident one and biases arrival times or phases (stripes at half-wavelength spacing upstream), while the halo is the kernel straddling the boundary (partial volume over 2Δ). Fix with directional filtering in k–f (or angular sectors in 2D), smaller kernels near edges, and compounding of left and right pushes; check the residual with a simulated inclusion run through the exact pipeline. Follow-up: cost of the filter? Edge smearing over about a wavelength and distortion of near-field components.

I9How would you measure the stiffness of the carotid wall with SWE? · research

Not with \(3\rho c^{2}\): the 0.6–1 mm wall carries guided (Lamb, tube) modes. Push in the wall, track along it at high frame rate, compute the k–f spectrum, extract \(c_{\mathrm{ph}}(f)\), and fit a plate or tube model: the immersed-plate fit \(c^{2} = \omega h c_t/(2\sqrt3)\) (Couade et al. 2010) in the high-frequency part, or the full leaky-Lamb or tube dispersion. Measure thickness from B-mode, synchronize with the ECG because pressure changes \(c_t\), and report the band. Follow-up: error from thickness? \(c_t \propto c^{2}/h\), so a 10 % thickness error is a 10 % \(c_t\) and 21 % \(E\) error.

I10Two vendors measure the same patient at 1.45 and 1.62 m/s. Is one of them wrong? · research

Not necessarily. 12 % is the size of the dispersion effects in liver: different push durations and tracking bands sample different frequencies, TTP on displacement versus TOF on particle velocity weights different spectra, ROI depth and focal configuration differ, and kPa conversion doubles any gap. The QIBA phantom work found exactly such system dependence growing in viscoelastic phantoms. The right comparison is c(f) over a stated band, ideally from a k–f or spectroscopy method, plus a phantom cross-calibration. Follow-up: what would you put in the 510(k) bench testing? Elastic and viscoelastic phantoms over depth, with the estimator's frequency response stated.

I11Where do elastography, sound-speed estimation and aberration correction meet? · research

All three are delay estimation problems on the same channel data. Sound-speed errors defocus the tracking beams and bias the displacement estimates (and the depth scale, so the measured shear speed scales with the assumed c). Aberration lowers tracking SNR and correlation, raising jitter. Conversely, a measured SoS map (Module 32) and an aberration correction (Module 33) improve tracking, and steatosis raises attenuation and lowers SoS while it changes shear dispersion, so multiparametric QUS + SWE models share one forward model. Follow-up: how much does a 2 % c error change the reported shear speed? Lateral distances are set by the array geometry and frame times by the clock, so to first order little; depth registration of the ROI shifts by 2 %.

I12A hepatologist asks why your 2D SWE result of 11 kPa does not match the patient’s FibroScan value of 14 kPa from last month. Answer. · onsite

They are different scales, not one of them wrong. ARFI-based systems read lower than transient elastography, with a gap that grows with stage, which is why the cut-offs differ (rule of four 5/9/13/17 kPa against rule of five 5/10/15/20/25 kPa); both results sit in their own grey zone. Physics does not give a conversion: transient elastography works at 50 Hz in the near field of its piston with its own depth window and calibration, ARFI systems at 100–500 Hz with different estimators, and dispersion alone would predict the opposite sign. Recommend follow-up on one system, named in the report, with the median, IQR/M and unit. Follow-up: what else could raise the FibroScan value? A recent meal, congestion, a transaminase flare or cholestasis; check the exam conditions before calling progression.

I13Why do the guidelines report the median and IQR/M, and why is the limit 15 % in m/s but 30 % in kPa? · screen

The median is robust to the occasional failed acquisition, and because \(3\rho c^{2}\) is monotonic it commutes with the conversion, so the kPa result does not depend on where the median is taken; a mean would not (\(\overline{3\rho c^{2}} = 3\rho(\bar c^{2} + \sigma_c^{2})\)). For quartiles \(\hat c(1 \mp q/2)\) the kPa ratio is \((1+q/2)^{2} - (1-q/2)^{2} = 2q\) exactly, so 15 % in speed is 30 % in modulus. The limit also bounds precision: IQR/M = 15 % means an acquisition CV near 11 %, and a median of 10 then has about 4 % speed and 9 % kPa standard error (30.23). Follow-up: is a 30 % spread small? About 1 kPa of standard error at 12 kPa, a quarter of the 9–13 kPa grey zone, so borderline values deserve a repeat.

Q1A shear wave travels at 2.5 m/s. What are μ and E?

Q4TTP regression over 8 mm with 33 traces, σt = 100 µs, c = 1.5 m/s. Relative precision (independent errors)?

Δx = 0.25 mm; \(\sigma_c/c = (c\sigma_t/\Delta x)\sqrt{12/(N(N^{2}-1))}\) = 0.6 × √(12/35 904) = 1.1 %.

Q5KV tissue with μ = 4 kPa and η = 2 Pa·s is measured by k–f at 300 Hz. What speed, and what E would a naive conversion report?

x = ωη/μ = 0.94; \(c_{\mathrm{ph}} = c_0\sqrt{2(1+x^{2})/(1+\sqrt{1+x^{2}})}\) = 2 × 1.261 = 2.52 m/s; \(3\rho c^{2}\) = 19.1 kPa against 12 kPa elastic. The excess is viscosity, not stiffness.

Q6A 0.8 mm arterial wall in water shows a 3 m/s phase velocity at 500 Hz. Estimate ct and E with the immersed-plate fit.

\(c_t = c^{2}\,2\sqrt3/(\omega h)\) = 9 × 3.464 / (3142 × 0.0008) = 12.4 m/s; \(E = 3\rho c_t^{2}\) ≈ 460 kPa. A bulk conversion of 3 m/s would have said 27 kPa.

λ = 5 mm; need \(2k\Delta < \pi\), i.e. Δ < λ/4 = 1.25 mm. At Δ = 1 mm the phase step is 2π·2/5 = 2.51 rad, already close to π with noise.
