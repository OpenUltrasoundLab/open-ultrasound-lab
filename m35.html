Image enhancement: speckle, denoising, contrast and 3D post-processing

Every scanner ships an enhancement chain between the beamformer and the screen: speckle reduction, edge and contrast enhancement, temporal filtering, compounding and, for volumes, rendering. Each block trades variance against resolution, texture against smoothness, or fidelity against looks. This module treats that chain as an estimation problem with stated assumptions, measures every trade on one phantom, and ends with the workflow that turns filters into a clinical image look you can defend.

You will be able to

  • Place an enhancement step at the right point of the chain (radio-frequency (RF) or in-phase/quadrature (IQ) data, envelope, log, scan-converted) and say what it can and cannot fix there
  • Write down the assumptions behind Lee, Kuan, Frost, median, anisotropic diffusion, speckle-reducing anisotropic diffusion (SRAD), bilateral, non-local means (NLM), wavelet, guided and block-matching 3D (BM3D) filters, and predict how each fails
  • Quantify every filter with the same five numbers: generalized contrast-to-noise ratio (gCNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), speckle signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), edge-spread width and point-target full width at half maximum (FWHM), and explain why peak SNR (PSNR) and the structural similarity index (SSIM) mislead for speckle
  • Design multiscale contrast, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) and temporal filters without halos, banding or motion smear
  • Denoise and render ultrasound volumes: maximum intensity projection (MIP), emission–absorption ray casting, transfer functions, gradient shading and shadows
  • Evaluate a deep-learning denoiser for hallucination, distribution shift and regulatory change control, and run the parameter-sweep, blinded-review and regression workflow behind a preset

35.1Where enhancement sits in the pipeline

Enhancement is not one block. It can act on the complex beamformed data, on the detected envelope, on the log-compressed image, or on the scan-converted display grid, and the same algorithm behaves differently at each point because the noise model changes. Write the chain as a sequence of signals:

\[ z(\mathbf{x}) = (h * s)(\mathbf{x}) + n(\mathbf{x}), \qquad A = \lvert z\rvert, \qquad D = 20\log_{10}\frac{A}{A_{\mathrm{ref}}}, \qquad g = \mathcal{M}\big(\mathcal{S}(D)\big) \]

Four places to insert an enhancement operator: on \(z\) (linear, phase available), on \(A\) or \(A^2\) (speckle is multiplicative with known statistics), on \(D\) (speckle is additive with a 5.57 dB spread), or after \(\mathcal{S}\) (pixels are interpolated and correlated, values are display-referenced).

Intuition

The further downstream, the cheaper and the more "visual" the processing, and the less it knows about the physics. A filter on the scan-converted grid never sees speckle as speckle: it sees an interpolated, clipped, gray-mapped texture whose grain size changes with depth.

Key takeaways
  • The same filter sees a different noise model at each stage: complex Gaussian on IQ, multiplicative speckle on the envelope (contrast 0.523), additive 5.57 dB speckle on the log image, interpolated and possibly clipped values after scan conversion.
  • Downstream stages are cheaper and more visual but know less physics: on the display grid the speckle grain changes with depth, so a fixed window acts differently across the image.
  • Write the per-pixel cost and the latency next to every proposal: direct non-local means at 512 × 512 pixels and 60 Hz is about 347 G operations/s, 55 G with integral images (35.2).

35.2Speckle: signal or noise?

Speckle is interference of many sub-resolution scatterers inside the PSF (Module 26). It is deterministic for a fixed probe position and fixed tissue, and random only across tissue realizations. Whether it is "noise" depends on the task: for the echogenicity map \(R(\mathbf{x})\) (lesion detection, boundaries) it is noise; for speckle tracking, elastography, flow and tissue characterization it is the signal.

\[ I(\mathbf{x}) = R(\mathbf{x})\, S(\mathbf{x}), \quad \mathrm{E}S = 1,\ \ \mathrm{var}\,S = 1 \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad 10\log_{10}I = 10\log_{10}R + 10\log_{10}S, \quad \mathrm{E}\,[10\log_{10}S] = -2.51\ \mathrm{dB},\ \ \mathrm{std} = 5.57\ \mathrm{dB} \]
(35.3)
\(I\)
intensity \(A^2\) at \(\mathbf{x}\)
\(R\)
local mean backscatter (echogenicity), the quantity most enhancement tries to estimate
\(S\)
speckle: unit-mean exponential for fully developed speckle (Module 26, (26.2))
\(\mathrm{E},\ \mathrm{var}\)

The model holds where many scatterers per resolution cell give circular Gaussian \(z\). The log turns the multiplicative speckle into an additive term whose distribution does not depend on \(R\): mean \(-4.343\gamma\) = −2.51 dB and std \(4.343\pi/\sqrt6\) = 5.57 dB (Module 26, (26.3)), which is why most "additive Gaussian" denoisers are run on log data. The log term is not Gaussian: it is skewed with a long dark tail.
\[ S_L \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\!\left(L, \tfrac{1}{L}\right): \quad C_I = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}, \qquad \mathrm{std}\,(10\log_{10}S_L) = 4.343\sqrt{\psi_1(L)}, \qquad \mathrm{ENL} = \frac{\langle I\rangle^2}{\mathrm{var}\,I} \]
(35.4)

\(C_I\)

Log-speckle std: 5.57 dB (\(L\) = 1), 3.49 (2), 2.31 (4), 1.58 (8), 1.10 dB (16). The mean bias shrinks too: −2.51, −1.17, −0.57, −0.28, −0.14 dB. ENL is how synthetic-aperture radar quantifies a despeckling filter; for ultrasound the same number is \((\mathrm{SNR}_0^{\mathrm{int}})^2\).

Speckle carries information that clinicians and algorithms use. Its first-order statistics (Rayleigh, Rician, K, Nakagami; Module 26) are quantitative-ultrasound (QUS) biomarkers; its texture is what speckle tracking and elastography correlate; its grain size reports the local PSF. Smoothing it away destroys those uses, and a smooth image that "looks like CT" invites readers to see boundaries the data cannot support. At a minimum, compute any quantitative parameter on data upstream of the enhancement chain, and label enhanced images as such.

Any linear smoother trades variance for resolution, and the speckle correlation area sets the exchange rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{var}\,\bar I_W}{\langle I\rangle^2} &= \frac{1}{\lvert W\rvert^2}\iint_W \lvert\mu(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')\rvert^2\, d\mathbf{x}\, d\mathbf{x}' \;\le\; \frac{A_c}{\lvert W\rvert}, \qquad A_c = \int \lvert\mu\rvert^2\, d\mathbf{x} \\ \mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{out}}^2 &\approx \mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{in}}^2 + \mathrm{FWHM}_w^2 \end{aligned} \]
(35.5)
\(\bar I_W\)
intensity averaged over window \(W\)
\(\lvert W\rvert\)
window area, mm²
\(\mu\)
normalized field autocorrelation of the PSF (Module 26, (26.8))
\(A_c\)
speckle correlation area, mm² (Gaussian amplitude PSF of std \(\sigma_x, \sigma_z\): \(2\pi\sigma_x\sigma_z\))
\(\mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{in}},\ \mathrm{FWHM}_{\mathrm{out}},\ \mathrm{FWHM}_w\)
widths of the PSF before and after smoothing and of the smoothing kernel (exact for Gaussian shapes)
The variance falls with the number of speckle cells \(\lvert W\rvert/A_c\) in the window, not with the number of pixels; the ratio of the two sides tends to 1 once the window spans many cells. On the 0.1 mm grid of the demos (speckle cell about 2.3 × 5.2 pixels, \(A_c \approx\) 13 px²) a 5 × 5 mean filter holds 1.9 cells and raises the intensity speckle SNR by a factor of 1.85 (Gaussian PSF), not 5; an 11 × 11 window gives 3.4. Every linear filter sits on the same curve; nonlinear filters try to move off it by smoothing only where \(R\) is flat.
Pitfall

"Speckle-free" is not a goal; it is a claim that the filter estimated \(R(\mathbf{x})\) correctly everywhere. Where the filter is wrong (an edge it did not see, a small lesion below its scale) the error is invisible precisely because the texture that would have revealed it is gone.

Key takeaways
  • Speckle is noise for the echogenicity map and signal for speckle tracking, elastography and QUS: compute those upstream of enhancement and label enhanced images as such.
  • Log speckle has mean −2.51 dB and std 5.57 dB for one look; averaging L independent looks gives 3.49, 2.31 and 1.58 dB for L = 2, 4, 8 (35.4).
  • Linear smoothing reduces variance by the number of speckle cells in the window (35.5): on a 2.3 × 5.2 pixel cell, 5 × 5 pixels gives ×1.85 in speckle SNR and 11 × 11 gives ×3.4.

35.3Classical spatial filters and their assumptions

Most speckle filters come from synthetic-aperture radar (SAR), which faced fully developed speckle twenty years before ultrasound adopted digital post-processing. They share one idea: estimate \(R\) by averaging, but average only over pixels that plausibly share the same \(R\). They differ in how they decide that.

Local-statistics filters: Lee, Kuan, Frost

Take the multiplicative model (35.3) in a window around each pixel and ask for the linear minimum-mean-square-error (LMMSE) estimate of \(R\). With the window mean \(\bar I\) and the local contrast \(C_I = \sigma_I/\bar I\):

\[ \hat R = \bar I + W\,(I - \bar I), \qquad W_{\mathrm{Lee}} = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{C_u^2}{C_I^2}\right), \qquad W_{\mathrm{Kuan}} = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{1 - C_u^2/C_I^2}{1 + C_u^2}\right) \]
(35.6)
\(\hat R\)
filtered value (estimate of the local mean)
\(\bar I,\ \sigma_I\)
mean and std in a window around the pixel
\(C_I\)
local contrast \(\sigma_I/\bar I\)
\(C_u\)
speckle contrast of a homogeneous region: 1 (single-look intensity), \(\sqrt{4/\pi - 1}\) = 0.523 (envelope), \(1/\sqrt{L}\) (\(L\) looks)
\(W\)
weight: 0 = window mean, 1 = unchanged pixel
Derivation: for \(I = RS\) with \(S\) independent of \(R\), \(\mathrm{cov}(R, I) = \mathrm{var}\,R\) and \(\mathrm{var}\,I = (1 + C_u^2)\mathrm{var}\,R + \bar R^2 C_u^2\), so \(W = \mathrm{var}\,R/\mathrm{var}\,I\) gives Kuan's exact LMMSE weight (Kuan et al. 1985). Lee (1980) linearizes \(I \approx \bar R S + (R - \bar R)\), which drops the \(1 + C_u^2\) factor. In a homogeneous region \(C_I \to C_u\) and \(W \to 0\); at an edge \(C_I \gg C_u\) and \(W \to 1\). The window must be large enough for \(C_I\) to be estimated: its sampling spread is what leaves speckle "salt" in flat regions.
\[ \hat R(\mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in W} m(\mathbf{x})\, I(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in W} m(\mathbf{x})}, \qquad m(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\big(-K\, C_I^2(\mathbf{x}_0)\, \lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\rVert\big) \]
(35.7)
\(m\)
Frost kernel weight
\(K\)
damping factor (user parameter)
\(C_I(\mathbf{x}_0)\)
local contrast at the centre pixel, as in (35.6)
\(\lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_0\rVert\)
distance in pixels
Frost et al. (1982): low local contrast gives a flat kernel (mean filter), high contrast a kernel narrowed toward the centre pixel. Implementations differ in using \(C_I\) or \(C_I^2\) and in normalization; the "enhanced" Lee and Frost filters add a lower and an upper threshold on \(C_I\) (Lopes et al. 1990). All three filters assume a single, known \(C_u\); log-compressed, clipped or interpolated data violate that, and then \(C_u\) becomes a tuning knob.

Median and order statistics

The median of a window has a 50% breakdown point: it removes isolated outliers and preserves step edges wider than half the window, but it rounds corners, deletes lines thinner than half the window, and on speckle it shifts the level (the median of an exponential intensity is \(\ln 2\) = 0.69 of its mean, −1.59 dB). Adaptive weighted medians (Loupas, McDicken and Allan 1989) give the centre more weight when the local contrast is high, the order-statistics analogue of the Lee weight.

Anisotropic diffusion

Perona and Malik (1990) replaced the heat equation, which is Gaussian smoothing, by a diffusion whose conductance falls where the gradient is large:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\big(g(\lvert\nabla u\rvert)\,\nabla u\big), \qquad g_1(s) = e^{-(s/\kappa)^2}, \quad g_2(s) = \frac{1}{1 + (s/\kappa)^2}, \qquad \phi(s) = s\,g(s) \]
(35.8)
\(u\)
image being diffused (usually the log image)
\(t\)
diffusion time (iterations × time step)
\(g\)
conductance, 1 for small gradients, → 0 for large ones
\(\kappa\)
edge threshold, units of \(\lvert\nabla u\rvert\) (dB per pixel on a log image)
\(\phi\)
flux magnitude across a 1D edge of slope \(s\)
\(\phi\) rises to a maximum at \(s = \kappa\) (for \(g_2\); \(\kappa/\sqrt2\) for \(g_1\)) and falls beyond it. Gradients below the maximum are smoothed; steeper ones are sharpened (backward diffusion in 1D). Speckle on a log image has gradients of several dB per pixel, the same order as real edges, which is why plain Perona–Malik either leaves speckle or erodes edges, and why it needs presmoothing of the gradient or a speckle-aware conductance.
\[ u^{n+1}_{\mathbf{p}} = u^{n}_{\mathbf{p}} + \Delta t \sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{N}_4(\mathbf{p})} g\big(\lvert u^n_{\mathbf{q}} - u^n_{\mathbf{p}}\rvert\big)\,\big(u^n_{\mathbf{q}} - u^n_{\mathbf{p}}\big), \qquad \Delta t \le \tfrac14\ \ (2\mathrm{D}),\ \ \tfrac16\ \ (3\mathrm{D}) \]
(35.9)
\(u^n_{\mathbf{p}}\)
value at pixel \(\mathbf{p}\) after \(n\) iterations
\(\mathcal{N}_4\)
the four nearest neighbours (six in 3D)
\(\Delta t\)
time step per iteration, pixel² units
With \(0 \le g \le 1\), the bound on \(\Delta t\) keeps every update a convex combination of the pixel and its neighbours, so no new maxima or minima appear (discrete maximum principle). The result depends on \(t = n\Delta t\), on \(\kappa\) and on when you stop: diffusion has no fixed point other than a constant image, so the stopping time is a parameter, not a convergence criterion.

SRAD. Yu and Acton (2002) derived a diffusion whose conductance is driven by an instantaneous coefficient of variation built from the gradient and the Laplacian, and which reduces to the Lee filter's behaviour in homogeneous regions:

\[ \frac{\partial I}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\big(c(q)\nabla I\big), \qquad q^2 = \frac{\frac12\left(\frac{\lvert\nabla I\rvert}{I}\right)^2 - \frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{\nabla^2 I}{I}\right)^2}{\left(1 + \frac14\frac{\nabla^2 I}{I}\right)^2}, \qquad c(q) = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{q^2 - q_0^2}{q_0^2\,(1 + q_0^2)}} \]
(35.10)
\(I\)
image on a linear scale (intensity in the original paper; the envelope here)
\(q\)
instantaneous coefficient of variation: an edge detector normalized by the local level
\(q_0\)
speckle scale: std/mean of a homogeneous region, re-estimated as diffusion proceeds (it decays roughly exponentially)
\(c\)
diffusion coefficient, clipped to [0, 1] in practice
\(\nabla^2\)
Laplacian
\(q \approx q_0\) (flat speckle) gives \(c \approx 1\); \(q \gg q_0\) (edge) gives \(c \to 0\). Because \(q\) divides the gradient by the local level, a 10 dB edge counts the same in bright and dark tissue, which is the multiplicative-noise version of Perona–Malik on the log image. The discrete form used here and in the lab uses one-sided differences to the four neighbours and the explicit update (35.9).

Bilateral and non-local means

Both average with weights that depend on similarity, not only distance. The bilateral filter (Tomasi and Manduchi 1998) compares single pixel values:

Non-local means (Buades, Coll and Morel 2005) compares patches, which averages the speckle in the comparison itself:

35.4Multiscale structure and contrast

Edge and contrast enhancement work on scale: a band-pass decomposition lets each band get its own gain, and the art is to raise the bands that carry anatomy without raising speckle or creating halos. The Laplacian pyramid (Burt and Adelson 1983) is the standard decomposition:

\[ \begin{aligned} G_0 &= u,\quad G_{k+1} = \big(w * G_k\big)\!\downarrow_2,\quad L_k = G_k - \mathcal{E}(G_{k+1}) \\ \hat u &= g_0 L_0 + \mathcal{E}\Big(g_1 L_1 + \mathcal{E}\big(g_2 L_2 + \cdots + \mathcal{E}(G_K)\big)\Big) \end{aligned} \]
(35.16)
\(G_k\)

\(w\)

\(\downarrow_2\)
keep every second sample
\(\mathcal{E}\)

\(L_k\)
band-pass (Laplacian) level
\(g_k\)
gain of level \(k\); all \(g_k = 1\) reconstructs \(u\) exactly
\(K\)
number of levels

On a 0.1 mm grid with 0.23 × 0.51 mm speckle cells, levels 0–1 hold most of the axial speckle detail and thermal noise, levels 1–3 the lateral speckle grain and tissue boundaries: no level is speckle-free.

(35.17)
\(\lambda\)

\(G_\sigma\)

\(H\)

Histogram equalization and CLAHE. Global equalization maps each value through the cumulative histogram; contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE: Pizer et al. 1987; Zuiderveld 1994) does it per tile, clips each tile's histogram to limit the slope, and blends the tile mappings bilinearly:

\[ T(v) = (L - 1)\sum_{u \le v}\frac{h_c(u)}{N}, \qquad h_c(u) = \min\!\Big(h(u),\ \beta\frac{N}{B}\Big) + \frac{E}{B}, \qquad \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta v}\Big|_{\max} = \frac{L - 1}{B}\Big(\beta + \frac{E}{N}\Big) \]
(35.18)

\(\beta\)

\(E\)

Pitfall

Small lesions and thin layers below the decision scale are where enhancement errors live.

35.5Temporal filtering: persistence, motion-adaptive and motion-compensated averaging

Module 34 derived recursive persistence, its time constant \(\tau = -T_F/\ln(1 - \alpha)\) and its variance ratio for correlated fluctuations ((34.24)–(34.25)). Two facts carry over. 35.6Compounding as enhancement

Compounding averages detected images of the same tissue acquired so that their speckle differs: different steering angles (spatial compounding), different sub-bands (frequency compounding), different sub-apertures. Module 26 gave the statistics ((26.11)–(26.13)); here the question is what it buys over post-processing. Coherent compounding (summing IQ, Module 23) narrows the PSF and lowers clutter but leaves speckle statistics unchanged; incoherent compounding reduces speckle by the number of independent looks.

\[ \begin{aligned} N_{\mathrm{eff}} &= \frac{N^2}{\sum_{k,l}\rho_{I,kl}}, \qquad \mathrm{SNR}_0^{\mathrm{env}} = \frac{1.913\,N}{\sqrt{\sum_{k,l}\rho_{A,kl}}} \\ d' &\approx C\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cells}}\,N_{\mathrm{eff}}}\ \xrightarrow{\ N \text{ disjoint sub-bands}\ }\ C\sqrt{\tfrac{N_{\mathrm{cells}}}{N}\cdot N} = C\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{cells}}} \end{aligned} \]
(35.22)
\(N\)
number of looks
\(\rho_{I,kl},\ \rho_{A,kl}\)

\(N_{\mathrm{eff}}\)

\(\mathrm{SNR}_0^{\mathrm{env}}\)
speckle SNR of the averaged envelopes
\(d'\)

\(C\)

Independent looks: speckle SNR rises by \(\sqrt N\) (1.91 → 3.83 for 4 looks). The detectability bookkeeping is the subtle part. 35.73D and 4D: volume denoising, scan conversion and rendering

A volume is acquired on a beam grid (a mechanically swept or matrix-array pyramid), denoised, scan converted to voxels and then rendered to a 2D image. Every 2D filter has a 3D version: diffusion with six neighbours (\(\Delta t \le 1/6\), (35.9)), 3D non-local means, BM4D (Maggioni et al. 2013). What changes is anisotropy and cost: the elevational resolution of most volume probes is several times worse than the in-plane resolution, and the grid spacing grows with depth.

\[ \begin{aligned} x &= r\sin\theta, \quad y = r\cos\theta\sin\varphi, \quad z = r\cos\theta\cos\varphi \\ \Delta_{\mathrm{lat}} &\approx r\,\Delta\theta, \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{elev}} \approx r\cos\theta\,\Delta\varphi, \quad \Delta_{\mathrm{ax}} = \frac{c}{2f_s} \end{aligned} \]
(35.23)

At 80 mm with 1° beam spacing the lateral spacing is 1.40 mm, against an axial spacing of 0.1 mm or less: voxels from a pyramid are strongly anisotropic and get worse with depth. Filtering before scan conversion keeps the noise model and the PSF shift-invariant along the beam grid; filtering after it is simpler but sees interpolation-correlated, depth-dependent speckle.

Rendering turns the volume into an image. The two families are projections and emission–absorption ray casting (Levoy 1988; Max 1995):

The background brightens where the volume is thick, and a hypoechoic structure is masked by the speckle maxima in front of and behind it. MIP is good for bright sparse targets (contrast microbubbles, bone, vessels in power Doppler) and poor for soft-tissue contrast.

35.8Deep learning: what a trained denoiser learns, and how it fails

A supervised denoiser is a regression from noisy input \(y\) to a target \(x\). The loss decides what it estimates:

\[ \begin{aligned} f^\star &= \arg\min_f \mathrm{E}\,\lVert f(y) - x\rVert^2 \;\Rightarrow\; f^\star(y) = \mathrm{E}[x \mid y] \\ \tilde x \sim p(x \mid y):\quad \mathrm{E}\,\lVert\tilde x - x\rVert^2 &= 2\,\mathrm{E}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}(x \mid y) = 2\,\mathrm{MMSE} \end{aligned} \]
(35.28)
\(f\)
denoiser (network) mapping input to output
\(y,\ x\)
input image and target (training pair)
\(\mathrm{E}[x \mid y]\)
posterior mean under the training distribution
\(\tilde x\)
a sample from the posterior (what an ideal generative model would output)
\(\mathrm{MMSE}\)
minimum mean squared error \(\mathrm{E}\lVert\mathrm{E}[x\mid y] - x\rVert^2\)
With an L2 loss the network learns the posterior mean of the training distribution: where the input is ambiguous it averages the possibilities, which looks smooth. Adversarial (generative adversarial network, GAN) and perceptual losses push the output toward samples of the posterior, which look sharp and realistic but have twice the MMSE even when the model is perfect: the extra detail is drawn from the prior, not from the data. Both are "correct" only for inputs drawn from the training distribution.

Training data. Clean ultrasound targets do not exist in vivo. Each option carries a bias. Simulation (Field-II-style or k-space simulators) gives exact targets but a domain gap in speckle statistics, clutter, reverberation, aberration and noise; compounded or averaged acquisitions give targets with residual speckle and motion blur; and filtered images as targets teach the network to imitate a classical filter, including its artefacts. Learned beamformers trained on simulated channel data and applied in vivo (e.g. Hyun et al. 2019) show both the promise and the need to test the gap explicitly.

Self-supervised training avoids clean targets by exploiting noise independence:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{E}\,\lVert f(y_1) - y_2\rVert^2 &= \mathrm{E}\,\lVert f(y_1) - x\rVert^2 + \mathrm{E}\,\lVert y_2 - x\rVert^2 \quad \text{if } \mathrm{E}[y_2 \mid x, y_1] = x \\ I_2 = R\,S_2:\quad \mathrm{E}[I_2 \mid I_1] &= \mathrm{E}[R \mid I_1] \end{aligned} \]
(35.29)
\(y_1,\ y_2\)
two noisy observations of the same \(x\) with independent zero-mean noise
\(I_1,\ I_2\)
two intensity images of the same tissue with independent speckle \(S_1, S_2\)
\(R\)
echogenicity, as in (35.3)
Noise2Noise (Lehtinen et al. 2018): the second term does not depend on \(f\), so training against a second noisy image has the same minimizer as training against the clean one. For speckle the pairs must be independent speckle realizations of the same tissue (decorrelated looks: different angles or sub-bands, (26.13)), not two frames of static tissue, whose speckle is identical. In the log domain the learned target is \(10\log_{10}R\) − 2.51 dB, a constant bias that must be removed if levels matter.
\[ f^\star\big(y_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}_i}\big) = \mathrm{E}\big[y_i \mid y_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}_i}\big] = \mathrm{E}\big[x_i \mid y_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}_i}\big] \quad \text{only if } n_i \perp n_{\Omega\setminus\mathcal{B}_i} \mid x, \qquad \mathrm{cov}(n_i, n_{i+\Delta}) \propto \lvert\mu(\Delta)\rvert^2 \]
(35.30)
\(\mathcal{B}_i\)
blind-spot region around pixel \(i\) hidden from the network
\(\Omega\)

\(n_i\)

\(\perp\ \cdot \mid x\)

\(\mu\)

Noise2Void (Krull et al. 2019) and Noise2Self (Batson and Royer 2019) predict each pixel from its neighbourhood with the pixel itself hidden. Speckle violates the condition: its intensity correlation \(\lvert\mu\rvert^2\) extends over the PSF, so a one-pixel blind spot lets the network predict the speckle from its correlated neighbours and it learns to reproduce speckle. The blind spot (or a subsampling of the image) must span the correlation length, several pixels laterally on a typical grid.

What to test before trusting a learned enhancer.

  • Hallucination and deletion: insert synthetic lesions of known contrast and size into real images (and remove real ones) and measure whether the output keeps, deletes or invents them; GAN-type losses are the documented risk (Cohen, Luck and Honari 2018 showed translation networks adding or removing tumours according to the training-set composition).
  • Instability: small, structured perturbations of the input can produce large output changes in learned reconstructions (Antun et al. 2020); test with perturbations at the noise level.
  • Distribution shift: other probes, frequencies, presets, sound speeds, body habitus, pathology prevalence, other sites. A model trained on one probe's speckle size meets a different speckle size on another probe, as in the "different probe" input above.
  • Residual analysis: the removed component \(y - f(y)\) should look like speckle and noise. Structure in the residual means anatomy was removed; structure in the output with none in the input means it was added.
  • Task-based evaluation: detectability, measurement agreement and reader performance, not PSNR (Section 35.9).

Regulatory angle. A learned enhancement that changes the clinical image is device software, and retraining changes the device. In the United States the Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) final guidance on predetermined change control plans (PCCPs) for artificial-intelligence (AI) enabled device software functions (December 2024) lets a manufacturer authorize planned modifications in advance with three elements: a description of the modifications, a modification protocol (data, retraining, performance evaluation and update procedures) and an impact assessment. Module 36 treats submission strategy for AI-enabled features in detail; the engineering consequence for this module is that the test suite of Section 35.11 becomes part of the authorized protocol, so it has to be designed before the first model ships.

Key takeaways
  • With an L2 loss a network estimates E[x | y] of its training distribution; a perfect posterior sampler (the aim of adversarial and perceptual losses) has twice that error (35.28).
  • Self-supervision needs independent noise: Noise2Noise pairs must be decorrelated speckle looks, and a blind spot must span the speckle correlation (|μ|² = 0.90 at a one-pixel lateral lag on the demo grid).
  • Out of distribution, a learned denoiser may insert training-set structure or suppress real structure: in Demo 35.10's toy data, curated training puts false bright fragments in 13% of flat-tissue pixels, balanced training in 1.9%.
  • A learned enhancement that changes the clinical image is device software; its test suite becomes part of the authorized change-control protocol.

35.9Evaluation: what to measure, and why PSNR and SSIM mislead for speckle

Report the same small set for every method, on the same phantom, with the definitions written down: gCNR and CNR for lesions (Module 26, (26.14)), speckle SNR in a homogeneous region, resolution preserved (point FWHM and edge-spread width) and, for anything that will be read by humans, a task-based observer or reader study.

\[ \mathrm{PSNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\mathrm{MAX}^2}{\mathrm{MSE}}, \qquad \mathrm{SSIM}(a, b) = \frac{(2\mu_a\mu_b + C_1)(2\sigma_{ab} + C_2)}{(\mu_a^2 + \mu_b^2 + C_1)(\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 + C_2)} \]
(35.31)
\(\mathrm{MAX}\)
peak signal value (e.g. the dB range)
\(\mathrm{MSE}\)
mean squared error against a reference image
\(\mu,\ \sigma^2,\ \sigma_{ab}\)
local means, variances and covariance in a sliding window
\(C_1,\ C_2\)
small stabilizing constants
Both need a reference, and for ultrasound the reference decides the answer. Against the echogenicity map (no speckle) the best PSNR goes to heavy smoothing that erases small targets; against the input itself any despeckling is "error"; against an independent speckle realization the PSNR optimum is the same as against the truth, by the Noise2Noise identity (35.29). SSIM (Wang et al. 2004) compares local structure, and for speckle the local structure is the speckle itself. Neither measures detectability or resolution.
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{LSF}(x) &= \frac{d\,\mathrm{ESF}(x)}{dx} \\ \text{Gaussian LSF of std } \sigma:\quad w_{10\text{–}90} &= 2\Phi^{-1}(0.9)\,\sigma = 2.563\,\sigma, \qquad \mathrm{FWHM} = 2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sigma = 2.355\,\sigma \end{aligned} \]
(35.32)
\(\mathrm{ESF}\)

\(\mathrm{LSF}\)

\(w_{10\text{–}90}\)

\(\Phi^{-1}\)

The edge width is the resolution test that nonlinear filters cannot game by smoothing only flat regions; the point FWHM is the one they game most often in the other direction (a point is a large "edge" and survives edge-preserving filters while the tissue around it is smoothed). The bench measures both. On a log image an edge between two speckle levels must be averaged along the edge over many speckle cells before fitting. Note that gCNR is invariant only to pointwise maps ((34.7)): any spatial smoothing narrows the inside and outside histograms and raises it, so a gCNR gain from a spatial filter means nothing without the resolution numbers next to it.

(35.33)

\(\mathbf{v}\)

35.10Lessons from other fields

Ultrasound enhancement borrowed most of its tools. The lenders also learned which assumptions break.

\[ \hat x^{(k+1)} = \hat x^{(k)} \cdot \left[h^{\mathsf T} * \frac{y}{h * \hat x^{(k)}}\right] \]

  • SAR gave the multiplicative model, local-statistics filters and ENL; OCT shows that decorrelated looks beat post-filtering for detail.
  • Astronomy's Richardson–Lucy teaches that the iteration count is a regularization parameter; burst photography teaches to align, merge and reject what does not align.
  • CT and MRI denoising showed that changes in noise texture affect reader acceptance at equal noise, and that regulators expect task-based evidence.

35.11Workflow: designing an image look and presets with clinicians

The algorithms above are parts. A product image is a preset: a parameter vector for compression, gray map, speckle reduction, edge enhancement, compounding, persistence and (for volumes) rendering, chosen per probe and per application. The engineering problem is to choose it well, defend the choice, and keep it from drifting.

  1. Start from tasks, not looks. For each application list what must be detected (small hypoechoic lesions, calcifications, needle tips), what is measured (thickness, diameter, volume) and which cues must survive (shadowing, posterior enhancement, B-lines in lung). These become acceptance criteria: for example "no preset may reduce 3 mm lesion gCNR by more than X" or "caliper bias on the edge phantom below Y".
  2. Sweep objectively first. Run each parameter over its range on phantoms and simulated images with known truth, compute the five bench numbers, and keep only the Pareto front (no other setting is better on every metric). Latin-hypercube or factorial designs keep the sweep small. Clinicians then choose among a handful of defensible options instead of tuning sliders.
  3. Review blinded. Show the same cine loops processed with each candidate, randomized and unlabeled, side by side or sequentially, and ask task questions ("is there a lesion?", "where is the boundary?") as well as preference. Record reader, case and score. Expect strong preference for smooth images; check it against the task scores.
  4. Test robustness. A look tuned on slim volunteers fails in high body-mass-index patients (more attenuation, more clutter, coarser speckle at depth). The robustness matrix spans habitus, probe, depth and frequency settings, motion (cardiac, respiratory, freehand), sites and operators; any cell that breaks a criterion is a finding.
  5. Freeze and regress. Replay a golden set of raw (IQ or pre-enhancement) data through every software build; compare the metrics with tolerances, and send image differences above a threshold to a human. A change in a filter's border handling or numeric precision is caught here, not in the field.
  6. Document. The design history records the tasks, sweeps, review results, robustness matrix and the rationale for each parameter, linked to the risk analysis (what happens if the enhancement hides or invents a structure). For learned components, the same record is the backbone of the change control plan of Section 35.8.

35.13How experts think about it

The expert model has three questions. First, which noise model holds at this point in the chain: complex Gaussian on IQ data, multiplicative speckle on the envelope, additive speckle on the log image, and something interpolated and clipped after scan conversion. Second, what decorrelates: only independent looks reduce speckle, while filters trade it for resolution at a scale they choose, and averaging over time removes only what changes between frames. Third, what the claim is: a smoother picture is not more information, so every proposal comes with the five bench numbers, a task-based reader result and a failure analysis out of distribution. The rules below are the working form of these questions.

Thermal noise is independent frame to frame; speckle of static tissue is not. Recursive persistence (35.20) lowers speckle only where tissue moves, and then it also smears it. 35.15Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Is speckle noise? · screen

It depends on the estimand. For the echogenicity map it is a multiplicative, deterministic-given-the-scatterers interference pattern with envelope contrast 0.523 and log std 5.57 dB (35.3), (35.4), so it limits lesion detection. For speckle tracking, elastography, flow and quantitative ultrasound it is the signal: its statistics and its motion carry the information. Practical consequence: compute those quantities upstream of enhancement, and label enhanced images as such.

I2Derive the Lee filter and explain its parameter. · screen

With \(I = RS\), \(\mathrm{E}S = 1\), the linear minimum-mean-square-error estimate of \(R\) from the local mean and variance is \(\hat R = \bar I + W(I - \bar I)\) with \(W = \mathrm{var}R/\mathrm{var}I\); under the multiplicative model this gives \(W = 1 - C_u^2/C_I^2\) (Lee drops the second-order term that Kuan keeps) (35.6). \(C_u\) is the speckle contrast of a flat region: 0.523 for single-look envelope data, less after compounding or interpolation. Too small a \(C_u\) leaves speckle; too large flattens texture. The window must hold several speckle cells for \(C_I\) to be estimated: at 7 × 7 pixels the local \(C_I\) in flat speckle is 0.47 ± 0.09 (Demo 35.2).

I3A vendor shows a new despeckling filter raising cyst-lesion gCNR from 0.5 to 0.95. What do you ask for? · onsite

The cost side: edge 10–90% width and point FWHM on the same images (SRAD on the bench reaches gCNR 1.00 with the edge widened from 0.40 to 1.38 mm), and the comparison against simple smoothing at equal edge width. Then whether the lesion is larger than the filter scale (gCNR rises for any smoother when the region holds many cells), how parameters were chosen (separate tuning and test sets), behaviour on small and low-contrast targets and out of distribution, and a reader or channelized-observer study, since post-processing cannot raise ideal-observer d′ (35.33).

I4Why does frame averaging leave speckle untouched on a static phantom, and when does it reduce it? · screen

Speckle is a deterministic function of the scatterer positions and the PSF; if neither changes, every frame has the same speckle, and averaging removes only thermal noise: persistence leaves \(\alpha/(2 - \alpha)\) of its variance, 0.18 at α = 0.3 (35.20). Speckle is reduced only when the frames decorrelate: tissue or probe motion (in-plane, out-of-plane), which also smears structure. Motion-compensated averaging realigns the speckle and so keeps speckle SNR near 1.95 (Demo 35.7).

I5Can you run non-local means at 60 Hz on a 512 × 512 image? Show the numbers. · onsite

\(R = N_{\mathrm{px}}f_Fn_{\mathrm{ops}}\) (35.2): 262 144 pixels × 60 Hz = 15.7 M pixels/s. Direct with 5 × 5 patches and a 21 × 21 search: \(2S^2P^2\) = 22 050 operations per pixel, 347 G operations/s. With one integral image per search offset: about \(8S^2\) = 3528, 55 G operations/s, feasible on a current GPU if memory traffic is managed (21² passes over the image). Cheaper still: a smaller search window, but it must span several speckle cells (a 5-pixel window gives only Neff 11), or filtering on a decimated beam grid before scan conversion.

I6Frequency compounding or spatial compounding for speckle reduction? · onsite

Both raise speckle SNR by \(\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{eff}}}\) (35.22). Frequency compounding costs no frame rate but splits a fixed bandwidth, so axial resolution falls (four sub-bands: cells 0.82 mm long instead of 0.25 mm) and the ideal-observer d′ barely changes (factor 0.96 in Demo 35.8). Spatial compounding adds new k-space coverage, reduces angle-dependent shadowing and improves boundary continuity, but costs N acquisitions per frame and suffers from motion; looks decorrelate only when \(\Delta\sin\theta \ge 1/F_\#\) (four looks at 4° give Neff 1.31).

I7Outline a 3D rendering pipeline for a fetal face and say why MIP is wrong for it. · onsite

Volume denoising on the acquisition grid (speckle model intact), scan conversion to Cartesian voxels (35.23), an opacity transfer function that suppresses fluid, front-to-back compositing with early termination (35.25), gradient shading from a smoothed gradient (35.26), optionally a light-transport shadow term (35.27). MIP picks the brightest sample on each ray; behind the face the uterine wall and placenta win, and the maximum of speckle is biased up by \(10\log_{10}H_M\) (6.8 dB for 64 cells) (35.24). MIP suits bright, sparse targets such as bone or contrast-filled vessels.

I8A deep-learning denoiser trained on liver images is going to be used on thyroid. What can go wrong and how do you test it? · research

With an L2 loss it estimates the posterior mean of liver images (35.28); on thyroid (different speckle size at higher frequency, nodules, calcifications, cysts) it can smooth away microcalcifications and insert liver-like texture or boundaries. Demo 35.10 shows the mechanism: curated training puts false wall fragments in 13% of flat pixels. Tests: phantoms and simulations with known truth at the new frequency and speckle size; inserted-structure and deleted-structure metrics; point and edge widths; failure-mode sets (small bright targets, low-contrast nodules); a reader study on the target anatomy; and a predetermined change-control plan if it is retrained.

I9How would you train a speckle denoiser without clean images? · research

Options: Noise2Noise with pairs whose noise is independent given the truth (35.29), for example decorrelated sub-aperture, sub-band or steered looks, accepting that their PSFs differ; many-look compounds or slow-motion frame averages as approximate targets; simulated data (Field-style or k-space speckle) with known echogenicity, at the risk of a domain gap; blind-spot methods only with a mask that covers the speckle correlation length (35.30), because \(\lvert\mu\rvert^2\) is 0.90 at a one-pixel lateral lag on a typical grid. Repeated frames of the same static tissue do not work: their speckle is identical.

I10A paper reports +3 dB PSNR and +0.1 SSIM for its denoiser. Is it better? · screen

Not by that evidence. Against an echogenicity or heavily averaged reference, PSNR and SSIM (35.31) reward estimating the local mean; in Demo 35.11 the best-PSNR Gaussian widens the point target from 0.55 to 4.4 mm. Ask what the reference is, and for gCNR, speckle SNR, edge 10–90% width, point FWHM, behaviour on small targets and a task-based reader result.

I11How do you choose κ and the number of iterations for anisotropic diffusion? · onsite

κ sets the gradient at the flux maximum (κ for \(g_2\), \(\kappa/\sqrt{2}\) for \(g_1\)) (35.8): set it from the distribution of neighbour differences in flat speckle on the log image (5.57 dB speckle gives differences of several dB per pixel), so that most speckle gradients fall below it and target edges above. Keep \(\Delta t \le 1/4\) for the 4-neighbour explicit scheme (35.9). The iteration count is then chosen on the bench: speckle SNR and gCNR saturate while edge and point widths keep growing (60 iterations: SNR 1.9 → 8.9, edge 0.34 → 1.32 mm; at 300 the point is gone).

I12When is CLAHE acceptable on a clinical ultrasound image? · onsite

When the task is visual search for boundaries and no comparison of brightness across the image is needed. It bounds the local slope by \(\frac{L - 1}{B}(\beta + E/N)\) (35.18) but uses a different map per tile, so equal echogenicity reads differently at different places; it amplifies speckle together with the lesion (gCNR 0.255 → 0.221 at β = 2 in Demo 35.6) and can equalize away a lesion comparable to a tile. It is not acceptable for echogenicity judgement (for example hepatorenal brightness), quantitative gray-level analysis or images that feed measurements.

35.17Self-check

Q1Three independent looks are averaged in intensity. What is the std of the log speckle?

\(4.343\sqrt{\psi_1(3)}\) with \(\psi_1(3) = \pi^2/6 - 1 - 1/4\) = 0.395: 2.73 dB (35.4), against 5.57 dB for one look.

Q2Four looks with pairwise intensity correlation 0.5. Neff, and the speckle SNR of the compound envelope if the looks were independent?

\(N_{\mathrm{eff}} = 16/(4 + 12 \times 0.5)\) = 1.6 (35.22). Independent: 1.913 × \(\sqrt{4}\) = 3.83.

Q3Lee filter with Cu = 0.523 at a pixel whose local CI = 0.70. Weight W, and what does it mean?

\(W = 1 - 0.523^2/0.70^2\) = 0.44 (35.6): the output keeps 44% of the pixel's deviation from the local mean; the filter treats the region as partly structure.

Q4Persistence with α = 0.2 at 30 Hz: fraction of independent noise variance left, and mean lag?

\(\alpha/(2 - \alpha)\) = 0.111; lag \((1 - \alpha)T_F/\alpha\) = 0.8 × 33.3/0.2 = 133 ms (35.20) (Module 34).

Q5A MIP ray crosses 16 independent speckle cells of a uniform region. How much brighter does the region look than its mean intensity?

\(H_{16}\) = 3.38, so \(10\log_{10}3.38\) = 5.3 dB (35.24).

Q6An unsharp mask with λ = 0.6 is applied to a 10 dB step on the log image. Overshoot at the step, and one σ away?

\(\lambda\Delta/2\) = 3 dB at the step; \(0.159\lambda\Delta\) = 0.95 dB one σ away (35.17).

Q7After filtering, an edge has a 10–90% width of 0.8 mm. If the line spread is Gaussian, what is its FWHM?

\(\sigma = 0.8/2.563\) = 0.312 mm; FWHM = 2.355σ = 0.735 mm (35.32).

Q8A channelized Hotelling observer gives d′ = 1.5 for a lesion task. What AUC does that correspond to, and could a denoiser raise the ideal observer's d′ on the same data?

AUC = \(\Phi(1.5/\sqrt{2})\) = 0.856 (35.33). No: post-processing cannot add information for the ideal observer; it can raise the d′ of a human or a channelized observer, whose channels and internal noise are not ideal.
