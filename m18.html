18.1The CMUT cell as a clamped plate

A CMUT cell is a capacitor whose top plate is a membrane. From the bottom up: a conductive (doped) silicon substrate that serves as the bottom electrode, an insulating layer of silicon nitride or oxide, a sealed vacuum gap, and a membrane of silicon or silicon nitride carrying the top electrode. Typical numbers: membrane thickness 0.5–2 µm (1 µm in the Stanford arrays described by Ergun et al., SPIE 2005), radius 10–50 µm, and gaps from about 40 nm (Park et al., MEMS 2008, ±2 nm across the wafer) to about 1 µm. One array element is tens to hundreds of such cells wired in parallel.

With a bias \(V\) the membrane deflects toward the substrate by \(w(r)\). Each patch of membrane feels the local parallel-plate pressure, so the static problem is the clamped-plate equation with a nonlinear load (18.1)–(18.2):

\[ \begin{aligned} D\,\nabla^{4} w(r) &= \frac{\varepsilon_0 V^{2}}{2\left(g_{\mathrm{eff}} - w(r)\right)^{2}} \\ w(a) &= w'(a) = 0 \end{aligned} \]

The piston spring \(k = 192\pi D/a^{2}\) is the force \(\pi a^{2}P\) divided by the average deflection \(Pa^{4}/(192D)\) of a clamped plate under uniform pressure \(P\). The distributed constant 27.8 comes from the finite-difference solution below (27.76; unchanged to 0.02 % between 60, 120 and 240 radial intervals). It holds for any \(a\), \(h\), \(g\) as long as the plate stays in small deflection (\(w \ll h\)) and the gap is uniform. Default cell (Si, \(a\) = 20 µm, \(h\) = 1 µm, \(g_0\) = 0.2 µm, 0.1 µm nitride): \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) = 53.1 V, against 76.0 V from the piston.

Why the piston is wrong by 30 %. The piston puts the whole electrode at the average gap. A plate concentrates its deflection at the centre, where the gap is smallest and the pressure, \(\propto 1/(g - w)^{2}\), is largest; the centre therefore softens first. At pull-in the centre has moved ≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (0.459 converged; coarse grids read slightly higher; the insulator enters only through \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) but the average only 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), less than half the piston's \(g/3\) (compare (18.3) and (18.4)). The numerical method: the conventional branch is traced by fixing the centre deflection \(w(0)\) and solving for both the shape and the voltage that holds it (a Newton solve on \([w;\ V^{2}]\)). Because \(w(0)\) increases monotonically along the branch, the solver walks through the pull-in fold, where \(V\) reaches its maximum, and continues on the unstable side.

Pitfall

"Pull-in at a third of the gap" is a statement about a rigid piston. For a clamped membrane, quote the centre deflection (≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) or the average deflection (≈ 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\)), and say which. Datasheets and FEM papers usually give the centre value, so do not compare it with \(g/3\).

18.2Coupling, collapse mode and hysteresis

Coupling as a stiffness ratio. Linearize the plate about its biased shape. Held at constant voltage (short circuit), the electrostatic pressure adds a negative stiffness at every node, \(-\varepsilon_0 V^{2}/(g_{\mathrm{eff}} - w)^{3}\) per unit area. Held at constant charge (open circuit), the electrode voltage readjusts as the capacitance changes, which adds back a rank-one term. The coupling coefficient is the fractional stiffness difference of the fundamental mode (18.5)–(18.6):

\(C = \sum \varepsilon_0 W_i/(g_{\mathrm{eff}} - w_i)\). For a piston the rank-one term exactly cancels the softening (\(K_Q = k\)) and \(k^{2} = 1 - k_{\mathrm{eff}}/k\), the Module 19 formula. For a plate it does not cancel fully, and \(k^{2}\) is lower: 0.04, 0.16, 0.26 and 0.37 at 0.5, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) for the default cell (piston: 0.08, 0.28, 0.43, 0.56). Bayram et al. (IEEE TUFFC 2003) found the same character with FEM: \(k_T^{2}\) exceeds 0.35 only above 85 % of the collapse voltage.

Collapse mode. Above \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) the centre lands on the insulator. The contact disc of radius \(b\) grows with bias and the free annulus between \(b\) and \(a\) carries the vibration (Figure 18.A, right). Three things change. The annulus is a much stiffer plate, so the resonance rises steeply with \(b\). The field across the thin insulator in the contact region is enormous, so the coupling of the annulus edge is high; Bayram et al. report \(k_T^{2} \approx 0.7\) at about 60 % of the collapse voltage after collapse, versus 0.3 averaged over a conventional swing. And the average displacement available for transmit is larger (0.25–0.45 µm versus 0.05–0.15 µm in their 1 µm-gap design). The collapsed branch is traced here by fixing the contact radius and requiring zero contact force at its edge, which again turns the fold into a monotonic path.

Snap-back and hysteresis. Lowering the bias, the membrane stays down until the contact force at the edge can no longer be maintained, at the snap-back voltage \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\). Since the electrostatic pressure in contact is \(\varepsilon_0 V^{2}/\left(2(t_i/\varepsilon_r)^{2}\right)\), \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\) is controlled by the insulator: the thinner the electrical gap at contact, the lower \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\) and the wider the hysteresis loop. For the default cell \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\) = 25.8 V = 0.49 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\); for a 1 µm gap with the same 0.1 µm nitride it is 0.26 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\). Bayram et al. computed 140 V and 68 V (0.49) for their 50 µm-radius, 1 µm-gap cell, which also had a 50 nm contact offset; adding that offset to this model moves the ratio from 0.26 to 0.43, so the offset matters as much as the insulator.

18.3Bandwidth, the water load and crosstalk

Per unit area, a CMUT membrane is a mass \(m_a\) on a spring \(k_a\) radiating into water with resistance \(Z_w = \rho_w c_w\) = 1.5 MRayl. Using the piston-equivalent constants of the clamped plate (spring \(192D/a^{4}\), modal mass \(1.8\rho h\) for the average displacement), a 1 µm silicon membrane of 20 µm radius has \(m_a\) = 4.2 g/m² and \(k_a = 1.8\times 10^{13}\) Pa/m. Its vacuum resonance is 10 MHz, and its quality factor in water is \(Q = \sqrt{k_a m_a}/Z_w \approx 0.18\): deeply over-damped. The velocity response per unit force is then flat between two corner frequencies (18.7):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{low}} &\approx \frac{\eta\, k_{a,\mathrm{eff}}}{2\pi Z_w}, \qquad f_{\mathrm{high}} \approx \frac{Z_w}{2\pi\eta\, m_a} \\ m_a &= 1.8\,\rho h + \frac{8}{3\pi}\,\rho_w a \end{aligned} \]

Crosstalk rides an interface wave. In a CMUT array, the neighbouring membranes and the water above them form a compliant surface. Bayram et al. (IEEE TUFFC 2007) measured and simulated 1D arrays and identified the main crosstalk path as a dispersive guided mode along the fluid–membrane interface: its phase velocity approaches the water sound speed at low frequency, falls with frequency and cuts off (near 4 MHz in conventional mode); first-neighbour crosstalk was about −17 dB, strongest near 2.1 MHz. In collapse mode the average crosstalk fell to about −39 dB and the cutoff rose to about 10 MHz. 18.4Making CMUTs: fabrication, charging, nonlinearity and CMOS integration

Two process families. In sacrificial release (surface micromachining) the gap is a sacrificial layer (polysilicon or oxide) deposited on the insulated substrate, covered by a deposited silicon nitride membrane, removed through etch holes and sealed under vacuum; the gap is the sacrificial thickness minus the nitride deposited during sealing. In wafer bonding the cavities are etched into a thermal oxide on one wafer and a silicon-on-insulator wafer is bonded on top; removing the handle and buried oxide leaves the single-crystal device layer (under 1.5 µm, uniform to better than 10 nm on 100 mm wafers) as the membrane (Ergun et al., SPIE 2005, Figs. 3 and 5). Bonding gives better membrane uniformity and material properties; because the silicon membrane is conductive, it needs a thicker insulator than a nitride membrane. Park et al. (MEMS 2008; J. Microelectromech. Syst. 2011) combined local oxidation (LOCOS) with direct bonding to set the gap and the oxide post height independently, reaching 40 nm gaps with ±2 nm uniformity, fewer breakdown paths and lower parasitic capacitance because most of the bonded area is oxide.

Charging. The vacuum gap sees fields of order \(V_{\mathrm{DC}}/g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), about 50 V over 0.21 µm, i.e. 240 V/µm; by continuity of \(D\) the nitride carries \(1/\varepsilon_r\) of that, about 30 V/µm (0.3 MV/cm), and in contact the full bias across \(t_i\), about 500 V/µm. Charge injected into it adds a bias offset \(V_q\): the membrane responds to \(V - V_q\), so the C–V curve, the pull-in voltage and the sensitivity drift with use. Remedies are structural (insulation only on posts or patterned, as in the LOCOS process), electrical (bipolar or alternating bias) and operational (stay below collapse).

The force is quadratic. With \(V = V_{\mathrm{DC}} + v\cos\omega t\) the electrostatic force contains \(V_{\mathrm{DC}}^{2} + v^{2}/2\) (static), \(2V_{\mathrm{DC}}v\cos\omega t\) (linear) and \((v^{2}/2)\cos 2\omega t\). The second-harmonic force is \(v/(4V_{\mathrm{DC}})\) of the fundamental: −32 dB at \(v = 0.1\,V_{\mathrm{DC}}\), −20 dB at \(v = 0.4\,V_{\mathrm{DC}}\). On top of that the \(1/(g - w)^{2}\) gap nonlinearity grows with stroke. For tissue-harmonic imaging the transmit harmonic must stay well below what tissue generates, which pushes CMUT drive schemes toward small AC on a large bias, predistortion, or pulse inversion that accounts for the \(V^{2}\) term.

The gap is the stroke. A membrane cannot move further than its gap. Its plane-wave surface pressure is \(Z_w\omega\bar x\) for an average displacement amplitude \(\bar x\): 50 nm at 5 MHz gives 2.4 MPa. Large gaps cost bias voltage (\(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto g^{3/2}\)), small gaps cost output pressure; collapse mode and tall-gap designs with pre-charged or high-voltage bias are the standard answers to this trade.

Integration for 2D arrays. A \(\lambda/2\)-pitch 2D aperture at 3 MHz has 257 µm elements; 32 × 32 such elements cannot each be cabled (Module 19, Section 19.4). CMUTs offer two ways out. Flip-chip: through-wafer vias bring each element's contact to the back of the CMUT chip, which is bump-bonded to a fan-out or an ASIC; Oralkan et al. (IEEE TUFFC 2003) built 128 × 128 arrays at 420 µm pitch this way and imaged in 3D with a 32 × 64 sub-array flip-chip bonded to a glass fan-out (their Figs. 1–2 show the via and the assembly). Monolithic CMUT-on-CMOS: the cells are built directly on the passivation of a finished CMOS wafer with a back-end-compatible, low-temperature process, so that every element has its pulser, preamplifier and often part of the beamformer underneath it; a commercial whole-body handheld probe built this way is described by Rothberg et al. (PNAS 2021). The same logic applies to PMUTs bonded to CMOS. In both cases micro-beamforming in the probe (Module 19, Section 19.5) is what makes the channel count tractable.

18.5PMUT: a flexural plate driven by a piezoelectric film

A piezoelectric micromachined ultrasonic transducer is a clamped plate made of a passive layer (Si, SiO₂ or SiN, 1–5 µm) and a piezoelectric film (AlN or ScAlN 0.5–2 µm, sol-gel or sputtered PZT up to a few µm) between electrodes. A field across the film makes it contract laterally through \(e_{31,f}\); because the film sits off the neutral axis of the stack, the lateral stress becomes a bending moment and the plate flexes (Qiu et al., Sensors 2015, Fig. 1(c)). No DC bias is needed, drive voltages suit CMOS, and AlN processing is CMOS-compatible.

Single crystals. [001]-poled relaxor-PT crystals reach \(k_{33}\) above 0.9 and \(d_{33}\) above 1500 pC/N (Zhang et al., Prog. Mater. Sci. 2015). For a transducer the gain is \(k_t\) (0.55–0.59 against 0.51) and, more importantly, \(k_{33}\) in diced or composite form, which buys bandwidth and sensitivity. The price is thermal and electrical fragility: PMN-PT depoles near its rhombohedral-to-tetragonal transition at 86–100 °C and has a coercive field of only about 2 kV/cm. PIN-PMN-PT, the "second generation", raises \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) to 105–130 °C and \(E_c\) to 4.5–6 kV/cm at slightly lower \(d_{33}\). Mn doping raises the mechanical \(Q\) further (TRS lists \(Q_m \approx\) 500 for Mn:PIN-PMN-PT against 80 for PMN-PT), which matters for therapy and high-power pushes rather than imaging.

AC poling and transparency. Poling with an alternating field instead of DC changes the domain structure of rhombohedral PMN-PT. Qiu et al. (Nature 2020) used it to remove the light-scattering 71° domain walls, obtaining near-perfect transparency together with \(d_{33}\) above 2100 pC/N and \(k_{33}\) ≈ 0.94 (their Fig. 3 compares AC- and DC-poled crystals). Two consequences matter for ultrasound: AC poling is now a route to higher \(d_{33}\) in ordinary crystal transducers, and transparent crystals let light pass through the transducer, which simplifies co-axial photoacoustic and optical-ultrasound probes.

Composites. A 1-3 composite of single-crystal pillars combines the crystal's \(k_{33}\) with the polymer's low impedance: \(k_t\) ≈ 0.83 against 0.69 for PZT-5H pillars, at the same 11–23 MRayl (Demo 18.6). The fine-scale requirement (Smith and Auld, IEEE TUFFC 1991) becomes the manufacturing limit at high frequency: at 20 MHz a half-wave PMN-PT composite is about 90 µm thick, so pillars must be under about 45 µm wide with narrow kerfs, beyond conventional dicing, which is why high-frequency composites are made by etching or other micro-machining routes. A 2-2 composite (plates instead of pillars, \(\nu = w/p\)) is easier to dice and suits 1D arrays, with coupling between the 1-3 and plate values.

Lead-free. Regulatory pressure on lead drives work on (K,Na)NbO₃ (KNN) and Bi-based (BNT, BKT) ceramics. Saito et al. (Nature 2004) reported a KNN-based ceramic with \(d_{33}\) above 300 pC/N, 416 pC/N when textured; KNN films reach \(\lvert e_{31,f}\rvert\) = 8.5–14.4 C/m² (Table 18.2). For imaging arrays they are not yet a drop-in replacement for PZT-5H, mainly on coupling, temperature stability and processing; treat property claims in this area as composition- and process-specific.

High frequency and polymers. Above about 30 MHz single elements are thin and small, so electrical impedance, not coupling, often decides: LiNbO₃ (\(\varepsilon^{S}\) 39, \(k_t\) 0.49) gives a large element a convenient impedance where PZT-5H (\(\varepsilon^{S}\) 1470) would be a near short. PVDF sits at the other extreme: \(Z \approx\) 4 MRayl, close to water, \(k_t\) 0.14 and a tiny permittivity, which together give a weak transmitter but an excellent wideband receiver with \(g_{33} \approx\) 0.33 V·m/N, about 18 times PZT-5H's. That is why membrane and needle hydrophones (Modules 21 and 22) are PVDF.

Its impedance is close to water's, so it barely perturbs the field it measures: the basis of membrane and needle hydrophones.

Exact formula\(g_{33} = d_{33}/\varepsilon_{33}^{T}\) with \(d_{33}\) and \(\varepsilon_{33}^{T}/\varepsilon_0\) from Table 18.1 mid values; PVDF datasheet value.
Low \(k_t\) makes PVDF a poor transmitter; \(\tan\delta \approx\) 0.25 at 1 MHz and a 70–80 °C limit restrict it further.

18.10Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What sets a CMUT's pull-in voltage, and how does it scale with membrane radius, thickness and gap? · screen

Balance of the plate's bending stiffness against the electrostatic pressure: \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}^{2} = c\cdot Dg_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}/(\varepsilon_0 a^{4})\) with \(c\) = 27.8 for a clamped plate (56.9 for the piston idealization) (18.4), so \(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto h^{3/2}g_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}/a^{2}\), and \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) includes \(t_i/\varepsilon_r\) of the insulator. A 1 µm Si membrane, 20 µm radius, 0.2 µm gap and 0.1 µm nitride: 53 V. Follow-up: "how would residual tensile stress change it?" It adds a membrane stiffness that raises \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) and the resonance; for thin, highly stressed membranes it dominates the bending term.

I2Why does a CMUT not need a matching layer, and what limits its bandwidth? · screen

Its moving mass per area is a few g/m², so \(\omega m\) is a fraction of water's 1.5 MRayl: the water load over-damps the mass–spring (\(Q \approx 0.2\)) and couples directly. The band runs from \(\approx \eta k_{\mathrm{eff}}/(2\pi Z_w)\) (spring) to \(\approx Z_w/(2\pi\eta m)\) (mass, including water added mass) (18.7). In practice higher membrane modes, parasitic capacitance, crosstalk and diffraction bring measured immersion bandwidths to about 100 % (Oralkan et al. 2002: 96 % uncompensated, 105 % compensated). Follow-up: "and what limits the low end?" Spring softening helps (\(k_{\mathrm{eff}}\) falls with bias), but element size limits radiation at low frequency.

I3Explain collapse-mode operation. Why would you use it, and what are the risks? · onsite

Biasing past \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) brings the membrane centre onto the insulator; the free annulus then vibrates at a higher, bias-tunable frequency (\(f/f_0 \approx 8.7\) at \(b/a\) = 0.5), with higher coupling (FEM: \(k_T^{2} \approx 0.7\)) and larger average stroke. The state is hysteretic: it releases only below \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\), set by the insulator's electrical thickness. Risks: dielectric charging under very high contact fields, wear and stiction at the contact, temperature and bias drift of the operating point. Follow-up: "how do you get into collapse reliably?" Overdrive briefly above \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\), then hold a bias inside the hysteresis window.

I4How do you define and compute the coupling coefficient of a CMUT, and why is the piston value optimistic? · onsite

\(k^{2} = 1 - K_V/K_Q\) (or \(1 - C^{S}/C^{T}\) electrically): the fraction of stiffness that is electrical. For a plate, linearize about the biased shape, form \(\mathbf{K}_V = \mathbf{K}\) minus the nodal softening and \(\mathbf{K}_Q = \mathbf{K}_V + V^{2}\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^{T}/C\), and compare the lowest eigenvalues (18.5)–(18.6). The piston puts all softening into one degree of freedom and gets \(2\xi/(1 - \xi) = 0.43\) at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\); the plate gets 0.26 because only the centre softens strongly. Follow-up: "which one do you measure?" From \(f_r/f_a\) of the impedance or from \(C\) at constant bias, as for a piezo element (Module 16).

I5What is the main crosstalk mechanism in CMUT arrays and how do you suppress it? · onsite

A dispersive guided wave at the fluid–membrane interface, slower than \(c_w\) and cut off near the membrane resonance; Bayram et al. (2007) measured −17 dB on the first neighbour near 2.1 MHz in conventional mode. It is a property of the compliant surface, not of the substrate (the substrate Lamb modes A0 and S0 were about 16 and 41 dB below the interface mode: −40 and −65 dB against −24.4 dB, all relative to the transmitting element). Suppression: operate in collapse mode (cutoff rises, average −39 dB), add a lossy coating (PDMS gave only a few dB in Bayram's experiments), shape the cell layout or trench between elements, or compensate in the beamformer. Follow-up: "why does it get worse with bias?" Softening lowers the cutoff into the band.

I6Sacrificial release or wafer bonding for a new CMUT array: how do you choose? · onsite

Wafer bonding gives single-crystal silicon membranes with excellent thickness control (device layer under 1.5 µm, < 10 nm variation), any cell shape and fill factor, and gap control by oxidation (LOCOS: 40 nm ± 2 nm), but needs SOI wafers, a thicker insulator for the conductive membrane, and is harder to do on top of CMOS. Sacrificial release uses deposited nitride membranes, works at lower temperature and is one natural route for CMUT-on-CMOS (low-temperature bonding onto the CMOS wafer is the other), at the cost of membrane stress and thickness variation and etch-hole sealing. Follow-up: "which would you use for a monolithic 2D array?" Sacrificial release (or low-temperature bonding variants), because the CMOS limits the thermal budget.

I7Sketch a 2D CMUT-on-CMOS array for 3 MHz cardiac imaging. What dominates the design? · research

\(\lambda/2\) = 257 µm pitch; a 20 × 20 mm aperture is about 80 × 80 = 6400 elements, impossible to cable, so each element gets a pulser and LNA under it and micro-beamforming reduces the channel count to the system (Module 19). Dominant constraints: power density (a few mW per channel already gives watts on a few cm2 at the patient), element capacitance versus LNA noise, bias distribution (tens of volts on a CMOS die, isolated per element or shared), acoustic crosstalk through the interface wave, and thermal limits on the probe face (Module 22). Follow-up: "how do you test it?" Per-element impulse response and C–V maps on wafer before dicing, then pulse-echo uniformity.

I8Give the PMUT resonance formula. Why is the electrode only about 70 % of the radius? · screen

\(f = 10.22/(2\pi a^{2})\cdot\sqrt{D/\bar\rho h}\) for a clamped bilayer plate with laminated stiffness \(D\), divided by \(\sqrt{1 + 0.67\rho_w a/\bar\rho h}\) in water (18.9)–(18.11). The electrode applies a uniform moment; the mode curvature changes sign at \(r = a/\sqrt{2}\), so the work done is \(\propto \beta_e^{2}(1 - \beta_e^{2})\): zero for a full electrode, maximal at 0.707. Example: AlN 1 µm on Si 2 µm, \(a\) = 50 µm: 5.6 MHz in vacuum, 2.4 MHz in water. Follow-up: "what does residual stress do?" Tension raises \(f\) and pushes toward membrane behaviour, compression lowers it and can buckle the plate.

I9AlN or PZT film for a PMUT receiver? For a transmitter? · onsite

Receiver: open-circuit voltage per stress \(\propto e_{31,f}/(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{33})\): AlN 11.3 GV/m and Sc0.15AlN 15 GV/m beat sol-gel PZT (0.7–1.8 as published) because their permittivity is 10–12 rather than hundreds; AlN also has a lower loss tangent. Transmitter: force per volt \(\propto e_{31,f}\), 8–14 C/m² for PZT versus 1.05 for AlN, and energy FOM \(e^{2}/\varepsilon\) up to 110 GPa for epitaxial PZT. ScAlN doubles AlN's energy FOM while staying CMOS-friendly. Follow-up: "what about one die doing both?" Use separate receive and transmit electrodes, or a ScAlN compromise.

I10Would you put single-crystal composite in a 2D matrix TEE probe? What would worry you? · research

The gain: \(k_t \approx\) 0.8 (composite) versus ≈ 0.5–0.7, which means wider bandwidth and higher sensitivity, valuable for harmonic imaging through a small aperture. The worries: PMN-PT depoles near \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) 86–100 °C and has \(E_c \approx\) 2 kV/cm, so bonding, sterilization and self-heating at the probe face, and bipolar drive at high voltage, all eat the margin; PIN-PMN-PT (\(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) 105–130 °C, \(E_c\) 4.5–6 kV/cm) is the usual answer. Also cost, dicing yield at small pitch, and the higher permittivity, which helps small elements drive the in-probe electronics. Follow-up: "Mn doping?" Raises \(Q_m\) (≈ 500), useful for therapy, less for imaging bandwidth.

I11What is AC poling and why did it attract attention? · research

Poling a relaxor-PT crystal with an alternating field instead of DC. In rhombohedral PMN-PT it changes the domain configuration; Qiu et al. (Nature 2020) removed the light-scattering 71° domain walls and obtained near-perfect optical transparency with \(d_{33}\) above 2100 pC/N and \(k_{33} \approx 0.94\). For ultrasound it offers higher \(d_{33}\) from the same crystal and transparent transducers through which light can be delivered, which simplifies photoacoustic and optical–acoustic probes. Follow-up: "what limits it?" Temperature stability and the same \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) and \(E_c\) constraints as any PMN-PT; poling-condition reproducibility in production.

18.12Self-check

Q1You double the membrane thickness and halve the radius of a CMUT cell. By what factor does \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) change, and the vacuum resonance?

\(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto h^{3/2}/a^{2} \to 2^{1.5}\times 4 = 11.3\) times higher. \(f \propto h/a^{2} \to 2\times 4 = 8\) times higher. Both at fixed gap and insulator.

Q2A colleague's piston model gives \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) = 100 V for a circular clamped cell. What do you expect from FEM, and where will the centre be at pull-in?

About 0.699 × 100 ≈ 70 V. The centre will have moved ≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), the average ≈ 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (not \(g/3\)). Residual tension or partial electrodes move this.

Q3A CMUT biased at 100 V is driven with 20 V amplitude. What is the second-harmonic force relative to the fundamental?

\(v/(4V_{\mathrm{DC}}) = 20/400 = 0.05\), i.e. −26 dB, before any gap nonlinearity.

Q4An AlN-on-Si PMUT (1 µm on 2 µm) has \(a\) = 40 µm. Estimate its resonance in vacuum and in water.

\(f\) scales as \(1/a^{2}\) from the \(a\) = 50 µm value 5.58 MHz: \(5.58\times(50/40)^{2} = 8.72\) MHz. \(\bar\rho h = 2330\times 2 + 3260\times 1\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) = 7.92 g/m², \(\beta = 0.67\times 1000\times 40\times 10^{-6}\) \(/\,7.92\times 10^{-3} = 3.38\), \(f_w = 8.72/\sqrt{4.38} = 4.17\) MHz (18.11).

Q5Design a 10 MHz PMN-PT/epoxy 1-3 composite at \(\nu\) = 0.5 with pillar width/thickness 0.5. Thickness, pillar width, pitch, kerf?

\(v \approx\) 3673 m/s (Smith–Auld) → \(t = v/(2f_0)\) = 184 µm. \(w = 0.5t\) = 92 µm, \(p = w/\sqrt{\nu}\) = 130 µm, kerf = \(p - w\) = 38 µm: still dicable. At 30 MHz everything shrinks threefold to a 13 µm kerf, below a practical saw.

Q6What average displacement amplitude does a CMUT need to produce 1 MPa at its surface at 3 MHz?

\(\bar x = p/(Z_w\cdot 2\pi f)\) \(= 10^{6}/(1.5\times 10^{6}\times 2\pi\times 3\times 10^{6})\) = 35 nm. With an average travel of about \(0.15\,g\) before pull-in, that asks for a gap of a few hundred nm, or collapse mode.

Q7Why does a PVDF membrane hydrophone give a larger open-circuit voltage than a PZT-5H disc of the same thickness, although PZT's \(d_{33}\) is 20 times larger?

Open-circuit voltage \(= g_{33}\cdot t\cdot p\) with \(g = d/\varepsilon^{T}\). PVDF: 0.33 V·m/N; PZT-5H: 622 pC/N / (\(3800\varepsilon_0\)) = 0.0185 V·m/N. The ratio is about 18 in PVDF's favour; PVDF also matches water (4 MRayl), so it disturbs the field less.
