CMUT, PMUT and new transducer materials

Module 19 introduced micromachined transducers with a lumped parallel-plate model. Here the membrane becomes a real clamped plate. That changes the pull-in voltage, the coupling and the collapse behaviour, and it explains where CMUT bandwidth, crosstalk and charging come from. The same plate physics, driven by a thin piezoelectric film, gives the PMUT. The last part updates the bulk-material choices: single crystals and their composites, lead-free ceramics, AC poling and transparent crystals, piezoelectric films and PVDF, with a sourced property table. In between, a packaging section takes the micromachined die to a probe: integration with CMOS, the lens on the membranes, bias and electrical safety, and a lifetime budget.

  4. Charging and nonlinearity: the electrostatic force grows with voltage squared, so a CMUT is inherently nonlinear. A PMUT bends only off the neutral axis: a PMUT (piezoelectric micromachined transducer) uses a film that shrinks sideways under voltage; this bends the plate only if the film sits off its neutral axis, hence a passive layer. From the field
  • Judge a new transducer technology at system level, not on one headline number. CMUTs and PMUTs win mainly on integration and cost (on-chip electronics, wafer-scale manufacture). Headline bandwidth shrinks: about 180 % in a 1D cell model, 91–105 % in measured immersion arrays, and it counts only if output pressure and signal-to-noise ratio survive. §18.3
  • Start lifetime stress tests in the first prototype round. Charge trapped in the CMUT insulator shifts the capacitance–voltage curve, moving the bias point and, near pull-in, risking collapse; collapse mode adds contact wear and sticking, and PMUT films need fatigue stability. Track the capacitance minimum and the resonance before and after each test. §18.4
  • Treat bias, leakage and patient isolation as day-one IEC 60601-1 (electrical safety) inputs. A CMUT holds tens of volts of bias (53 V pull-in, default cell) across sub-micrometre insulators behind the patient surface. Ground the patient-side electrode so the DC sits behind it, against a DC patient leakage limit usually quoted as 10 µA. §18.1 §18.6
  • Budget the CMUT transmit second harmonic before promising tissue-harmonic imaging. The force goes as voltage squared, putting \(v/(4V_{DC})\) of the fundamental at twice the frequency (v drive amplitude, \(V_{DC}\) bias): −20 dB at \(v=0.4V_{DC}\), which contaminates the tissue harmonic. Use small drive on a large bias, predistortion, or square-law-aware pulse inversion. §18.4
  • Size bias and coupling with a clamped-plate model, not the rigid piston. The plate centre moves first, so the piston model overstates pull-in voltage (76 V against 53 V, default cell) and coupling (0.43 against about 0.26 at 0.9 of pull-in). Bias sized on the piston number drives the cell into collapse. §18.2

18.1The CMUT cell as a clamped plate

A CMUT cell is a capacitor whose top plate is a membrane. From the bottom up: a conductive (doped) silicon substrate that serves as the bottom electrode, an insulating layer of silicon nitride or oxide, a sealed vacuum gap, and a membrane of silicon or silicon nitride carrying the top electrode. The membrane is typically 0.5–2 µm thick and 10–50 µm in radius, over gaps from about 40 nm to about 1 µm, and one array element is tens to hundreds of such cells wired in parallel.

Sources for the cell dimensions

The Stanford arrays described by Ergun et al. (SPIE 2005) used 1 µm membranes; Park et al. (MEMS 2008) reached 40 nm gaps, ±2 nm across the wafer.

With a bias \(V\) the membrane deflects toward the substrate by \(w(r)\). Each patch of membrane feels the local parallel-plate pressure, so the static problem is the clamped-plate equation with a nonlinear load (18.1)–(18.2):

Where the constants 56.9 and 27.8 come from

The piston spring \(k = 192\pi D/a^{2}\) is the force \(\pi a^{2}P\) divided by the average deflection \(Pa^{4}/(192D)\) of a clamped plate under uniform pressure \(P\). The distributed constant 27.8 comes from the finite-difference solution below (27.76; unchanged to 0.02 % between 60, 120 and 240 radial intervals). It holds for any \(a\), \(h\), \(g\) as long as the plate stays in small deflection (\(w \ll h\)) and the gap is uniform.

The piston is wrong by 30 % because it puts the whole electrode at the average gap. A plate concentrates its deflection at the centre, where the gap is smallest and the pressure, \(\propto 1/(g - w)^{2}\), is largest, so the centre softens first. At pull-in the centre has moved ≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) but the average only 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), less than half the piston\'s \(g/3\) (compare (18.3) and (18.4)).

How the two branches are computed

The centre value is 0.459 converged; coarse grids read slightly higher, and the insulator enters only through \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\). The conventional branch is traced by fixing the centre deflection \(w(0)\) and solving for both the shape and the voltage that holds it (a Newton solve on \([w;\ V^{2}]\)). Because \(w(0)\) increases monotonically along the branch, the solver walks through the pull-in fold, where \(V\) reaches its maximum, and continues on the unstable side. The collapsed branch (§18.2) is traced by fixing the contact radius and requiring zero contact force at its edge, which again turns the fold into a monotonic path.

Pitfall

"Pull-in at a third of the gap" is a statement about a rigid piston. For a clamped membrane, quote the centre deflection (≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\)) or the average deflection (≈ 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\)), and say which. Datasheets and FEM papers usually give the centre value, so do not compare it with \(g/3\).

18.2Coupling, collapse mode and hysteresis

Coupling is a stiffness ratio. Linearize the plate about its biased shape. Held at constant voltage (short circuit), the electrostatic pressure adds a negative stiffness at every node, \(-\varepsilon_0 V^{2}/(g_{\mathrm{eff}} - w)^{3}\) per unit area. Held at constant charge (open circuit), the electrode voltage readjusts as the capacitance changes, which adds back a rank-one term. The coupling coefficient is the fractional stiffness difference of the fundamental mode (18.5)–(18.6):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{K}_V &= \mathbf{K} - V^{2}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon_0 W_i}{(g_{\mathrm{eff}} - w_i)^{3}}\right) \\ \mathbf{K}_Q &= \mathbf{K}_V + \frac{V^{2}\,\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^{T}}{C} \end{aligned} \]

For a plate the rank-one term does not fully cancel the softening (for a piston it does, folded below), so \(k^{2}\) is lower: 0.04, 0.16, 0.26 and 0.37 at 0.5, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) for the default cell (piston: 0.08, 0.28, 0.43, 0.56). The piston limit of (18.6)

For a piston the rank-one term exactly cancels the softening (\(K_Q = k\)) and \(k^{2} = 1 - k_{\mathrm{eff}}/k\), the Module 19 formula.

Above \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) the centre lands on the insulator. The contact disc of radius \(b\) grows with bias and the free annulus between \(b\) and \(a\) carries the vibration (Figure 18.A, right). The annulus is a much stiffer plate, so the resonance rises steeply with \(b\). The field across the thin insulator in the contact region is enormous, so the annulus edge couples strongly: Bayram et al. report \(k_T^{2} \approx 0.7\) at about 60 % of the collapse voltage after collapse, versus 0.3 averaged over a conventional swing. The average displacement available for transmit is also larger (0.25–0.45 µm versus 0.05–0.15 µm in their 1 µm-gap design).

As the bias is lowered, the membrane stays down until the contact force at its edge can no longer be maintained, at the snap-back voltage \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\). In contact the electrostatic pressure is \(\varepsilon_0 V^{2}/\left(2(t_i/\varepsilon_r)^{2}\right)\), so the insulator controls \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\): the thinner the electrical gap at contact, the lower \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\) and the wider the hysteresis loop. For the default cell \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\) = 25.8 V = 0.49 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\); for a 1 µm gap with the same 0.1 µm nitride it is 0.26 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\).

18.3Bandwidth, the water load and crosstalk

Per unit area, a CMUT membrane is a mass \(m_a\) on a spring \(k_a\) radiating into water with resistance \(Z_w = \rho_w c_w\) = 1.5 MRayl. Using the piston-equivalent constants of the clamped plate (spring \(192D/a^{4}\), modal mass \(1.8\rho h\) for the average displacement), a 1 µm silicon membrane of 20 µm radius has \(m_a\) = 4.2 g/m² and \(k_a = 1.8\times 10^{13}\) Pa/m. It resonates at 10 MHz in vacuum, but its quality factor in water is \(Q = \sqrt{k_a m_a}/Z_w \approx 0.18\): deeply over-damped. Its velocity per unit force is then flat between two corner frequencies (18.7):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\mathrm{low}} &\approx \frac{\eta\, k_{a,\mathrm{eff}}}{2\pi Z_w}, \qquad f_{\mathrm{high}} \approx \frac{Z_w}{2\pi\eta\, m_a} \\ m_a &= 1.8\,\rho h + \frac{8}{3\pi}\,\rho_w a \end{aligned} \]
(18.7)

The added-mass term, and the PZT contrast

The second mass term is the water added mass of an isolated baffled cell, \((8/3)\rho_w a^{3}\) per cell; in an array moving in phase it is smaller, so the true \(f_{\mathrm{high}}\) lies between the two estimates. A PZT element, in contrast, is a half-wave resonator with mechanical impedance ≈ 34 MRayl and \(Q \approx (\pi/2)Z_p/(Z_B + Z_L)\) that needs matching layers to broaden (Modules 16–17).

18.4Making CMUTs: fabrication, charging, nonlinearity and stroke

CMUTs are made in two process families. In sacrificial release (surface micromachining) the gap is a sacrificial layer (polysilicon or oxide) deposited on the insulated substrate, covered by a deposited silicon nitride membrane, removed through etch holes and sealed under vacuum; the gap is the sacrificial thickness minus the nitride deposited during sealing. In wafer bonding the cavities are etched into a thermal oxide on one wafer and a silicon-on-insulator wafer is bonded on top; removing the handle and buried oxide leaves the single-crystal device layer (under 1.5 µm, uniform to better than 10 nm on 100 mm wafers) as the membrane (Ergun et al., SPIE 2005, Figs. 3 and 5). Bonding gives better membrane uniformity and material properties, but because the silicon membrane is conductive it needs a thicker insulator than a nitride membrane.

LOCOS and direct bonding (Park et al.)

Park et al. (MEMS 2008; J. Microelectromech. Syst. 2011) combined local oxidation (LOCOS) with direct bonding to set the gap and the oxide post height independently, reaching 40 nm gaps with ±2 nm uniformity, fewer breakdown paths and lower parasitic capacitance because most of the bonded area is oxide.

Charging follows from the fields. The vacuum gap sees fields of order \(V_{\mathrm{DC}}/g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), about 50 V over 0.21 µm, i.e. 240 V/µm; by continuity of \(D\) the nitride carries \(1/\varepsilon_r\) of that, about 30 V/µm (0.3 MV/cm), and in contact the full bias falls across \(t_i\), about 500 V/µm. Charge injected into the insulator adds a bias offset \(V_q\): the membrane responds to \(V - V_q\), so the C–V curve, the pull-in voltage and the sensitivity drift with use. §18.6 sizes the offset, (18.13), and the structural, electrical and operational remedies.

The electrostatic force is quadratic in voltage: with \(V = V_{\mathrm{DC}} + v\cos\omega t\) it contains \(V_{\mathrm{DC}}^{2} + v^{2}/2\) (static), \(2V_{\mathrm{DC}}v\cos\omega t\) (linear) and \((v^{2}/2)\cos 2\omega t\). The second-harmonic force is \(v/(4V_{\mathrm{DC}})\) of the fundamental: −32 dB at \(v = 0.1\,V_{\mathrm{DC}}\), −20 dB at \(v = 0.4\,V_{\mathrm{DC}}\). On top of that the \(1/(g - w)^{2}\) gap nonlinearity grows with stroke. For tissue-harmonic imaging the transmit harmonic must stay well below what tissue generates, which pushes CMUT drive schemes toward small AC on a large bias, predistortion, or pulse inversion that accounts for the \(V^{2}\) term.

The gap is also the stroke: a membrane cannot move further than its gap. Its plane-wave surface pressure is \(Z_w\omega\bar x\) for an average displacement amplitude \(\bar x\): 50 nm at 5 MHz gives 2.4 MPa. Large gaps cost bias voltage (\(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto g^{3/2}\)), small gaps cost output pressure; collapse mode and tall-gap designs with pre-charged or high-voltage bias are the standard answers to this trade.

Key takeaways
  • Sacrificial release deposits the membrane over a sacrificial gap; wafer bonding gives a single-crystal membrane uniform to better than 10 nm on 100 mm wafers, and LOCOS bonding reached 40 nm gaps with ±2 nm uniformity.
  • Charge injected into the insulator (≈ 30 V/µm in the nitride, ≈ 500 V/µm in contact) offsets the bias by Vq, so the C–V curve, pull-in voltage and sensitivity drift with use.
  • Force ∝ V²: the second-harmonic force is v/(4VDC) of the fundamental (−32 dB at 0.1 VDC, −20 dB at 0.4 VDC); the gap is the stroke, and 50 nm at 5 MHz gives 2.4 MPa.

18.6Packaging, bias and lifetime of micromachined probes

A micromachined die is not yet a probe. Between the die and the patient sit the interconnect to the electronics, a coating or lens that must protect a membrane a micrometre thick and insulate tens of volts, and a bias or film that has to stay put for years. This section follows those layers outward. Module 17 covers the same steps for a PZT stack (§17.11–§17.15), and Module 19 the array side (§19.4 matrix arrays, §19.5 micro-beamforming).

The interconnect problem starts with the pitch. A \(\lambda/2\)-pitch 2D aperture at 3 MHz has 257 µm elements, and 32 × 32 of them cannot each be cabled. Each micromachined element is also a small capacitor: about 2.1 pF for a 250 µm CMUT element with 0.8 fill and \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) = 0.213 µm. The divider of Module 17, §17.15, then decides the route. A 200 pF cable leaves −40 dB of the signal, whereas the 0.1–0.5 pF of a via and pad costs only 0.4–1.9 dB, which is why the electronics must sit within a fraction of a millimetre of the element.

There are three ways to get them that close (Table 18.3). In monolithic CMUT-on-CMOS the cells are built with a low-temperature process on the passivation of a finished CMOS wafer, so every element has its pulser and preamplifier underneath it; Zahorian et al. (IEEE TUFFC 2011) measured a parasitic capacitance 200 times lower than with wire bonding. With flip-chip bonding, a through-silicon via brings each element\'s contact to the back of the die, which is bump-bonded to an ASIC or fan-out (Oralkan et al., IEEE TUFFC 2003, Figure 18.C); where the pad grid differs from the element grid an interposer redistributes it (M17 §17.15). Wafer-to-wafer bonding suits PMUTs, and the film decides it: AlN is deposited below 400 °C and is CMOS-compatible, whereas sol-gel PZT is crystallized at higher temperature (typically 600–700 °C) and is made on its own wafer, then joined.

The lens or coating sits directly on the membranes, so it has three jobs instead of one: focus in elevation, insulate the patient from the electrodes, and protect a membrane about a micrometre thick. Silicone is the usual choice because its impedance is close to water's, so a layer of it barely changes the over-damped membrane's load; its loss is the same as in a PZT probe (Module 17 uses 1000 m/s and 3 dB/mm at 5 MHz, \(\propto f^{1.5}\)). A stiff conformal film behaves differently, because it becomes part of the plate. One micrometre of parylene (typical \(E\) ≈ 2.8 GPa, \(\rho\) ≈ 1290 kg/m³) on the default 1 µm silicon cell raises \(D\) by 24 % through (18.10) and the mass by 55 %, so \(f_{\mathrm{vac}}\) falls from 10.2 to 9.2 MHz and \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) rises 11 %, from 53.1 to 59.1 V. Wygant et al. (2008) tested in non-conductive oil and noted that in vivo use needs such an insulating layer; a lossy PDMS layer also lowers the interface-wave crosstalk (§18.3).

Bias delivery is a safety decision as much as a circuit one. A CMUT carries a DC bias (about 48 V at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) for the default cell, more in collapse mode) a few micrometres behind the patient surface. Wygant et al. put the negative bias on the top, patient-side electrode and noted that it could instead be grounded with the IC supplies shifted. Grounding the patient-side electrode is attractive because the DC then sits behind a grounded membrane and the coating only has to hold off pulses and faults. The limit that matters is a DC one: IEC 60601-1 is usually quoted with a DC patient leakage limit of 10 µA in normal condition and 50 µA under a single fault for every applied-part type (folded table). Two routes avoid an external bias altogether. A PMUT needs none, and a pre-charged CMUT stores its bias as charge deliberately trapped in the insulator (Munir et al. 2019; Saccher et al. 2023).

The number to compare it with is the pull-in margin. The default cell biased at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) sits 5.3 V below pull-in, so a 2.4 V offset of the right sign uses 45 % of that margin, and it moves the transmit force, \(\propto V - V_q\), by 5 % (2.4 of 47.8 V) before the steep coupling curve near pull-in amplifies the change. Mitigation works on each term of (18.13). Structure removes the insulator from the contact area: the isolation-post CMUT of Huang et al. (IEEE TUFFC 2005) keeps dielectric only on the posts, and the LOCOS process of §18.4 does the same with patterned oxide. Material choice changes the trap density, and Saccher et al. (IUS 2023) found that nitride traps more charge than alumina; that is a fault in a biased CMUT but the point of a pre-charged one, for which they predict 7.8 years of retention with nitride against 1.2 with alumina. Operation limits the injection by alternating the bias polarity, avoiding collapse contact where it is not needed, and tracking the C–V minimum so the bias can be trimmed. Most published reliability evidence comes from accelerated tests (review: Munir et al., Microelectron. Reliab. 2019).

PMUT films have their own lifetime physics, and it divides them cleanly. AlN is not ferroelectric: its polarity is fixed during growth, so it needs no poling and cannot depole, and it is already the film of mass-produced bulk-acoustic-wave filters. Its quality shows up as texture, residual stress (which shifts the resonance, (18.10)) and leakage. ScAlN raises \(d_{33}\) with scandium content (Table 18.2), but Fichtner et al. (J. Appl. Phys. 2019) observed ferroelectric switching from 27 % Sc, with a coercive field near 5 MV/cm at 27 % falling below 2 MV/cm at 43 %; below 22 % the films broke down before they could switch. A 20 V drive across 1 µm is 0.2 MV/cm, a tenth of the lowest of those fields, so normal drive does not switch it. PZT film is ferroelectric and ages after poling. Akkopru-Akgun et al. (J. Am. Ceram. Soc. 2019) measured \(d_{33,f}\) ageing of about 2–7 % per decade of time for Mn-doped films and 7–16 % per decade for Nb-doped films, aged at 180 °C, with imprint stabilizing the Mn-doped films. From one hour after poling to ten years is 4.9 decades, so 3 % per decade costs 15 % of \(d_{33,f}\) (−1.4 dB). Calibrate after pre-ageing, and drive PZT films unipolar along the poling direction, because a 10 V bipolar swing across 1 µm (100 kV/cm) exceeds the coercive field of typical PZT films.

Single-chip devices show what the integration buys. An 8960-element CMUT-on-CMOS chip forms a handheld probe that Rothberg et al. (PNAS 2021) report as cleared for 13 clinical indications (chip: Sanchez et al., ISSCC 2021), so one wideband 2D aperture covers applications that would otherwise need several PZT probes. The catheter chip of Table 18.3 runs 104 elements through 13 connections at about 20 mW. The fingerprint sensor of Jiang et al. (Microsyst. Nanoeng. 2017) puts 6160 AlN PMUTs on CMOS by wafer bonding, a consumer device that nonetheless proves the PMUT-on-CMOS route at array scale. What the open literature shows less of is long-term field data on drift, so a new design still has to generate its own.

  • A 250 µm CMUT element is about 2 pF: a 200 pF cable leaves −40 dB, a via and pad (0.1–0.5 pF) cost 0.4–1.9 dB, so the electronics go under the element: monolithic (200× less parasitic than wire bonds), flip-chip with through-silicon vias, or wafer-bonded PMUT-on-CMOS (AlN deposited below 400 °C).
  • The lens or coating sits on the membranes and also insulates and protects; soft silicone barely loads them, but 1 µm of parylene on the default cell lowers fvac from 10.2 to 9.2 MHz and raises VPI 11 %.
  • Ground the patient-side electrode so the DC bias sits behind it; the DC patient leakage limit is usually quoted as 10 µA (normal) and 50 µA (single fault). PMUTs and pre-charged CMUTs need no external bias.
  • Trapped charge offsets the bias by Vq = σtq/(ε0εr): 1012 e/cm² at the surface of 0.1 µm nitride is 2.4 V, 45 % of the default cell's 5.3 V margin at 0.9 VPI.
  • AlN cannot depole; ScAlN switches from about 27 % Sc, but only at 2–5 MV/cm; PZT film ages 2–16 % per decade (dopant-dependent, 180 °C), about −1.4 dB over ten years at 3 % per decade.

[001]-poled relaxor-PT crystals reach \(k_{33}\) above 0.9 and \(d_{33}\) above 1500 pC/N (Zhang et al., Prog. Mater. Sci. 2015). For a transducer the gain is \(k_t\) (0.55–0.59 against 0.51) and, more importantly, \(k_{33}\) in diced or composite form, which buys bandwidth and sensitivity. The price is thermal and electrical fragility: PMN-PT depoles near its rhombohedral-to-tetragonal transition at 86–100 °C and has a coercive field of only about 2 kV/cm. PIN-PMN-PT, the "second generation", raises \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) to 105–130 °C and \(E_c\) to 4.5–6 kV/cm at slightly lower \(d_{33}\).

Mn-doped crystals for high power

Mn doping raises the mechanical \(Q\) further (TRS lists \(Q_m \approx\) 500 for Mn:PIN-PMN-PT against 80 for PMN-PT), which matters for therapy and high-power pushes rather than imaging.

AC poling (an alternating poling field instead of DC) changes the domain structure of rhombohedral PMN-PT. Qiu et al. (Nature 2020) used it to remove the light-scattering 71° domain walls, obtaining near-perfect transparency together with \(d_{33}\) above 2100 pC/N and \(k_{33}\) ≈ 0.94. For ultrasound this matters twice: AC poling is now a route to higher \(d_{33}\) in ordinary crystal transducers, and transparent crystals let light pass through the transducer, which simplifies co-axial photoacoustic and optical-ultrasound probes.

A 1-3 composite of single-crystal pillars combines the crystal's \(k_{33}\) with the polymer's low impedance: \(k_t\) ≈ 0.83 against 0.69 for PZT-5H pillars, at the same 11–23 MRayl (Demo 18.6). The fine-scale requirement (Smith and Auld, IEEE TUFFC 1991) becomes the manufacturing limit at high frequency: at 20 MHz a half-wave PMN-PT composite is about 90 µm thick, so pillars must be under about 45 µm wide with narrow kerfs, beyond conventional dicing, which is why high-frequency composites are made by etching or other micro-machining routes.

Lead-free ceramics (KNN, Bi-based) are not yet a drop-in replacement for PZT-5H in imaging arrays (details folded below).

Above about 30 MHz single elements are thin and small, so electrical impedance, not coupling, often decides: LiNbO₃ (\(\varepsilon^{S}\) 39, \(k_t\) 0.49) gives a large element a convenient impedance where PZT-5H (\(\varepsilon^{S}\) 1470) would be a near short. PVDF sits at the other extreme: \(Z \approx\) 4 MRayl, close to water, \(k_t\) 0.14 and a tiny permittivity, which together give a weak transmitter but an excellent wideband receiver with \(g_{33} \approx\) 0.33 V·m/N, about 18 times PZT-5H's. That is why membrane and needle hydrophones (Modules 21 and 22) are PVDF.

Lead-free ceramics: KNN and Bi-based compositions

Regulatory pressure on lead drives work on (K,Na)NbO₃ (KNN) and Bi-based (BNT, BKT) ceramics. Saito et al. (Nature 2004) reported a KNN-based ceramic with \(d_{33}\) above 300 pC/N, 416 pC/N when textured; KNN films reach \(\lvert e_{31,f}\rvert\) = 8.5–14.4 C/m² (Table 18.2). For imaging arrays they are not yet a drop-in replacement for PZT-5H, mainly on coupling, temperature stability and processing; treat property claims in this area as composition- and process-specific.

The 2-2 variant

A 2-2 composite (plates instead of pillars, \(\nu = w/p\)) is easier to dice and suits 1D arrays, with coupling between the 1-3 and plate values.

18.9How experts think about it

A CMUT is a nonlinear capacitor with a plate for a top electrode: bias trades stiffness for coupling until the plate folds, and the water load turns its mass-spring resonance into a broadband response. A PMUT is the same plate with a built-in moment instead of a bias, so its design is about the neutral axis, the electrode radius and the film's figure of merit. Bulk materials are chosen on three numbers, coupling, impedance and robustness, and every new material buys one of them by spending another.

18.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What sets a CMUT's pull-in voltage, and how does it scale with membrane radius, thickness and gap? · screen

Balance of the plate's bending stiffness against the electrostatic pressure: \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}^{2} = c\cdot Dg_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3}/(\varepsilon_0 a^{4})\) with \(c\) = 27.8 for a clamped plate (56.9 for the piston idealization) (18.4), so \(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto h^{3/2}g_{\mathrm{eff}}^{3/2}/a^{2}\), and \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) includes \(t_i/\varepsilon_r\) of the insulator. A 1 µm Si membrane, 20 µm radius, 0.2 µm gap and 0.1 µm nitride: 53 V. Follow-up: "how would residual tensile stress change it?" It adds a membrane stiffness that raises \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) and the resonance; for thin, highly stressed membranes it dominates the bending term.

I2Why does a CMUT not need a matching layer, and what limits its bandwidth? · screen

Its moving mass per area is a few g/m², so \(\omega m\) is a fraction of water's 1.5 MRayl: the water load over-damps the mass–spring (\(Q \approx 0.2\)) and couples directly. The band runs from \(\approx \eta k_{\mathrm{eff}}/(2\pi Z_w)\) (spring) to \(\approx Z_w/(2\pi\eta m)\) (mass, including water added mass) (18.7). In practice higher membrane modes, parasitic capacitance, crosstalk and diffraction bring measured immersion bandwidths to about 100 % (Oralkan et al. 2002: 96 % uncompensated, 105 % compensated). Follow-up: "and what limits the low end?" Spring softening helps (\(k_{\mathrm{eff}}\) falls with bias), but element size limits radiation at low frequency.

I3Explain collapse-mode operation. Why would you use it, and what are the risks? · onsite

Biasing past \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) brings the membrane centre onto the insulator; the free annulus then vibrates at a higher, bias-tunable frequency (\(f/f_0 \approx 8.7\) at \(b/a\) = 0.5), with higher coupling (FEM: \(k_T^{2} \approx 0.7\)) and larger average stroke. The state is hysteretic: it releases only below \(V_{\mathrm{SB}}\), set by the insulator's electrical thickness. Risks: dielectric charging under very high contact fields, wear and stiction at the contact, temperature and bias drift of the operating point. Follow-up: "how do you get into collapse reliably?" Overdrive briefly above \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\), then hold a bias inside the hysteresis window.

I4How do you define and compute the coupling coefficient of a CMUT, and why is the piston value optimistic? · onsite

\(k^{2} = 1 - K_V/K_Q\) (or \(1 - C^{S}/C^{T}\) electrically): the fraction of stiffness that is electrical. For a plate, linearize about the biased shape, form \(\mathbf{K}_V = \mathbf{K}\) minus the nodal softening and \(\mathbf{K}_Q = \mathbf{K}_V + V^{2}\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c}^{T}/C\), and compare the lowest eigenvalues (18.5)–(18.6). The piston puts all softening into one degree of freedom and gets \(2\xi/(1 - \xi) = 0.43\) at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\); the plate gets 0.26 because only the centre softens strongly. Follow-up: "which one do you measure?" From \(f_r/f_a\) of the impedance or from \(C\) at constant bias, as for a piezo element (Module 16).

I5What is the main crosstalk mechanism in CMUT arrays and how do you suppress it? · onsite

A dispersive guided wave at the fluid–membrane interface, slower than \(c_w\) and cut off near the membrane resonance; Bayram et al. (2007) measured −17 dB on the first neighbour near 2.1 MHz in conventional mode. It is a property of the compliant surface, not of the substrate (the substrate Lamb modes A0 and S0 were about 16 and 41 dB below the interface mode: −40 and −65 dB against −24.4 dB, all relative to the transmitting element). Suppression: operate in collapse mode (cutoff rises, average −39 dB), add a lossy coating (PDMS gave only a few dB in Bayram's experiments), shape the cell layout or trench between elements, or compensate in the beamformer. Follow-up: "why does it get worse with bias?" Softening lowers the cutoff into the band.

I6Sacrificial release or wafer bonding for a new CMUT array: how do you choose? · onsite

Wafer bonding gives single-crystal silicon membranes with excellent thickness control (device layer under 1.5 µm, < 10 nm variation), any cell shape and fill factor, and gap control by oxidation (LOCOS: 40 nm ± 2 nm), but needs SOI wafers, a thicker insulator for the conductive membrane, and is harder to do on top of CMOS. Sacrificial release uses deposited nitride membranes, works at lower temperature and is one natural route for CMUT-on-CMOS (low-temperature bonding onto the CMOS wafer is the other), at the cost of membrane stress and thickness variation and etch-hole sealing. Follow-up: "which would you use for a monolithic 2D array?" Sacrificial release (or low-temperature bonding variants), because the CMOS limits the thermal budget.

I7Sketch a 2D CMUT-on-CMOS array for 3 MHz cardiac imaging. What dominates the design? · research

\(\lambda/2\) = 257 µm pitch; a 20 × 20 mm aperture is about 80 × 80 = 6400 elements, impossible to cable, so each element gets a pulser and LNA under it and micro-beamforming reduces the channel count to the system (Module 19). Dominant constraints: power density (a few mW per channel already gives watts on a few cm2 at the patient), element capacitance versus LNA noise, bias distribution (tens of volts on a CMOS die, isolated per element or shared), acoustic crosstalk through the interface wave, and thermal limits on the probe face (Module 22). Follow-up: "how do you test it?" Per-element impulse response and C–V maps on wafer before dicing, then pulse-echo uniformity.

I8Give the PMUT resonance formula. Why is the electrode only about 70 % of the radius? · screen

\(f = 10.22/(2\pi a^{2})\cdot\sqrt{D/\bar\rho h}\) for a clamped bilayer plate with laminated stiffness \(D\), divided by \(\sqrt{1 + 0.67\rho_w a/\bar\rho h}\) in water (18.9)–(18.11). The electrode applies a uniform moment; the mode curvature changes sign at \(r = a/\sqrt{2}\), so the work done is \(\propto \beta_e^{2}(1 - \beta_e^{2})\): zero for a full electrode, maximal at 0.707. Example: AlN 1 µm on Si 2 µm, \(a\) = 50 µm: 5.6 MHz in vacuum, 2.4 MHz in water. Follow-up: "what does residual stress do?" Tension raises \(f\) and pushes toward membrane behaviour, compression lowers it and can buckle the plate.

I9AlN or PZT film for a PMUT receiver? For a transmitter? · onsite

Receiver: open-circuit voltage per stress \(\propto e_{31,f}/(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{33})\): AlN 11.3 GV/m and Sc0.15AlN 15 GV/m beat sol-gel PZT (0.7–1.8 as published) because their permittivity is 10–12 rather than hundreds; AlN also has a lower loss tangent. Transmitter: force per volt \(\propto e_{31,f}\), 8–14 C/m² for PZT versus 1.05 for AlN, and energy FOM \(e^{2}/\varepsilon\) up to 110 GPa for epitaxial PZT. ScAlN doubles AlN's energy FOM while staying CMOS-friendly. Follow-up: "what about one die doing both?" Use separate receive and transmit electrodes, or a ScAlN compromise.

I10Would you put single-crystal composite in a 2D matrix TEE probe? What would worry you? · research

The gain: \(k_t \approx\) 0.8 (composite) versus ≈ 0.5–0.7, which means wider bandwidth and higher sensitivity, valuable for harmonic imaging through a small aperture. The worries: PMN-PT depoles near \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) 86–100 °C and has \(E_c \approx\) 2 kV/cm, so bonding, sterilization and self-heating at the probe face, and bipolar drive at high voltage, all eat the margin; PIN-PMN-PT (\(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) about 100–135 °C and \(E_c\) about 4.5–7 kV/cm, depending on the supplier; see Module 16 §16.3) is the usual answer. Also cost, dicing yield at small pitch, and the higher permittivity, which helps small elements drive the in-probe electronics. Follow-up: "Mn doping?" Raises \(Q_m\) (≈ 500), useful for therapy, less for imaging bandwidth.

I11What is AC poling and why did it attract attention? · research

Poling a relaxor-PT crystal with an alternating field instead of DC. In rhombohedral PMN-PT it changes the domain configuration; Qiu et al. (Nature 2020) removed the light-scattering 71° domain walls and obtained near-perfect optical transparency with \(d_{33}\) above 2100 pC/N and \(k_{33} \approx 0.94\). For ultrasound it offers higher \(d_{33}\) from the same crystal and transparent transducers through which light can be delivered, which simplifies photoacoustic and optical–acoustic probes. Follow-up: "what limits it?" Temperature stability and the same \(T_{\mathrm{rt}}\) and \(E_c\) constraints as any PMN-PT; poling-condition reproducibility in production.

I12You are taking a 2D CMUT array from wafer to a clinical probe. Which packaging decisions dominate, and in what order? · onsite

Interconnect first, because it is set by physics: a 250 µm element is about 2 pF, so a 200 pF cable leaves −40 dB while a via and pad (0.1–0.5 pF) cost 0.4–1.9 dB. That forces electronics under the element, either monolithic (low-temperature cells on finished CMOS, about 200× less parasitic than wire bonds) or flip-chip through-silicon vias to an ASIC, with an interposer if the grids differ (Module 17 §17.15, Module 19 §19.5). Then the coating: silicone near water impedance barely loads the membranes, but a stiff film becomes part of the plate (1 µm parylene: \(f_{\mathrm{vac}}\) −11 %, \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) +11 %). Then bias and safety: ground the patient-side electrode so the DC sits behind it, design the insulation against the DC patient leakage limit (usually quoted as 10 µA normal, 50 µA single fault). Finally lifetime: budget \(V_q\) (18.13) against the pull-in margin and run accelerated tests that track the C–V minimum and the resonance. Follow-up: what changes for a PMUT? No bias; the film's thermal budget decides the integration (AlN below 400 °C on CMOS, PZT on its own wafer), and PZT ageing replaces charging.

I13A CMUT probe's sensitivity drifts down over weeks of use. How do you diagnose and fix it? · research

Measure the C–V curve. A sideways shift of its minimum is a bias offset \(V_q\) from trapped charge; convert it to a sheet density with (18.13) and compare it with the pull-in margin (5.3 V for the default cell at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\)). A change in shape or height points elsewhere: a damaged or stiffened membrane, a delaminated or swollen lens, or leakage, which the resonance frequency and the impedance spectrum separate. Charging fixes act on each term: re-trim the bias from the tracked C–V minimum, alternate the bias polarity, avoid collapse contact, and in the next design move the dielectric off the contact area (isolation posts, patterned oxide) or choose a lower-trap dielectric. Follow-up: in a pre-charged CMUT the trapped charge is the bias, so retention becomes the lifetime (Saccher et al. 2023 predict 7.8 years for nitride against 1.2 for alumina).

18.13Self-check

Q1You double the membrane thickness and halve the radius of a CMUT cell. By what factor does \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) change, and the vacuum resonance?

\(V_{\mathrm{PI}} \propto h^{3/2}/a^{2} \to 2^{1.5}\times 4 = 11.3\) times higher. \(f \propto h/a^{2} \to 2\times 4 = 8\) times higher. Both at fixed gap and insulator.

Q2A colleague's piston model gives \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\) = 100 V for a circular clamped cell. What do you expect from FEM, and where will the centre be at pull-in?

About 0.699 × 100 ≈ 70 V. The centre will have moved ≈ 0.46 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\), the average ≈ 0.15 \(g_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (not \(g/3\)). Residual tension or partial electrodes move this.

Q3A CMUT biased at 100 V is driven with 20 V amplitude. What is the second-harmonic force relative to the fundamental?

\(v/(4V_{\mathrm{DC}}) = 20/400 = 0.05\), i.e. −26 dB, before any gap nonlinearity.

Q4An AlN-on-Si PMUT (1 µm on 2 µm) has \(a\) = 40 µm. Estimate its resonance in vacuum and in water.

\(f\) scales as \(1/a^{2}\) from the \(a\) = 50 µm value 5.58 MHz: \(5.58\times(50/40)^{2} = 8.72\) MHz. \(\bar\rho h = 2330\times 2 + 3260\times 1\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) = 7.92 g/m², \(\beta = 0.67\times 1000\times 40\times 10^{-6}\) \(/\,7.92\times 10^{-3} = 3.38\), \(f_w = 8.72/\sqrt{4.38} = 4.17\) MHz (18.11).

Q5Design a 10 MHz PMN-PT/epoxy 1-3 composite at \(\nu\) = 0.5 with pillar width/thickness 0.5. Thickness, pillar width, pitch, kerf?

\(v \approx\) 3673 m/s (Smith–Auld) → \(t = v/(2f_0)\) = 184 µm. \(w = 0.5t\) = 92 µm, \(p = w/\sqrt{\nu}\) = 130 µm, kerf = \(p - w\) = 38 µm: still dicable. At 30 MHz everything shrinks threefold to a 13 µm kerf, below a practical saw.

Q6What average displacement amplitude does a CMUT need to produce 1 MPa at its surface at 3 MHz?

\(\bar x = p/(Z_w\cdot 2\pi f)\) \(= 10^{6}/(1.5\times 10^{6}\times 2\pi\times 3\times 10^{6})\) = 35 nm. With an average travel of about \(0.15\,g\) before pull-in, that asks for a gap of a few hundred nm, or collapse mode.

Q7Why does a PVDF membrane hydrophone give a larger open-circuit voltage than a PZT-5H disc of the same thickness, although PZT's \(d_{33}\) is 20 times larger?

Open-circuit voltage \(= g_{33}\cdot t\cdot p\) with \(g = d/\varepsilon^{T}\). PVDF: 0.33 V·m/N; PZT-5H: 622 pC/N / (\(3800\varepsilon_0\)) = 0.0185 V·m/N. The ratio is about 18 in PVDF's favour; PVDF also matches water (4 MRayl), so it disturbs the field less.
