Array architectures: 1D, 1.25D/1.5D/1.75D, 2D matrix, row-column, CMUT/PMUT

An array is a sampled aperture whose samples are themselves small apertures, wired to a system with a fixed number of channels. This module turns those three facts into design rules: element directivity and pitch for 1D arrays, elevation control from a fixed lens to 1.75D, and the channel-count problem of 2D arrays with its four answers (sparse layouts, multiplexing, micro-beamforming in the probe, row-column addressing), then the micromachined transducers that make dense 2D apertures and in-probe electronics practical.

19.1The element: geometry, directivity and crosstalk

A 1D array element is a strip of width w (azimuth) and height H (elevation), separated from its neighbour by a kerf; pitch \(d = w\) + kerf. Typical numbers: kerf 20–40 µm, fill fraction \(w/d \approx 0.8\text{–}0.9\), H from about 3 mm (high-frequency linear) to well over 10 mm (low-frequency abdominal). In azimuth the element is a small aperture, so its far-field directivity is the Fourier transform of its velocity profile:

\[ \begin{gathered} E_{\mathrm{rigid}}(\theta) = \operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{w\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right) \\ E_{\mathrm{soft}}(\theta) = \operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{w\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)\cos\theta \\ \operatorname{sinc}(x) = \frac{\sin(\pi x)}{\pi x} \end{gathered} \]
(19.1)

Crosstalk

Crosstalk is any path by which driving one element moves its neighbours: mechanical coupling through the kerf filler, backing and matching layers; guided waves (Lamb and Scholte-type waves) in the lens and at the lens–tissue interface; and capacitive coupling in the flex circuit and cable. It widens the effective element (so the directivity narrows below the sinc prediction), and guided waves add lobes at the angle where their trace velocity matches the wave in tissue. Full dicing through the matching layers, soft kerf fillers and sub-dicing of wide elements (to push lateral modes above the band, Module 17) are the standard countermeasures. Nearest-neighbour crosstalk is commonly measured by driving one element and recording its neighbours electrically or with a laser vibrometer, with design targets around −30 dB or lower.

Intuition

The element factor is the same Fourier pair as the array factor, one scale smaller. The array samples the aperture at pitch d, so its pattern repeats every \(\lambda /d\) in \(\sin \theta\); each sample has width \(w \approx d\), so the element factor is a sinc whose first null sits at \(\sin \theta = \lambda /w\), right next to the first grating lobe at \(\lambda /d\). That is why the element factor helps against grating lobes when steering is small and cannot help when steering is large.

Pitfall

The pitch rule is a narrowband statement at one frequency. A "\(\lambda\)/2" phased array designed at 2.5 MHz is a \(0.80\lambda\) array at its 4 MHz band edge and for harmonic imaging receive. Broadband pulses smear grating lobes and lower their peak (Module 23), so real designs accept some violation at the band edge; quote the frequency whenever you quote \(d/\lambda\).

19.3Elevation: fixed lens, 1.25D, 1.5D, 1.75D

A 1D array focuses electronically in azimuth only. In elevation it has a fixed aperture H and a fixed focus set by the lens (Module 17), so the slice thickness (the elevation −6 dB two-way beam width) is small only near the lens focus and grows on either side. Multi-row arrays split H into rows. The naming is standardised by what the rows can do (Wildes et al., IEEE TUFFC 1997):

For QUS (backscatter coefficient, attenuation by spectral difference) the elevation beam enters every diffraction correction. A 1D probe's reference-phantom method cancels it only if the phantom is scanned at the same depths and settings; a 1.5D or 1.75D probe whose elevation aperture switches with depth changes the diffraction term at each switch, which shows up as steps in the attenuation estimate if the reference data were not acquired with identical switching.

Sparse arrays are Module 23's random thinning in 2D. With K elements placed at random among \(N^2\) grid positions, far-field phasors at a sidelobe direction add as a random walk, so the mean sidelobe power relative to the peak is \((N^2 - K)/(K(N^2 - 1)) \approx 1/K\): −25.3 dB for \(K = 256\) of 1024, two-way about twice that in dB for independent transmit and receive sets, because the two-way pattern is the product of the one-way patterns, the transform of the coarray (transmit layout convolved with receive layout, Section 23.4). Periodic thinning instead puts grating lobes at full height. Deterministic layouts (vernier pairs, Fermat or density-tapered spirals) shape the floor; density-tapered spirals of 256 elements let a single 256-channel system drive a 2D aperture (Ramalli et al., IEEE TUFFC 2015). Fully wired research systems have driven 32 × 32 matrices with 1024 channels for 3D ultrafast imaging (Provost et al., Phys. Med. Biol. 2014).

Grating lobes in two dimensions: the (u, v) plane

In 2D the direction of a plane wave needs two coordinates. Use the direction cosines \(u = \sin\theta\cos\varphi\), \(v = \sin\theta\sin\varphi\), with \(\theta\) measured from the array normal and \(\varphi\) the azimuth from the x axis. Every real direction lies in the unit disc \(u^2 + v^2 \le 1\), the visible region. Steering to \((\theta_0, \varphi_0)\) is a linear phase in both coordinates, so the pattern of an \(N_x \times N_y\) grid is the 1D array factor (19.3) in each axis, shifted to the steering point, and it repeats on a lattice:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{AF}(u, v) = \mathrm{AF}_{N_x, d_x}(u - u_0)\cdot \mathrm{AF}_{N_y, d_y}(v - v_0) \\ (u_{pq},\, v_{pq}) = \left(u_0 + \frac{p\,\lambda}{d_x},\ v_0 + \frac{q\,\lambda}{d_y}\right) \\ \text{grating lobe} \iff u_{pq}^2 + v_{pq}^2 \le 1,\quad (p, q) \ne (0, 0) \end{gathered} \]
(19.7)

\(\lvert\mathrm{AF}\rvert = 1\) at every replica centre, so a replica inside the unit circle is a grating lobe at full main-lobe height, reduced only by the element factor, \(E(u_{pq}, v_{pq})/E(u_0, v_0)\). Replicas outside the circle are evanescent, but a replica within about one main-lobe width of the edge still leaks its skirt into visible space. In 2D the 1D rule \(\sin\theta_g = \sin\theta_0 \pm \lambda/d\) becomes "translate a lattice and look through a round window".

Steering moves the whole lattice by \((u_0, v_0)\); the window stays put. A grid is free of grating lobes for every steering direction inside a cone \(\sin\theta \le \sin\theta_{\max}\) if every replica stays outside the unit circle while the main lobe sweeps a disc of radius \(\sin\theta_{\max}\), that is, if every nonzero lattice vector is longer than \(1 + \sin\theta_{\max}\). For a rectangular grid the shortest is \(\lambda/\max(d_x, d_y)\). 19.5Micro-beamforming: subarray grating lobes and the steering limit (4D TEE)

Micro-beamforming splits each beamforming delay into a fine part applied in the probe and a coarse part applied in the system. Elements are grouped into m × m subarrays. An ASIC under (or next to) the array amplifies each element, delays it by a small programmable amount, and sums the group onto one cable conductor; the system then beamforms the subarray outputs as if each were one large element. Savord and Solomon described a fully sampled matrix transducer for real-time 3D built this way (IEEE Ultrasonics Symposium 2003), and it is the architecture inside matrix TEE and transthoracic volume probes. In TEE it is not optional: the cable runs through a gastroscope, so the channel reduction must happen in the tip.

Write element n as subarray centre \(X_k\) plus local offset \(\xi_i\). The fine delays steer each subarray toward \(u_0 = \sin \theta_0\) (the pre-steer direction); the coarse delays steer the subarray centres toward the beam direction \(u_s\). The far-field pattern factorizes:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(u) &= \sum_k e^{\,j2\pi X_k (u - u_s)/\lambda}\cdot \sum_i e^{\,j2\pi \xi_i (u - u_0)/\lambda} \\ &= \mathrm{CAF}(u - u_s)\cdot \mathrm{SF}(u - u_0) \end{aligned} \]

This one equation gives the whole error budget:

  • On the pre-steer direction \((u_s = u_0)\), every coarse grating lobe lands on a null of the subarray factor and cancels exactly (narrowband). The micro-beamformed aperture is then equivalent to the fully sampled one.
  • Off the pre-steer direction by \(\delta\), the main lobe is weighted by \(\mathrm{SF}(\delta)\) (a gain loss) and the grating lobes at \(u_s \pm \lambda /(md)\) are no longer on nulls. For 3 × 3 subarrays at \(\lambda /2\): \(\delta = 5^{\circ}\) costs 0.2 dB with lobes at −15 dB; \(\delta = 10^{\circ}\) costs 0.9 dB with a −8.3 dB lobe; \(\delta = 20^{\circ}\) and the lobe is as large as the main beam.
  • Curvature inside a subarray is negligible. The quadratic (focusing) phase across a subarray of width md is \(\pi (md/2)^{2}/(\lambda R)\): 0.027 rad for \(3 \times \lambda /2\) at \(R = 20\) mm and 5 MHz. Only the direction each subarray looks matters. When focusing near the probe, outer subarrays see the focus from a different angle than the centre; if all subarrays share one pre-steer, the outer ones are effectively steered off by \(\delta_k \approx \arctan (X_k /R)\), and at F# 1 the edge sees 27°.
  • Channel reduction is \(m^2\) (9 for 3 × 3, 16 for 4 × 4, 25 for 5 × 5); the subarray must stay small because the subarray pitch md sets where the coarse grating lobes sit.

The steering window, in closed form. What the 4D TEE literature reports

Commercial matrix TEE probes carry on the order of a few thousand elements in the tip. Published academic designs show the numbers concretely: a 32 × 32 PZT matrix on a front-end ASIC with 3 × 3 receive subarrays and 96 delay-and-sum subarray beamformers, a ninefold reduction in receive cables at 0.27 mW per channel (Chen et al., IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits 2017); its acoustic characterization reports 5.8 MHz centre frequency, a directly wired 8 × 8 transmit sub-aperture and ±37° pre-steering range (Daeichin et al., Ultrasound Med. Biol. 2018). Bera et al. (Phys. Med. Biol. 2018) built multiline 3D beamforming on such micro-beamformed data and report volume rates of 300 Hz with 25 transmit events and 44 Hz with 169; partitioned transmit/receive apertures with 4 × 4 receive subarrays reached up to 59 Hz (Ultrasound Med. Biol. 2018).

Ultrasound link: 4D TEE

Three consequences for anyone processing matrix-TEE data. First, there are no per-element channel data: every algorithm that needs them (aberration correction by element-wise delay estimation, coherence factors computed over elements, sound-speed estimation from channel-data focusing) can only work on subarray outputs, a coarser and pre-steered aperture. Second, the receive beam quality varies across the volume with the distance from each subarray's pre-steer direction, so a phantom PSF measured on axis is not the PSF at the sector edge with parallel beams. Third, the per-channel ASIC power (sub-mW) times thousands of elements sets the tip temperature budget, which in turn caps transmit voltage, duty cycle and volume rate.

Pitfall

"The micro-beamformer limits the steering angle" is imprecise. Fine delays are programmable per beam, so a single receive line at 40° can be pre-steered to 40°. What is limited is the angular distance between the pre-steer direction and any beam formed from the same subarray data (parallel receive beams, retrospective beamforming), and the maximum fine delay the analog delay lines hold, \((m - 1)d \sin \theta_0 /c\) across the subarray per axis: 120 ns for \(3 \times \lambda /2\) at 37° and 5 MHz.

19.6Row-column addressed arrays: N + N channels for an N × N aperture

Pattern the top electrode of a 2D plate into N rows and the bottom electrode into N columns. Each row is one long element along x, each column one long element along y, and the plate is addressed with 2N connections instead of \(N^2\) (256 instead of 16 384 for 128 + 128). The idea was assessed theoretically as a "crossed electrode" 2D array by Morton and Lockwood (IEEE Ultrasonics Symposium 2003) and built at scale as a 256 × 256 row-column array by Seo and Yen (IEEE TUFFC 2009). The standard scheme transmits on the rows and receives on the columns:

  • Transmit: delaying the rows focuses (or steers) the beam in y only. Each row is uniform along x, so the transmit field is a fan focused in y and unfocused in x.

Edge waves and ghost echoes

A finite strip with uniform weighting radiates like three sources: the stationary point closest to the scatterer and the two ends, where the truncated aperture launches edge waves (the same edge waves that give a focused beam its far sidelobes, Module 23). In transmit-receive, three sources times three receivers give up to nine echoes per scatterer (Rasmussen et al. 2015); after beamforming the ones from the strip ends appear as ghost copies of bright targets. Apodizing along each strip removes them, but a strip has one electrical connection, so the weighting has to be built into the element itself. Rasmussen et al. did this with a roll-off region at each strip end, in which the effective weighting built into the element tapers over \(16\lambda\) and report the peak ghost reduced by 43 dB at (8, 3, 30) mm with the main lobe essentially unchanged; a plain Hann weighting along the strips also suppresses ghosts but attenuated the response at that position by 24 dB because off-centre points see the low-weight part of the strip.

Why CMUTs are broadband. The membrane's mechanical impedance per area near resonance is of order \(\omega \rho h\): for 1 µm of silicon at 10 MHz, \(2\pi \cdot 10^{7} \cdot 2.33 \times 10^{-3} \approx 0.15\) MRayl, a tenth of water's 1.5 MRayl. The water load dominates and damps the resonance (Q well below 1), so the bandwidth is set by the electrical and spring roll-offs and by higher membrane modes rather than by a mechanical Q; fractional bandwidths above 100 % have been reported in immersion (Oralkan et al., IEEE TUFFC 2002). No matching layer is needed because there is no high-impedance ceramic to match.

Collapse mode. Biasing beyond pull-in lets the membrane centre touch the insulated substrate; the free annulus then vibrates at a higher frequency with higher coupling and output pressure (Bayram et al., IEEE TUFFC 2003). Lowering the bias releases it only at a lower snap-back voltage (hysteresis). Charge trapping in the dielectric shifts the effective bias over time and is a classic CMUT reliability issue, together with the high DC bias and the nonlinearity of the electrostatic force (\(V^2\), which generates harmonics unless the AC drive is small compared with the bias).

PMUT. A piezoelectric micromachined ultrasonic transducer is a thin piezoelectric film (AlN, ScAlN or PZT) on a passive membrane, driven in flexure through the transverse (\(e_{31}\)) effect (Qiu et al., Sensors 2015). It needs no bias, has an impedance that suits low-voltage CMOS, and AlN is CMOS-process compatible; the price is lower effective coupling (a few percent for AlN films) and usually narrower bandwidth than a CMUT. Its resonance follows the same plate law, \(f \propto h/a^2\). CMUT-on-CMOS has reached a commercial handheld 2D-array probe, and PMUT-based handheld probes are also sold; integration on CMOS is the route where micromachining pays off most: thousands of elements bonded directly to the electronics that solve the channel problem.

A conventional probe connects through a multi-pin connector (zero-insertion-force designs with a few hundred pins are common) and a 1.5–2.5 m bundle of micro-coax lines with capacitance of roughly 50–120 pF/m. For matrix probes the cable carries subarray outputs, digitized or multiplexed data, bias and control lines, and power for the ASIC, which is why matrix probe cables are designed together with the in-probe electronics rather than as passive wiring.

19.9How experts think about it

An array designer sees three nested Fourier pairs and one wiring budget. The element is a small aperture whose transform is the acceptance cone; the array samples the aperture, so its transform repeats at \(\lambda /d\); a subarray is an aperture of apertures, so its transform multiplies a coarse lattice whose replicas it must null. The wiring budget (channels, cable capacitance, data rate, tip power) decides which of these structures the hardware can afford, and every architecture from 1.5D to row-column to micro-beamforming is a different compromise between how many independent delays exist and where they are applied.

19.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why is a phased array pitched at \(\lambda /2\) and a linear array at about \(\lambda\)? · screen

Grating lobes sit at \(\sin \theta_0 \pm \lambda /d\). A phased array steers the whole aperture over ±45° or more, so it needs \(d \le \lambda /(1 + \sin \theta_{\mathrm{max}})\): \(0.59\lambda\) for 45°, \(\lambda /2\) for a full sector. A linear array translates an unsteered sub-aperture; with \(d = \lambda\) the first replica is at ±90°, where the element factor is zero, so it is harmless. Follow-up: "what happens when you steer the linear array 20° for compounding?" The lobe enters at −41°, about 6 dB below the main lobe narrowband; broadband pulses and the transmit/receive product lower it further, but that is why linear-array steering stays modest.

I2Define 1.25D, 1.5D and 1.75D arrays precisely. · screen

All have several rows in elevation. 1.25D: the elevation aperture is switched (outer rows added with depth), focus fixed by the lens. 1.5D: aperture, apodization and focus are dynamic, but rows are paired symmetrically about the centreline, so no elevation steering; channels N(R + 1)/2. 1.75D: rows independent, so the delay profile can be asymmetric and the beam can steer a few degrees; channels NR. 2D: steering in elevation comparable to azimuth. Follow-up: "why does 1.5D help QUS?" Thinner and more uniform slice over depth means less partial-volume and a diffraction term that changes less with depth, but switching steps must be matched in the reference data.

I3What is the element factor, and how does it limit the receive F-number? · screen

The far-field directivity of one element: \(\operatorname{sinc} (w \sin \theta /\lambda)\) for a rigid baffle, times \(\cos \theta\) for a soft baffle, which matches kerfed arrays better. Edge elements of an aperture at F# see the pixel at atan(1/(2F#)); if that exceeds the −6 dB angle they add noise and clutter faster than signal. \(F\#_{\min} = 1/(2 \tan \theta_{-6\,\mathrm{dB}})\): 0.69 for 0.27 mm elements at 5 MHz, 1.05 at 7.5 MHz. Follow-up: "so what F# should the beamformer use for a broadband pulse?" A frequency-dependent one, or apodization based on element directivity, because the acceptance cone is twice as wide at the low band edge as at the high one.

I4You must design a 3D TEE probe with a few thousand elements, but the cable carries about 100 conductors. Walk through the options. · onsite

Required reduction ≈ 30×. Fully wired is impossible through a gastroscope. Sparse: keeps the aperture with \(K \approx 100\) elements but the floor is ≈ −20 dB one-way and sensitivity drops by K/N; unacceptable for cardiac contrast. RCA: 2N lines (a 64 × 64 aperture in 128 lines) but one-way focusing per dimension and a rectilinear field of view, a poor fit for a sector through a small window. Micro-beamforming: subarrays of 5 × 5 to 6 × 6 cut channels 25–36× with full sensitivity, at the cost of an ASIC in the tip (power ≈ sub-mW per element against a temperature limit), fine delays with limited range, and pre-steering limits on multiline. That is why matrix TEE uses micro-beamforming. Follow-up: "how do you transmit?" Pulsers in the ASIC with per-element delays, or a smaller directly wired transmit sub-aperture as in the 32 × 32 academic designs.

I5Derive the micro-beamformer pattern and say where its errors come from. · onsite

Element position = subarray centre \(X_k\) + offset \(\xi_i\). Fine delays steer to \(u_0\), coarse delays steer centres to \(u_s\): \(P(u) = \mathrm{CAF}(u - u_s) \cdot \mathrm{SF}(u - u_0)\). CAF has grating lobes at \(u_s \pm \lambda /(md)\); SF has nulls at \(u_0 \pm \lambda /(md)\). Equal steering cancels the lobes. Errors: (1) \(\delta = \theta_s - \theta_0 \ne 0\) (parallel beams, near-field focusing with shared pre-steer): gain \(\mathrm{SF}(\delta)\) and lobes \(\mathrm{SF}(\delta - \lambda /(md))\); 3 × 3 at \(\lambda /2\): −0.9 dB and −8 dB lobe at 10°. (2) Curvature inside the subarray: \(\pi (md/2)^{2}/(\lambda R)\), negligible for small m beyond a few mm. (3) Fine-delay range and quantization, and channel gain mismatch in the ASIC. Follow-up: "why do grating lobes matter less in pulse-echo?" The transmit beam multiplies them; they hurt most with broad transmits and many parallel receive beams.

I6How does a row-column array work, what is its PSF, and what are the ghosts? · onsite

Top electrodes in rows, bottom in columns: 2N connections for an N × N footprint. Transmit on rows focuses in y only, receive on columns focuses in x only, so \(\mathrm{PSF} = T(y)R(x)\): one-way per dimension, −13.3 dB axis sidelobes (uniform) and 1.21 \(\lambda\)F# width instead of −26.5 dB and 0.89 \(\lambda\)F#. Each strip acts as three sources (the closest point and two ends), so a scatterer gives up to nine echoes; the end contributions beamform into ghosts (the strongest edge echo is −26 dB in Rasmussen et al.'s single row/column simulation, their Fig. 4). Fix: weighting built into the strip ends (roll-off apodization), which keeps the centre at unit weight; Hann along the whole strip also works but kills off-centre sensitivity. Follow-up: "how do you get high volume rates?" Orthogonal plane waves: rows transmit tilted plane waves, columns receive, then swap, and compound.

I7A random sparse 2D array uses 256 of 1024 positions. Predict its sidelobes and say how you would improve it. · onsite

Mean sidelobe power ≈ \((N^2 - K)/(K(N^2 - 1)) = -25.3\) dB; the peak sidelobe of a draw is 10–15 dB higher, near −10 to −15 dB, comparable to a uniform aperture's −13 dB but everywhere. Improvements: independent transmit and receive layouts so the two-way floor multiplies (or vernier/coarray designs with no gaps), density tapering (spirals) to apodize the floor, optimization of the layout, broadband pulses which decorrelate the random sum across frequency. Follow-up: "what does thinning cost besides sidelobes?" Receive \(\mathrm{SNR} \propto K\) and transmitted energy ∝ K: −6 dB each for \(K = N^2 /4\).

I8Why does a CMUT need a DC bias, and what happens at pull-in? · onsite

Electrostatic force ∝ \(V^2\): without bias an AC drive produces force at 2f and the receive sensitivity \((\partial Q/\partial x \propto V)\) is zero. The bias linearizes transmit and sets the receive gain. The bias also softens the spring: \(k_{\mathrm{eff}} = k(1 - 2x/(g_0 - x))\), zero at \(x = g_0 /3\), the pull-in voltage \(\sqrt{8kg_0^3 /(27\varepsilon_0 A)}\). Approaching it, \(k_T^2 \to 1\) and the resonance drops (0.75 \(f_0\) at 0.9 \(V_{\mathrm{PI}}\)). Beyond it the membrane collapses onto the insulator; collapse-mode operation gives higher coupling and pressure with hysteresis. Follow-up: "reliability issues?" Dielectric charging shifts the effective bias, high DC voltage in the patient-applied part, and nonlinearity at large AC drive.

I9Estimate the receive loss of connecting one 2D element directly to a 2 m cable. · screen

0.2 × 0.2 mm, 0.3 mm thick, \(\varepsilon_r^S \approx 1500\): \(C_e = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \cdot 1500\) \(\cdot\, 4 \times 10^{-8}/3 \times 10^{-4} \approx 1.8\) pF. Cable ≈ 75–100 pF/m × 2 m ≈ 150–200 pF. Divider \(C_e /(C_e + C_c) \approx 0.009\text{–}0.012\), −39 to −41 dB before the first amplifier. A 1D element of 60 pF loses ≈ 11–13 dB. Follow-up: "and with a preamp in the handle?" The element then sees only a few pF of trace and input capacitance, the loss drops to a few dB, and the cable is driven from a low impedance.

I10You want 3D ULM in a rat brain with one 256-channel system. Which array architecture, and what do you give up? · research

Options: (a) RCA 128 + 128 with orthogonal plane waves: full footprint and kHz volume rates, but one-way focusing per dimension, cross-shaped PSF and edge ghosts; ULM localizes microbubbles to a fraction of the PSF, so a separable but larger PSF mainly costs SNR and increases overlap between bubbles, and ghosts must be suppressed (roll-off apodization, ghost-aware tracking). (b) 256-element sparse (spiral) array: true 2D focusing, low channel count, but low sensitivity \((K = 256)\) and a high clutter floor, which raises false detections. (c) Multiplexed 1024-element matrix: four firings per volume, motion between them. A strong answer quantifies: localization precision scales as PSF width / \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}}\) with SNR as a power ratio (Module 12), so compare width × \(\mathrm{SNR}^{-1/2}\) across options at the same volume rate. Follow-up: "how do you validate localization in 3D?" Microchannel or wire phantoms with known geometry, and Fourier ring/shell correlation on split datasets.

I11What is crosstalk in an array, how does it show up, and how is it measured? · onsite

Coupling of one element's drive into its neighbours: mechanical (kerf filler, backing, matching layers), guided waves in the lens and at the lens–tissue interface, and electrical (capacitive coupling in flex and cable). It widens the effective element, so the measured directivity is narrower than sinc predicts; guided modes add lobes or notches at specific angles and frequencies; it also adds a correlated component to neighbouring channels that biases coherence-based measures. Measurement: drive one element, record neighbours electrically in a water tank or measure surface velocity with a laser vibrometer; report nearest-neighbour levels in dB versus frequency. Follow-up: "design countermeasures?" Dice through the matching layers, soft kerf filler, sub-dicing, lens and matching-layer materials with high attenuation for the guided modes.

I12A square matrix at 0.6λ pitch must steer a 40° cone in every azimuth. Any grating lobes? Would a hexagonal grid help? · onsite

Replicas sit at \(\lambda/d = 1.667\) from the main lobe along the axes; the cone is lobe-free while \(1.667 \ge 1 + \sin\theta_{\max}\), i.e. up to 41.8°, so 40° passes with a thin margin (skirts of the nearest replica approach the edge at 40° along an axis). A triangular grid with the same element density has spacing \(0.6\sqrt{2/\sqrt{3}} = 0.645\lambda\) and six replicas at \(2\lambda/(\sqrt{3}\cdot 0.645\lambda) = 1.79\): lobe-free to 52.3°. Equivalently, for the 40° cone it allows 0.70\(\lambda\) spacing against 0.61\(\lambda\) for the square grid, 13 % fewer elements, or the same count over a larger aperture. Follow-up: "why are most matrix probes still rectangular?" Dicing, interconnect to a rectangular ASIC cell grid, and the fact that the micro-beamformer, not the element pitch, usually sets the usable steering per acquisition.

I13A 4D TEE probe with 3 × 3 micro-beamforming at λ/2 forms 16 parallel receive lines across ±20° around one pre-steer. What do you expect, and how do you fix it? · research

Lines at 20° from the pre-steer carry a subarray grating lobe at +0.4 dB relative to their own main lobe, which is itself 3.7 dB down; the −20 dB window is only ±3.0°, the −10 dB window ±8.5° (19.10). The outer lines show ghost copies of bright structures displaced by \(\lambda/D = 0.67\) in \(u\), partly suppressed by the transmit beam in two-way. Fixes: narrow the fan of lines per pre-steer (more transmit events, or one pre-steer zone per group of lines), reprogram fine delays per zone (14 zones cover ±45° per axis at −20 dB), use 2 × 2 subarrays if the cable budget allows (window ±3.6° at −20 dB), or broaden the transmit only as far as the window. Follow-up: "can retrospective beamforming of the subarray data fix it?" No: the lobe is baked into the analog sum; only the coarse lattice can be re-steered.

19.13Self-check

Q1A phased array at 3 MHz must steer ±60°. What is the largest pitch without grating lobes?

\(d \le \lambda /(1 + \sin 60^{\circ}) = 0.536\lambda\); \(\lambda = 0.513\) mm, so d ≤ 0.275 mm.

Q2An element of width \(\lambda\) (soft baffle) has its −6 dB point at 32.3°. What is \(F\#_{\min}\)?

1/(2 tan 32.3°) = 0.79.

Q3How many system channels does a 1.5D array with 128 azimuth elements and 5 rows need? A 1.75D?

1.5D pairs rows symmetrically: 3 independent row groups × 128 = 384. 1.75D: 5 × 128 = 640.

Q4A 48 × 48 matrix uses 4 × 4 micro-beamforming at \(\lambda /2\). How many channels, and where are the coarse grating lobes for a broadside beam?

2304/16 = 144 channels. Subarray pitch \(2\lambda\), so coarse lobes at \(u = \pm \lambda /(md) = \pm 0.5\) (±30°), and also ±1.0. They are cancelled by the subarray factor's nulls if the pre-steer is also broadside.

Q5Why does a row-column array have −13.3 dB sidelobes along x even though it transmits and receives?

Transmit (rows) is uniform along x, so T has no x dependence; only the receive columns focus in x. Along the x axis the two-way PSF is the one-way receive sinc.

Q6A CMUT is biased at 95 % of its pull-in voltage. What are \(x/g_0\), \(k_T^2\) and \(f/f_0\) in the parallel-plate model?

Solve \((27/4)\xi (1 - \xi)^{2} = 0.9025\): \(\xi = 0.219\), \(k_T^2 = 2\xi /(1 - \xi) = 0.56\), \(f/f_0 = \sqrt{(1 - 3\xi)/(1 - \xi)} = 0.66\).

Q7Doubling a CMUT membrane's radius at fixed thickness changes its vacuum resonance by what factor?

\(f \propto h/a^2\): one quarter (10 MHz → 2.5 MHz for 1 µm silicon from \(a = 20\) to 40 µm).

Q8A matrix has \(d_x = 0.5\lambda\), \(d_y = 0.8\lambda\) and steers 30° in the y–z plane (\(\varphi_0 = 90^{\circ}\)). Where is the grating lobe, and how strong is it with a soft-baffle element 0.9 pitch wide?

\(v_0 = 0.5\); the (0, −1) replica sits at \(v = 0.5 - 1/0.8 = -0.75\), inside the circle (−48.6°). \(\lvert\mathrm{AF}\rvert = 1\) there; the element factor ratio \(E(0, -0.75)/E(0, 0.5)\) gives −5.1 dB. Steering the same 30° along x is lobe-free because \(d_x = \lambda/2\). Equation (19.7).

Q9A 64 × 64 matrix at λ/2 uses 4 × 4 micro-beamforming. How many cable conductors, and how far from the pre-steer can a receive line point with subarray lobes below −20 dB?

\(N^2/m^2 = 4096/16 = 256\). From (19.10) with \(\rho_{\max} = 0.1\): \(x = 0.1\sin 45^{\circ}/(1 + 0.1\cos 45^{\circ}) = 0.066\) and \(\Delta u_{\max} = (2/\pi)\arctan x = 0.042\), ±2.4° around broadside, independent of N.
