Part IV · Module 24

Fourier-domain beamforming: f-k migration and k-space imaging

Module 23 read every transmit scheme as a way of filling k-space. This module turns that picture into an imaging algorithm: transform the channel data once, move every spectral sample to the object wavenumber it measures, transform back. Stolt's f-k migration and its plane-wave versions are the result: the physics of DAS at FFT cost, with every approximation visible as a mapping error you can plot and bound.

You will be able to

  • Read channel data in \((k_x, f)\): the propagating cone, the evanescent region, spatial aliasing for plane-wave and zero-offset data
  • Derive Stolt's map for zero-offset data from the exploding-reflector model, including the \(c/2\) and the Jacobian, and know its one homogeneous-medium error (interpolation)
  • Derive Garcia's plane-wave ERM fit \((\hat{v}, \beta, \gamma)\), bound its error, and write the exact transmit-plus-receive wavevector map
  • Predict the k-space support and PSF of a plane-wave set and compound in k-space
  • Defend f-k against DAS on accuracy (F#, aliasing, interpolation, gain), cost and the medium model, and extend it to layers by phase shift

24.1The angular spectrum: channel data as a sum of plane waves

In a homogeneous medium every solution of the wave equation is a superposition of plane waves \(e^{\,j2\pi(k_x x + k_z z + f t)}\) (sign of \(f t\) chosen to match the transform in (24.1), so positive f is the analytic band). Wavenumbers are in cycles/mm and frequencies in MHz throughout, with \(c = 1.54\) mm/µs. The wave equation ties the three together, \(k_x^2 + k_z^2 = (f/c)^2\), so the array, which measures only x and t, determines \(k_z\) up to a sign:

\[ \begin{aligned} D(k_x, f) &= \iint d(x, t)\, e^{-j2\pi(k_x x + f t)}\, dx\, dt \\ k_z &= \pm\sqrt{(f/c)^{2} - k_x^{2}} \\ k_x &= (f/c)\sin\alpha \end{aligned} \]

\(k_z\) is real for \(\lvert k_x\rvert \le \lvert f\rvert/c\) (propagating) and imaginary otherwise (evanescent). A sample at \((k_x, f)\) is a plane wave arriving from angle α; the cone edge \(\lvert k_x\rvert = f/c\) is a grazing arrival. At 5 MHz, \(f/c = 3.25\) cycles/mm.

Where the data live. In plane-wave imaging the receive leg is a one-way wave from each scatterer to the array, so the data spectrum fills the cone \(\lvert k_x\rvert \le f/c\). Back-propagating evanescent components would multiply them by \(e^{+2\pi\lvert k_z\rvert\Delta z}\): 81 dB per millimetre at \(1.1\,f/c\), applied to noise. Every Fourier imaging method masks them.

Spatial sampling. Elements at pitch p sample x, so \(k_x\) is periodic with period \(1/p\) and the Nyquist limit is \(1/(2p)\). The plane-wave cone stays inside it at every angle only if \(p \le \lambda_{\min}/2\). A 0.30 mm pitch is about λ at 5 MHz: the cone meets Nyquist at \(f = c/(2p) = 2.57\) MHz, and above it every arrival steeper than \(\alpha_{\mathrm{alias}} = \arcsin\!\big(c/(2pf)\big)\) folds (30.9° at 5 MHz, 20.0° at 7.5 MHz). For a 0° plane wave the share of channel energy beyond \(1/(2 \times 0.30\ \mathrm{mm})\) is 82 % for a target at 5 mm, 65 % at 10 mm, 34 % at 20 mm and 10 % at 30 mm.

Pitfall

The receive cone is not the image's k-space. The image spectrum of a plane-wave acquisition is the receive wavevector plus the transmit wavevector (Section 24.4), which doubles the axial wavenumber and shifts the lateral one by \((f/c)\sin\theta\). Reading resolution off the raw data spectrum underestimates the axial extent by a factor of two.

This is the one-way arrival time of a source at the reflector that fires at t = 0 into a medium of speed \(v = c/2\). Every reflector "explodes" at t = 0, the recorded data are a one-way wave field, and imaging means propagating it back to t = 0 (Loewenthal et al., 1976; Claerbout, 1985).

The weight is the Jacobian: the cosine of the propagation angle of the plane wave. Since \(f \ge v\lvert k_x\rvert\) on the output grid, the evanescent region is never read. Only \(f > 0\) is used, so the image is analytic and \(\lvert r\rvert\) is the envelope.
  1. FFT in t and in x, zero-padded (Section 24.5 says how much).
  2. For each \(k_x\) column and each output \(k_z\), compute \(f = v\sqrt{k_x^2 + k_z^2}\) and interpolate \(D(k_x, \cdot)\) there: one 1D interpolation per column.
  3. Multiply by the Jacobian.
  4. Inverse 2D FFT.

What it does to a sample. A sample at \((k_x, f)\) moves to \(k_z = \sqrt{(f/v)^2 - k_x^2}\): straight down in \(k_z\), along its \(k_x\) column (Figure 24.A). A flat reflector (\(k_x = 0\)) is only relabelled, \(k_z = 2f/c\), which is time-to-depth conversion. Steep components (large \(k_x\)) move most; this is the dip correction that collapses hyperbolas.

Interpolation is the only error in a homogeneous medium. Linear interpolation of \(e^{-j2\pi f t}\) between bins Δf apart at fractional position w is off by \(w(1 - w)\varphi^2/2\) with \(\varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta f\, t\). The mean part is a smooth gain loss (\(\varphi^2/12\)); the part that varies from one \(k_x\) column to the next (rms \(0.037\,\varphi^2\), verified by Monte Carlo) spreads each echo across x at its own depth. Two cures, both cheap: zero-pad the record before the FFT (smaller Δf) and centre the time origin (multiply by \(e^{\,j2\pi f t_c}\) before, \(e^{-j2\pi F t_c}\) after, so t becomes \(t - t_c\)).

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{smear} \approx 20\log_{10}\!\left(0.037\,\varphi^{2}\right)\ \mathrm{dB\ per\ echo} \\ \varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta f\,\lvert t - t_c\rvert \\ -30\ \mathrm{dB\ needs}\ \varphi \le 0.92 \end{gathered} \]

Velocity and sampling. Migrating zero-offset data with \(v = c\) instead of \(c/2\) halves every \(k_z\): a target at 10 mm reappears at 19.94 mm, 21 to 24 dB below its focused peak (m24_fk_migration_zero_offset.m). Intuition

DAS sums along a hyperbola in (x, t) for every pixel. Stolt does the same bookkeeping once for all pixels: in the Fourier domain a hyperbola family becomes a family of curves \(f(k_x, k_z)\), and summing along all of them at once is a resampling followed by an inverse FFT.

24.5f-k against DAS: same physics, different bookkeeping

Why they agree. Under the same model (Born, homogeneous, point elements) DAS and f-k both apply the adjoint of the forward operator: DAS sums the data along each pixel's delay curve; f-k evaluates the same integral in the Fourier domain. 24.10Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why does Stolt migration of pulse-echo data use \(c/2\)? · screen

Zero-offset delays are \(2r/c\), identical to one-way delays \(r/v\) with \(v = c/2\) from sources placed at the reflectors and fired at t = 0 (exploding reflector, (24.4)). The data are then a one-way field, and migration back-propagates it to t = 0 with speed \(c/2\). Using c doubles depths (10 → 19.94 mm in the lab) and defocuses by about 21 dB. Follow-up: "And for plane waves?" There is no exact equivalent speed; Garcia's fit gives \(\hat{v} = c/\sqrt{1 + \cos\theta + \sin^2\theta}\), \(c/\sqrt{2}\) at 0°.

I2What is the evanescent region of a \((k_x, f)\) spectrum, and why is it empty in channel data? · screen

\(\lvert k_x\rvert > f/c\), where \(k_z\) is imaginary and the field decays as \(e^{-2\pi z\sqrt{k_x^2 - (f/c)^2}}\): 81 dB per mm at \(1.1\,f/c\) at 5 MHz. Scatterers are millimetres away, so nothing evanescent reaches the array; what shows up there is truncation leakage (−22 dB for a 5 mm target, rectangular aperture). Follow-up: "What if you back-propagate it?" The decay becomes a gain on noise; mask it.

I3Derive Stolt's mapping and its Jacobian. What happens if you drop the Jacobian? · onsite

\(r(x, z) = \iint D(k_x, f)\, e^{\,j2\pi(k_x x + k_z z)}\, df\, dk_x\) with \(k_z = \sqrt{(f/v)^2 - k_x^2}\). Substitute \(f = v\sqrt{k_x^2 + k_z^2}\), \(df = v k_z/\sqrt{k_x^2 + k_z^2}\, dk_z\); the image is an inverse 2D FFT of D resampled at that f times the Jacobian (24.6). Dropping it changes the k-space weighting (a \(\cos\varphi\) obliquity), hence slightly the PSF shape and amplitude, not the focusing and not the \(\sqrt{z}\) depth gain of unnormalized f-k. Follow-up: "Where does Stolt need interpolation and how fine?" Along f; keep \(2\pi\,\Delta f\,\lvert t - t_c\rvert \lesssim 1\).

I4Why can't zero-offset Stolt be applied to plane-wave data directly? Derive Garcia's ERM velocity. · onsite

The plane-wave delay \([x_0\sin\theta + z_0\cos\theta + \sqrt{(x - x_0)^2 + z_0^2}]/c\) is not a hyperbola and its transmit part depends on \(x_0\). Steering compensation (shift by \(-x\sin\theta/c\)) makes it depend on \(x - x_0\) only; then fit \(\sqrt{(u - \hat{u})^2 + \hat{z}^2}/\hat{v}\) in value, slope and curvature at u = 0. That gives \(R^2 = z_0^2(1 + \cos\theta)^2/(1 + \cos\theta + \sin^2\theta)\), \(\hat{v} = c/\sqrt{1 + \cos\theta + \sin^2\theta}\), \(\beta = (1 + \cos\theta)^{3/2}/(1 + \cos\theta + \sin^2\theta)\), \(\gamma = \sin\theta/(2 - \cos\theta)\) (24.10)–(24.11). Follow-up: "How big is the error?" \(z\tan^4\alpha/16\) at 0°: F# ≳ 1.1 at 30 mm, 5 MHz.

I5How do you implement an F-number and apodization in f-k? · onsite

Receive angle α corresponds to data wavenumber \(k_x = (f/c)\sin\alpha\), so the F# is a fan filter \(\lvert k_x\rvert \le (f/c)\sin(\arctan(1/(2F\#)))\) applied before steering compensation and mapping, and apodization is a window across the fan. It equals a constant-F# dynamic aperture. Follow-up: "Is it identical to DAS?" Not at the array edges (DAS's aperture saturates) and not for aliased data, which can fold into the fan.

I6Your f-k images show a lateral haze at the depth of every bright reflector, absent in DAS. Diagnose. · onsite

Interpolation along f is too coarse: the error \(w(1 - w)\varphi^2/2\) with \(\varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta f\, t\) varies from column to column and spreads each echo across x at its depth (rms \(0.037\,\varphi^2\); −10 dB at φ = 3). Fix: zero-pad the time FFT (2–4× the record) and centre the time origin. In the lab, doubling the FFT length took beside-block clutter from −10.6 to −18.2 dB (DAS −18.7). Second suspect: lateral wrap or aliasing; check padding in x and the fan. Follow-up: "Why only at the depth of the echo?" The error multiplies that echo's own spectrum.

I7Compare the cost of f-k and DAS. When is f-k not worth it? · onsite

DAS: \(N_{\mathrm{pix}} \times N_{\mathrm{active}}\) interpolations per angle (3.4 M in the lab). f-k: FFTs of the padded data (N log N), one interpolation per output sample, one IFFT; compounding adds spectra and needs one IFFT per frame. Measured 2.1–2.5× in Octave; the asymptotic gain is about \(N_{\mathrm{active}}/\log_2 N\). f-k loses for small ROIs or a few lines, arbitrary pixel grids, heterogeneous media, and when a GPU DAS is memory-bound anyway. Follow-up: "And for 3D with a matrix array?" The element factor grows to thousands, which is where Fourier methods pay most.

I8The exact plane-wave map is closed form. Why would anyone use the ERM fit? · research

Both are one 1D interpolation per column after the same steering compensation, so cost is not the reason. The ERM keeps the Stolt square-root structure, shares code with zero-offset migration, and its error is negligible at moderate F# and steering. The exact map (24.15) removes the fit error (35 µm lateral offset at 9° in the lab, none for the exact map). A defensible choice today: the exact map, unless the code base is built around ERM. Follow-up: "What does the exact map still assume?" Born, homogeneous c, an infinite plane wave and point elements.

I9How would you extend f-k imaging to a subcutaneous fat layer, and then to lateral heterogeneity? · research

Horizontal layers: phase-shift migration, stepping Ψ by \(e^{\,j2\pi(k_z(z) + f/c(z))\Delta z}\) (24.17); \(k_x\) is conserved by Snell's law, so it is exact. An 8 mm layer at 1450 m/s shifts targets by 0.497 mm under single-speed Stolt; phase shift removes it. Lateral variation: split-step Fourier (a phase screen \(e^{\,j2\pi f\Delta s(x)\Delta z}\) per slab, then a homogeneous step) or phase shift plus interpolation between reference speeds; beyond that, time-domain delays from an eikonal solver. Follow-up: "Where does the speed map come from?" From a focus or coherence sweep (cheap in f-k), or tomography (Module 13).

I10How can Fourier-domain imaging help sound-speed estimation and aberration correction research? · research

At broadside the map is \(f = c\,g(\mathbf{K})\), so each trial c reuses the data FFTs: a dense global-c sweep costs one interpolation and one IFFT per value. For local aberrations, keep the full reflection matrix (all transmit angles and receive elements) instead of the confocal sum: propagating both sides to a depth in the angular-spectrum domain gives focused-basis matrices whose off-confocal structure reveals the aberration (distortion-matrix approach). Follow-up: "And for focused-beam data?" REFoCUS recovers the synthetic-aperture data set first.

I11A 128-element probe at 0.30 mm pitch runs at 7.5 MHz. Will f-k images alias, and what do you do? · screen

Nyquist is \(1/(2 \times 0.30) = 1.67\) /mm; the receive cone at 7.5 MHz reaches 4.87 /mm, so arrivals steeper than \(\arcsin(c/(2pf)) = 20.0°\) fold. With F# 1.5 the fan needs \(0.316 \times 4.87 = 1.54\) /mm, still inside Nyquist, but steep energy folded from beyond can land in the fan. Use the fan filter, let element directivity help, and limit the angles that matter, or accept DAS where the aperture window keeps steep elements out. Follow-up: "Zero-offset at the same pitch?" Cone \(2f/c\), hopeless without a small aperture.

24.12Self-check

Q1At 7.5 MHz, where is the cone edge in \(k_x\), and which arrivals alias with a 0.30 mm pitch?

\(f/c = 7.5/1.54 = 4.87\) cycles/mm. Nyquist is \(1/(2 \times 0.30) = 1.67\) /mm, so arrivals with \(\sin\alpha > 1.67/4.87 = 0.342\), i.e. α > 20.0°, alias.

Q2Zero-offset data of a point at 20 mm are migrated with \(v = c\). Where does it appear, and is it focused?

At 40 mm: \(k_z \approx f/c\) instead of \(2f/c\) doubles depth. It is not focused, because the hyperbola curvature corresponds to the wrong speed; the lab shows 21–24 dB peak loss for the same error at 10 mm.

Q3Compute Garcia's \(\hat{v}/c\), β and γ at θ = 15°.

\(\cos 15° = 0.9659\), \(\sin^2 15° = 0.0670\), so \(1 + \cos\theta + \sin^2\theta = 2.0329\). \(\hat{v}/c = 1/\sqrt{2.0329} = 0.7014\); \(\beta = 1.9659^{1.5}/2.0329\) = \(2.7564/2.0329 = 1.356\); \(\gamma = 0.2588/(2 - 0.9659) = 0.250\).

Q4A 60 µs record is migrated with linear interpolation. What bin spacing Δf keeps the smear at −30 dB, with and without time-centring? FFT length at 20 MHz?

Need \(\varphi = 2\pi\,\Delta f\,\lvert t - t_c\rvert \le 0.92\), i.e. \(\Delta f\,\lvert t - t_c\rvert \le 0.146\). Uncentred, \(\lvert t\rvert \le 60\) µs: \(\Delta f \le 2.44\) kHz, 8192 points. Centred, \(\lvert t - t_c\rvert \le 30\) µs: \(\Delta f \le 4.88\) kHz, 4096 points.

Q5Estimate the narrowband lateral −6 dB width at 5 MHz for a continuous plane-wave span of ±10° with receive F# 1.5.

Lateral k-space is the convolution of the transmit span (half-width \((f/c)\sin 10° = 0.564\) /mm) and the receive fan (\((f/c)\sin\alpha = 1.027\) /mm), so the PSF is \(\mathrm{sinc}(2 \times 0.564\, x)\cdot\mathrm{sinc}(2 \times 1.027\, x)\). Its −6 dB width is 0.53 mm, against 0.59 mm receive-only. A discrete 7-angle set at ±9° simulated 0.505 mm: a discrete transmit factor has a slightly narrower central lobe.

Q6A 6 mm fat layer (1450 m/s) lies over tissue (1540 m/s). How far off is a single-speed (1540 m/s) Stolt image below it?

\(\Delta z = (c_2/c_1 - 1)\,d_1\) = \((1.0621 - 1) \times 6 = 0.37\) mm too deep, the same for every target below the layer. Phase-shift migration with the layer speed removes it.

Q7Why does compounding N angles in f-k need only one inverse 2D FFT, and what does it not fix?

The inverse FFT is linear: \(\sum_\theta \mathrm{IFFT}(R_\theta) = \mathrm{IFFT}(\sum_\theta R_\theta)\), so remapped spectra are summed on the common grid first. Each angle still needs its own forward FFTs, steering compensation and interpolation, and motion between angles still adds phase errors.
