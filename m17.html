In 60 seconds
  1. The specification sets the pitch: how far the beam must steer fixes element spacing, because too coarse a pitch creates grating lobes (false beams). The pitch p must satisfy \(p\le\lambda/(1+\lvert\sin\theta_0\rvert)\), with λ the wavelength and \(\theta_0\) the steering angle, so a phased array needs \(p\le\lambda/2\) while a barely steering linear array lives with \(p\approx\lambda\). §17.1
  2. Matching layers fix a mirror: ceramic (34.2 MRayl) and tissue (1.5 MRayl) differ so much in acoustic impedance that only 0.161 of the power crosses the face per pass and the plate rings. A quarter-wavelength layer with impedance \(\sqrt{Z_pZ_L}\), the geometric mean of piezo and load impedances, makes its two face echoes cancel at the design frequency. §17.2 §17.3
  3. Take bandwidth from the layers, not the backing: backing damps the plate by absorbing energy that would have been signal; matching layers widen the band by passing it forward. In the two-layer 5 MHz reference stack, 34 MRayl backing instead of air widened the band from 63 to 83 % but raised two-way loss from 2.0 to 17.5 dB. §17.4 §17.6
  4. The lens focuses, attenuates and filters: a silicone lens focuses in elevation because sound is slower in silicone than in tissue. Its focal length \(F=R/(c_t/c_l-1)\), with R the lens radius and \(c_t,c_l\) the tissue and lens speeds, is 1.85 R for 1000 m/s silicone in 1540 m/s tissue. Its thick centre attenuates most, more so at high frequency. §17.7
  5. Real elements are bars, not plates: when an element's width approaches its thickness, a sideways vibration mode crosses the thickness mode and splits the spectrum. This happens at width-to-thickness ratios of about 0.8–2, so elements stay below about 0.6 or are sub-diced (cut into narrower bars). One-dimensional models miss it; finite-element models catch it. §17.8 §17.9
  6. Design for the spread, then write the test: tolerances belong in the design. Glue bond lines can only add thickness, so they shift the mean centre frequency down rather than just spreading it. Each dead element adds sidelobe background of only about 1/N (N active elements), so allowing one isolated failure buys much yield. §17.10 §17.12
  7. Heat and electronics decide what survives: drive power not leaving as sound heats the probe head, so heat is routed backward. Drive duty (active elements × cycles × pulse rate ÷ centre frequency) rises about sevenfold from B-mode to pulsed Doppler. A matrix probe puts electronics under the stack: a 1 pF element cannot drive a cable. §17.11 §17.13 §17.15
From the field
  • Model real bond lines in the nominal design, then set test windows from the Monte Carlo spread. A glue film can only add thickness, so it shifts the mean, not just the spread: in the running case 3 ± 1.5 µm bond lines pull the built mean centre frequency 1.7 % below the perfect-stack design. §17.10
  • Read a low centre frequency as layer thickness or speed before blaming the ceramic. A matching layer 10 % too thick moves its transmission peak to 0.91 of the design frequency but costs only 0.5 dB there, so thickness errors show as centre-frequency spread, not lost sensitivity. Subtract the lens downshift first. §17.5
  • Verify lens focus at body temperature in a tissue-speed medium, not only in water. Focal length is the lens radius divided by a small number, tissue-to-lens speed ratio minus one (0.54 for silicone in tissue), so a lens focusing at 40 mm in 1540 m/s tissue focuses at 48 mm in 1450 m/s fat-like tissue. §17.7
  • Keep element width-to-thickness below about 0.6, or sub-dice. Width modes cross the thickness mode at ratios of about 0.8–2 and steal its energy, and 1D models such as KLM cannot show them. A notch or double peak in a measured element spectrum points at a lateral mode first and a bond line second. §17.8
  • Accept one isolated dead element, never two adjacent. Adjacent failures form coherent sidelobes, while isolated ones add little background and each allowed failure raises yield steeply: in the running case (0.2 % dead, 1 dB spread, ±3 dB window) yield is 77 % with one allowed failure and 41 % with none. §17.12
  • Size the heat path for pulsed Doppler in still air, not B-mode on tissue. Drive duty rises about sevenfold from B-mode to pulsed-wave (PW) Doppler, and still air removes heat only by natural convection: in the running-case model the face warms 1.2 °C in B-mode but 5.2 °C in PW Doppler. §17.13

17.1From specification to stack

Roadmap step 1 of 11 · specification

A probe specification starts with the clinical task, not with the ceramic. Depth and resolution set the centre frequency \(f_0\): tissue attenuation of roughly 0.5 dB/cm/MHz one way limits penetration, so abdominal probes sit near 2–5 MHz, vascular and general linear probes near 5–10 MHz and small-parts probes above that. The imaging modes set the fractional bandwidth (FBW, the −6 dB band divided by its centre): harmonic imaging needs the receive band to reach \(2f_0\), spectral QUS (quantitative ultrasound) wants a wide, smooth band, and Doppler wants sensitivity near \(f_0\) more than width. Sensitivity follows from the penetration budget of Module 20. Once these three are fixed, almost every dimension of the stack follows from formulas you already have, which is why a one-page worksheet can carry a design from requirement to first stack.

The array geometry comes next. Grating lobes appear wherever the element phases repeat, at

\[ \begin{gathered} \sin\theta_g = \sin\theta_0 - m\,\frac{\lambda}{p}, \quad m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots \\ \text{no grating lobe in view if } p \le \frac{\lambda}{1 + \lvert\sin\theta_0\rvert} \end{gathered} \]
(17.1)

What changes for a phased array

Pitch drops to λ/2 (154 µm at 5 MHz), so with a 25 µm kerf the element is 129 µm wide and w/t stays near 0.3 without sub-dicing. The penalty is electrical: the element area halves, so \(C_0\) halves and \(\lvert Z_C\rvert\) doubles, and the cable divides the signal more. Phased arrays are often built on 1-3 composite (lower impedance, higher \(k_t\)) and use larger elevation apertures for deep cardiac imaging.

What changes for a matrix probe

Pitch is λ/2 in both directions, so the element is a square of about 0.13 × 0.13 mm at 5 MHz and \(C_0\) is about a picofarad (§17.15). No cable can carry thousands of such elements, so the specification now includes an in-probe ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit), its power budget and the channel count to the system.

17.2The mismatch: why a bare plate rings

Roadmap step 3 of 11 · matching stack: the mismatch

Acoustic impedance \(Z = \rho c\) (in MRayl = \(10^{6}\) kg m−2 s−1) sets how a plane wave divides at an interface. For a wave going from medium 1 into medium 2 at normal incidence, with pressure amplitudes:

\[ \begin{gathered} R = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}, \qquad T_p = \frac{2 Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} \\ T_I = 1 - R^2 = \frac{4 Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2} \end{gathered} \]
(17.3)
\(Z_1,\ Z_2\)
acoustic impedances of the incident and transmitting media, MRayl
\(R\)
pressure reflection coefficient
\(T_p\)
pressure transmission coefficient
\(T_I\)
intensity (power) transmission coefficient
\(T_I\) is the intensity (power) transmission. \(T_p\) can exceed 1 going into a stiffer medium; energy is still conserved because intensity is \(p^2 /Z\).

PZT (≈ 30–36 MRayl; this module uses 34.2 MRayl, \(\rho = 7500\) kg/m³, \(v = 4560\) m/s) into tissue (1.5–1.7 MRayl; here 1.5) gives \(R = -0.916\) and \(T_I = 0.161\): −7.9 dB of power one way, with 84 % of each wave reflected back into the plate. The reflected energy bounces between the faces and leaks out only a little per round trip (one period at the half-wave resonance), which is ring-down. For a half-wave plate between a backing \(Z_B\) and a load \(Z_L\), the amplitude ratio per round trip is \(r = R_{\mathrm{front}} R_{\mathrm{back}}\), so

\[ Q = \frac{\pi}{\ln(1/\lvert r\rvert)} \approx \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{Z_p}{Z_B + Z_L} \qquad (Z_B,\ Z_L \ll Z_p) \]
(17.4)
\(r\)

\(Z_p\)

\(Z_B,\ Z_L\)

\(Q\)

\(\ln\)

Amplitude falls as \(e^{-\pi n/Q}\) after n periods. Air backing: \(Q \approx 36 (r = 0.916\), −0.76 dB per cycle, 26 cycles to −20 dB). Backing 5 MRayl: \(Q \approx 8.2 (r = 0.682\), −3.3 dB per cycle, 6 cycles to −20 dB). Mechanical (acoustic) Q only; the electrical port adds its own filtering. Demo 17.4 (§17.6) reads out r, the decay per cycle and both forms of Q for any backing.
Intuition

Think of the plate as a cavity with two leaky mirrors. Matching layers make the front mirror transparent, backing makes the back mirror absorbing. Both shorten the pulse; only the first sends the energy where you want it.

Running case

Without matching layers the running-case plate on a 5 MRayl backing would ring with \(Q \approx 8.3\) by (17.4): about −3.3 dB per cycle, six cycles to −20 dB, a pulse far too long for a 5 MHz imaging probe. The bare face would also pass only 16 % of the power into tissue. Both numbers point to matching layers rather than a heavier backing, which §17.6 confirms.

Key takeaways
  • TI = 4Z1Z2/(Z1 + Z2)²: PZT (34.2 MRayl) into tissue (1.5 MRayl) gives R = −0.916 and TI = 0.161, −7.9 dB one way.
  • The reflected energy leaks out a little per round trip (one period at the half-wave resonance): ring-down with Q ≈ (π/2)Zp/(ZB + ZL) for ZB, ZL ≪ Zp.
  • Matching layers make the front mirror transparent and send the energy forward; backing makes the back mirror absorbing and loses it. Both shorten the pulse.

(17.5)

\[ \begin{gathered} \Gamma = \frac{r_1 + r_2\, e^{-j2\theta}}{1 + r_1 r_2\, e^{-j2\theta}} \\ r_1 = \frac{Z_1 - Z_p}{Z_1 + Z_p}, \qquad r_2 = \frac{Z_L - Z_1}{Z_L + Z_1} \end{gathered} \]

Pitfall

"Quarter wave" means a quarter of the wavelength in the layer, \(\lambda_1 = c_1 /f_0\), not in the plate or in tissue. A 2500 m/s epoxy layer at 5 MHz is 125 µm thick; at 15 MHz it is 41.7 µm, and a ±5 µm lapping tolerance becomes ±12 % of the thickness.

Running case

At 5 MHz a 2500 m/s layer is \(\lambda_1/4\) = 125 µm thick. The lapping target of ±4 µm (§17.11) is ±3.2 % of that thickness, well inside the ±10 % that would move a single layer's peak to 0.91 \(f_0\).

Whether a design survives production depends less on the rule than on what the line can hold. Thickness sets frequency: a layer 10 % too thick moves its transmission peak to 0.91 \(f_0\) but costs only 0.5 dB at \(f_0\), so thickness errors show up as centre-frequency spread, not as lost sensitivity. 17.6Backing: damping against sensitivity

Roadmap step 4 of 11 · backing

The backing absorbs the energy the plate radiates rearward. A backing equal to \(Z_p\) removes the back mirror entirely: one-pass emission, very broadband, but at least half of the energy goes into the backing each way (half when the layers present \(Z_p\) to the plate, about 96 % for a bare face into tissue). Air backing sends everything forward but rings. The KLM model quantifies the trade:

\[ \begin{gathered} Q_m \approx \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{Z_p}{Z_B + Z_{L,\mathrm{eff}}} \\ \text{fraction of plate energy into the backing} \approx \frac{Z_B}{Z_B + Z_{L,\mathrm{eff}}} \end{gathered} \]
(17.11)
\(Z_{L,\mathrm{eff}}\)

\(Q_m\)

\(Z_p,\ Z_B\)

\(Z_{\mathrm{L,eff}}\) is the load the plate sees through the matching layers (≈ \(Z_p\) at \(f_0\) for an ideal √ layer, lower off resonance). Order-of-magnitude reasoning only; the curves below come from the full chain.

For two Desilets layers, raising the backing from air to 34 MRayl widens the −6 dB band from 63 % to 83 % and raises the two-way loss from 2.0 dB to 17.5 dB. Without matching layers, the same sweep runs from a 16 % band at −3.6 dB (on air backing the 50 Ω electrical port, not the backing, does the damping) to 127 % at −31.2 dB: backing alone buys bandwidth only by spending sensitivity. Production arrays typically sit at moderate backing impedances (a few MRayl) and get most of their bandwidth from the matching layers.

The backing must also absorb what it takes. Energy that reflects from its rear face returns to the plate as a late echo at \(2d_b /c_b\), and the round-trip attenuation \(2\alpha_b d_b\) plus the transmission losses must push that echo below the displayed dynamic range. Heavily filled epoxies (tungsten in epoxy, for example) give both a tunable Z and high scattering loss; some designs also shape or angle the rear face so the reflection does not return coherently.

17.7The acoustic lens: elevation focus, loss and downshift

Roadmap step 5–6 of 11 · lens and elevation

A 1D array focuses electronically in azimuth but needs a fixed focus in elevation, usually a cylindrical lens of RTV silicone, slower than tissue (\(c_l \approx 950\text{–}1050\) m/s against \(c_t \approx 1540\) m/s). A slower material delays the wave, so the lens must be thickest at the centre, that is, convex. Paraxially, a lens of thickness \(T(y) = T_c - y^2 /(2R)\) adds a delay \(T(y)(1/c_l - 1/c_t)\); focusing at F requires a delay that falls as \(y^2 /(2Fc_t)\) away from the centre. Equating the \(y^2\) terms:

\[ F = \frac{R}{c_t/c_l - 1} = 1.85\,R \quad \text{for } c_l = 1000,\ c_t = 1540\ \mathrm{m/s} \]
(17.12)
\(F\)
elevation focal length from the lens apex, mm
\(R\)
radius of curvature of the lens, mm
\(c_l,\ c_t\)

The optical lensmaker's \(1/f = (n - 1)/R\) with \(n = c_t /c_l\). F is measured from the lens apex into tissue. Because the speed contrast is large, F depends strongly on \(c_t\): the same lens focuses at 48.0 mm in 1450 m/s fat-like tissue instead of 40.0 mm.

Example: \(F = 40\) mm needs \(R = 21.6\) mm. Over a 6 mm elevation aperture the sag is 0.209 mm; with a 0.3 mm edge the centre is 0.509 mm thick. The exact Snell ray trace focuses the marginal ray 0.92 mm short of the paraxial focus (spherical aberration, 2.3 % of F), small against the elevation depth of field at F# 6.7.

Lens attenuation (≈ 2–4.5 dB/mm at 5 MHz for filled silicones) acts in three ways, and each must be booked before a measured probe is compared with the stack model:

  • Loss: two-way through the centre at 5 MHz with 3 dB/mm, 2 × 3 × 0.509 = 3.1 dB.
  • Inverse apodization: the centre is thickest, so it is attenuated most (two-way 3.1 dB at the centre, 1.8 dB at the edge). The aperture is weighted towards its edges, which narrows the main lobe slightly and raises sidelobes (−26.5 dB → −25.7 dB two-way in this example).
  • Spectral downshift: attenuation that rises with frequency multiplies the pulse-echo spectrum by a low-pass factor. For a Gaussian spectrum of standard deviation \(\sigma\) and a linear law (\(\beta\) Np/MHz two-way), the centre moves by \(-\beta \sigma^2\). With \(\alpha \propto f^{1.5}\) and the aperture-mean thickness (0.439 mm), a 70 % band at 5 MHz shifts its centroid to 4.81 MHz.

The lens impedance should match tissue (filled silicones reach 1.4–1.6 MRayl; unfilled ones are nearer 1.0–1.1 MRayl, pressure reflection coefficient \(\approx -0.2\) against tissue, a −14 dB reverberation source between lens face and matching stack).

17.8Kerf, sub-dicing and lateral modes

Roadmap step 7 of 11 · kerf and dicing

An array element is not a plate but a bar of width w (azimuth), thickness t and length equal to the elevation height, cut free from its neighbours by kerfs. When w is comparable with t, thickness motion couples through Poisson's ratio into width motion, and the bar has resonances a 1D model cannot know about.

  • w/t ≪ 1: the bar is a slender column. Its thickness resonance drops from the plate value v/(2t) to the bar value; in plane strain \(f\,t/c_L = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 - 2\nu }/(1 - \nu) = 0.441\) for \(\nu = 0.32\). For real piezoceramics the bar mode also has a higher coupling (\(k_{33}^{\prime}\) above \(k_t\)), one reason narrow elements are used.
  • \(w/t \approx 0.8\text{–}2\): the first width mode, near f \(t/c_L \approx 0.441/(w/t)\), crosses the thickness mode. The modes repel and share energy: the spectrum splits into two or three peaks inside the band.
  • w/t ≫ 1: the thickness mode returns to 0.5 with a comb of weak width harmonics beside it.

The common design guideline keeps w/t below about 0.6 (the exact value depends on the material and the analysis). An element that must be wider is sub-diced: cut into two or more sub-elements that are wired together, so each piece is slender. Composites carry the same logic to pillars, whose aspect ratio and lateral period must keep the lateral (stop-band) resonances of the periodic structure well above the thickness mode. Kerf filler and the shared backing and matching layers couple neighbours mechanically; designs commonly target adjacent-element crosstalk around −30 dB or lower.

A 1D model (KLM, Mason, ABCD) is exact for an infinite plate with plane layers and fails as soon as the structure has edges: lateral modes and kerfs (Section 17.8), crosstalk through filler, backing and lens, element directivity and baffle effects, curved lenses, non-uniform poling, and composites whose effective properties depend on pillar geometry. These need a 2D or 3D model of the actual structure. Explicit time-domain codes suit broadband transients in large structures; implicit frequency-domain codes give exact eigenmodes and admittance at chosen frequencies.

That FDTD has the core ingredients of an explicit time-domain code (staggered grid, CFL-limited step, free boundaries) but no piezoelectric coupling, anisotropy, electrodes, kerf filler, backing, lens or absorbing boundaries, which are exactly what a commercial FE model adds.

Mesh density is the first decision in any such model. Second-order schemes (central-difference FD, and lumped-mass linear FE on a uniform mesh, which reduce to the same stencil in 1D) under-estimate the phase velocity: −1.24 % at 10 points per wavelength and Courant number 0.5, −0.31 % at 20. That is 4.5° of phase per wavelength travelled at 10 points, which accumulates to more than a full cycle over 100 \(\lambda\). The mesh is set by the shortest wavelength anywhere in the model, which is usually a shear wave in the epoxies of the matching layers, backing and kerf filler, not the longitudinal wave in the ceramic (very soft silicone is usually modelled as a lossy, nearly fluid medium). An explicit scheme needs \(c_{\mathrm{max}} \Delta t/\Delta x \le 1/\sqrt{d}\) on this stencil (\(d = 2\) or 3 dimensions), so halving \(\Delta x\) doubles the number of steps: cost grows as (points per wavelength)\(^{d+1}\).

Boundaries, and the outputs to compare with measurement

Real models are truncated with absorbing boundaries (perfectly matched layers, Berenger 1994, or graded damping zones) or by extending a lossy backing until its echoes are below the noise; unchecked boundary reflections look exactly like ringing. The model outputs compared with measurements are electrical impedance magnitude and phase versus frequency (impedance analyzer), pulse-echo waveform and spectrum from a flat reflector (centre frequency, −6 dB bandwidth, loop sensitivity), surface displacement (laser vibrometry), crosstalk to neighbouring elements in dB versus frequency, and element directivity. Module 21 covers the measurements.

The workflow follows from these limits. Use KLM/ABCD to choose impedances, thicknesses and backing; use FE to check the real geometry (kerf, sub-dicing, lens, crosstalk) and to refine; build a prototype; measure impedance and pulse-echo; then fit the model's material constants (\(k_t\), \(\varepsilon^S\), losses, layer speeds) to the measurement and iterate. A model you have fitted to your own measured impedance is worth more than any datasheet.

Running case

The FE check (illustrative plan) models in 2D one element with its two sub-elements and two neighbours on each side, with kerf filler, layers, lens and a damped backing. Its mesh is set by the shortest wavelength at the upper band edge, about 9 MHz: a shear wave in an epoxy with a shear speed near 1000–1100 m/s (one measured epoxy: 1041 m/s, Franco et al. 2005) has λ ≈ 0.12 mm there, so 15–20 points per wavelength means cells of 6–8 µm, finer than the 20 µm sub-kerf by a factor of three. Key takeaways
  • 1D models (KLM, Mason, ABCD) are exact only for infinite plane layers; kerfs, crosstalk, curved lenses and composites need a 2D or 3D model.
  • Second-order schemes lose 1.24 % of phase speed at 10 points per wavelength (Courant 0.5) and 0.31 % at 20; explicit cost grows as ppwd+1.
  • Workflow: KLM/ABCD → FE → prototype → measure impedance and pulse-echo → fit kt, εS, losses and layer speeds, then iterate.

17.10Tolerance analysis: design for the spread, not the nominal

Roadmap step 10 of 11 · test and yield, planned at design time

Every number in §17.4 assumed a perfect build. A real line laps layers to a few micrometres, mixes filled epoxies whose impedance drifts with filler fraction, leaves glue films between layers and cuts kerfs with a blade that wears. Each variation is small, but centre frequency, bandwidth and sensitivity respond to all of them at once, and not always linearly. For small, independent variations the spread of an output \(y\) follows the familiar propagation of uncertainty:

\[ \sigma_y^2 \approx \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_i}\right)^{2} \sigma_{x_i}^2 \]
(17.13)
\(y\)
an output: \(f_c\) (MHz), FBW (%) or two-way loss (dB)
\(x_i\)
a build parameter: layer thickness, layer impedance, bond-line thickness, plate thickness, kerf
\(\sigma_{x_i},\ \sigma_y\)
standard deviations of the parameter and of the output
\(\partial y/\partial x_i\)
sensitivity of the output to the parameter, from the model
First-order (linearized) propagation for independent parameters, the GUM (Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement) law. It fails when an output is non-linear or one-sided in a parameter, as bond lines are: a glue film can only be thicker than zero, so it shifts the mean, not only the spread.

The sensitivities \(\partial y/\partial x_i\) come from the KLM + ABCD chain by finite differences; the demo plots them as a tornado chart. A Monte Carlo run goes one step further. It draws every parameter from its distribution, builds a few hundred virtual probes with the full model and reads the output distributions directly, so the non-linear and one-sided effects come out without approximation. Together the two views answer what a design review asks: which tolerance dominates, whether the mean build sits where the design was centred, and what fraction of builds would pass a given test window.

17.11Fabrication step by step, and the defect each step can cause

Roadmap steps 8–9 of 11 · interconnect and fabrication

An array is built as a stack of irreversible steps, and each step leaves a characteristic defect that a later test must catch. Knowing the map from step to defect is what lets an engineer read a failing element profile and say "that is a dicing short" or "that is a bond void" before anyone opens the probe. The sequence below is generic for a diced linear array on a PZT plate; composites, curved arrays and matrix probes change the order of some steps but not the logic.

  1. Lapping and electroding. The ceramic is lapped to the plate thickness from §17.1 and electroded by sputtering, electroless plating or fired silver. Thickness is frequency (\(t = v/2f_0\) for a plate, less for a slender bar), so a lapping error appears directly as a centre-frequency shift; weak electrode adhesion shows up later, when dicing and thermal cycling peel the electrode.
  2. Poling and depoling risk. The plate is poled by a strong field at elevated temperature, or bought poled. Every later step that heats the ceramic (adhesive cures, soldering, lens moulding) or applies a reverse field or high stress can partly depole it (DoITPoMS, University of Cambridge). Materials suppliers give a rule of thumb that keeps use and process temperatures near or below half the Curie temperature. Datasheet Curie temperatures depend on formulation: roughly 190–230 °C for soft PZT-5H types (Navy Type VI; one catalogue lists 230 °C) against about 350 °C for harder PZT-5A types (Navy Type II), typical datasheet values rather than a specification. Depoling shows as a lower effective coupling, read from the spacing of the resonance and antiresonance in the impedance (Module 16).
  3. Matching-layer bonding. Layers are cast in place and lapped, or bonded as sheets. The defect is the bond line of §17.5: a few micrometres cost little at 5 MHz, but a void or disbond under an element removes it from the image. An acoustic C-scan of the bonded plate catches large voids, per-element pulse-echo the rest.
  4. Flex and backing. The signal flex is bonded to the rear electrodes and the backing is cast or bonded behind it. A disbonded or cracked trace is an open element; flex that wrinkles adds a lossy layer behind the plate.
  5. Dicing, sub-dicing and kerf fill. A saw cuts through the layers, the electrodes, the ceramic and the flex and stops in the backing (Figure 17.D). Chipping and micro-cracks weaken elements, smeared electrode metal can short neighbours, and a kerf filler with voids changes the crosstalk from element to element. The running case needs 192 element cuts plus 192 sub-dicing cuts, each within a few micrometres of position.
  6. Interconnect. Each element needs a signal path and a common ground. In conventional arrays the signal flex fans out to the cable, and the front electrodes are tied together by a thin ground foil or a metallized inner matching layer. Joints are made by soldering, wire bonding or ACF (anisotropic conductive film, an adhesive with conductive particles that conducts only through its thickness under pressure); each joint is a place for an open or a high resistance. Soldering is also a heat exposure (step 2). In matrix probes the interconnect is flip-chip or bump bonding directly onto an interposer or the ASIC (§17.15).
  7. Lens. The silicone lens is moulded on the array or bonded with a primer. Bubbles scatter and attenuate, a thickness error moves the elevation focus and the loss of §17.7, and poor adhesion becomes lens delamination, a classic field failure (§17.14).
  8. Housing, strain relief and cable. The acoustic module is sealed into the housing and the cable is terminated element by element behind a strain relief. Seal leaks admit gel and disinfectant, a weak strain relief concentrates bending at the cable entry until conductors break, and a swapped pair of wires produces a perfect element in the wrong place.
Running case

The process plan for the running case (illustrative): lap the ceramic to 397 ± 4 µm (±1 %, which moves the plate resonance by the same fraction), lap each matching layer to 125 µm with a ±4 µm (±3 %) target, keep bond lines at 3 ± 1.5 µm as assumed in §17.10, cut 384 kerfs (25 µm element kerfs, 20 µm sub-kerfs), keep every heat step below about 95 °C (half of a 190 °C Curie temperature, the low end of the PZT-5H range, so the rule holds for any supplier), terminate 192 coaxial conductors plus grounds. Each tolerance in this list is the σ that went into the Monte Carlo of §17.10.

What changes for a matrix probe

Steps 4–6 merge into one: the diced stack sits on a dematching layer and an interposer or directly on the ASIC, and the interconnect is thousands of bumps or a conductive-adhesive array instead of a flex fan-out (§17.15). There is no cable per element, so a wiring swap cannot happen, but a single bad bump row can take out a line of elements.

What you produce
  • A process flow with a control plan: each step, its controlled parameter and tolerance, the defect it can cause and the in-process test that detects it
  • A thermal exposure limit for every step (depoling budget)
  • A failure-analysis map from symptom (dead, weak, notched, noisy, crosstalk) back to process step
Key takeaways
  • Each step leaves a signature: lapping → fc shift, bond voids → weak or notched elements, dicing → shorts and cracks, joints → opens, lens → delamination and focus error.
  • Keep every heat step well below the Curie temperature (suppliers' rule of thumb: about half; PZT-5H types roughly 190–230 °C, PZT-5A about 350 °C) and check coupling from fr, fa after heat steps.
  • The running case needs 384 cuts and 192 terminations; the per-step tolerances are the σ values of the Monte Carlo in §17.10.

17.12Production acceptance tests and yield

Roadmap step 10 of 11 · test and yield

Every finished array is measured element by element before it ships, with the bench methods of Module 21 (§21.6 and §21.8). The electrical test reads each element's impedance: capacitance at low frequency for opens and shorts, resonance and antiresonance for coupling and depoling. The acoustic test fires each element at a flat reflector and records echo amplitude (sensitivity), centre frequency, bandwidth and pulse length; driving one element and listening on its neighbours gives the crosstalk. The acceptance rule is written on the resulting profile across the array: how many dead elements are allowed, how far a weak element may fall below the median, how much the centre frequency may vary.

Those limits trade yield against image quality, and the trade has a clean statistical form. Random element errors scatter energy evenly into all directions, so the mean beam pattern acquires a flat background under the designed sidelobes (Ramsdale and Howerton, 1980). If each element works with probability \(q\), has a relative amplitude error of variance \(\sigma_a^2\) and a phase error of variance \(\sigma_\varphi^2\), the mean one-way background relative to the main-lobe peak is

\[ \begin{gathered} \overline{B} \approx \frac{(1 - q) + \sigma_a^2 + \sigma_\varphi^2}{q\,N\,\eta_w} \\ \eta_w = \frac{\left(\sum_n w_n\right)^2}{N \sum_n w_n^2} \end{gathered} \]
(17.14)
\(\overline{B}\)
mean background (power) relative to the main-lobe peak, one way
\(q\)
probability that an element works (\(1 - q\) = dead fraction)
\(\sigma_a\)
relative amplitude error; for a spread of \(\sigma_{\mathrm{dB}}\) decibels, \(\sigma_a \approx 0.115\,\sigma_{\mathrm{dB}}\)
\(\sigma_\varphi\)
phase error, rad
\(N\)

\(w_n\)

\(\eta_w\)

Derived from \(E\lvert AF\rvert^2 = \lvert E\,AF\rvert^2 + \sum \mathrm{var}(w_n)\) for independent errors, small-error form. The background falls as 1/N, so a dead element hurts a 64-element aperture twice as much as a 128-element one. One realization has peaks well above the mean background; Ramsdale and Howerton report the peak sidelobe of a single array about 6 dB above the peak sidelobe of the mean pattern.

The yield side is a binomial count. If a single element fails the test (dead, or outside the sensitivity window \(\pm L\)) with probability \(p_e\), a probe of \(N_{\mathrm{tot}}\) elements with at most \(k\) failures allowed passes with probability

\[ \begin{gathered} Y = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \binom{N_{\mathrm{tot}}}{i} p_e^{\,i} (1 - p_e)^{N_{\mathrm{tot}} - i} \\ p_e = p_d + (1 - p_d)\, 2\Phi\!\left(-\frac{L}{\sigma_{\mathrm{dB}}}\right) \end{gathered} \]
(17.15)
\(Y\)
probe yield (pass fraction)
\(N_{\mathrm{tot}}\)
elements per probe
\(k\)
failures allowed by the acceptance rule
\(p_e\)
probability that an element fails
\(p_d\)
probability that an element is dead (process defect)
\(L\)
sensitivity window, ± dB from the median
\(\sigma_{\mathrm{dB}}\)
element sensitivity spread, dB (Gaussian)
\(\Phi\)

For small \(p_e\), \(Y \approx e^{-\lambda}\sum_{i \le k} \lambda^i/i!\) with \(\lambda = N_{\mathrm{tot}} p_e\) (Poisson). Allowing one or two failures raises yield steeply when \(\lambda\) is near 1.

Together the two equations explain the shape of real acceptance rules. Tightening the window \(L\) barely changes the background, because elements just outside the window carry little error energy, but it can cut the yield in half; allowing one dead element raises yield a lot and costs about \(1/N\) of background. Clusters are what the averages hide: two adjacent dead elements in the centre of the aperture produce a coherent sidelobe structure, not a flat background, which is why many rules forbid adjacent failures even when they allow isolated ones.

17.13Thermal design: where the heat comes from and where it goes

Roadmap step 11 of 11 · reliability: thermal design

A probe face warms for reasons that have little to do with tissue absorption. Electrical power that enters an element and does not leave it as sound into the patient becomes heat inside the probe head. The sources are the dielectric loss of the ceramic (its loss tangent \(\tan\delta\)), mechanical loss in the plate (finite \(Q_m\)), the energy the backing absorbs by design (§17.6), loss in the matching layers, and absorption in the lens, which sits right at the patient-contact surface. Probes with electronics in the head add the ASIC power (§17.15). The surface temperature is limited by IEC 60601-2-37 (clause 201.11), separately from the thermal indices of Module 22, with a simulated-use test against a tissue-mimicking test object and a separate test in still air. For the 2007 edition with its 2015 amendment the limits are usually quoted as 43 °C when the transducer is coupled to the tissue-mimicking object and 50 °C in still air (Spicci and Cati 2012, a secondary source; Module 20, §20.7). The 2024 edition revised this clause and its values could not be confirmed from a public source, so check the edition you test to (Module 22, §22.7).

The time-averaged heat follows from the drive duty. For a line-by-line mode each transmit event drives \(N_{\mathrm{act}}\) elements for \(N_{\mathrm{cyc}}/f_0\) seconds, so

(17.16)

\[ \begin{gathered} R_t = \frac{1}{4\,k\,a}, \qquad \tau \approx R\,C \\ \Delta T_{\mathrm{ss}} = \overline{P}_{\mathrm{heat}}\left(\frac{1}{R_t} + \frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{back}}}\right)^{-1} \end{gathered} \]
(17.17)

The isothermal-disc result of steady conduction theory (Carslaw and Jaeger). For a 64-element running-case aperture (19.2 × 7 mm, \(a\) = 6.5 mm) \(R_t \approx\) 77 K/W, so 0.1 W into tissue alone would warm the face by about 8 K. In still air the face loses heat by natural convection only (\(h \approx 10\) W/(m²·K), about 250 K/W for the running-case face), which is why the still-air test is usually the harder one and why heat must be routed backwards. Constants: soft-tissue conductivity from the IT'IS tissue database (muscle 0.49, skin 0.37, fat 0.21 W/(m·K)); \(h\) from the 2–25 W/(m²·K) range for free convection in gases (Incropera, Table 1.1). Both are typical values, and \(R_t\) scales as \(1/k\).

Real designs follow from these two equations. Modes with long bursts and high event rates, pulsed Doppler and shear-wave pushes, dominate the budget rather than B-mode, so the system holds per-mode and per-probe output limits and an auto-freeze timer (Module 20, §20.7). The backward path is engineered: a backing that conducts, a metal or graphite spreader behind it and a housing that sheds heat lower \(R_{\mathrm{back}}\) and carry most of the heat away from the patient. And the lens is a heat source at the contact surface, so a lossier or thicker lens raises the face temperature twice, once by absorbing more and once by forcing a higher drive for the same output.

17.14Reliability and cleaning: how probes fail in the field

Roadmap step 11 of 11 · reliability

A probe is dropped, its cable is bent thousands of times, it is wiped or soaked in disinfectant after every patient and it cycles between room temperature and a warm patient. Its failure modes follow from the materials of the stack. Silicone, epoxy and ceramic expand by very different amounts with temperature (silicone by roughly two orders of magnitude more per kelvin than ceramic, typical values), so every thermal cycle shears the bonds between them; that is the root of lens and matching-layer delamination. Ceramic is brittle, so a drop on the face can crack elements even when the lens looks intact. Depolarization (§17.11) can also happen in use, from overheating or excessive drive. The cable and its strain relief fail by fatigue, and every seal is a path for gel, moisture and disinfectant into the electronics, which is also an electrical-safety path for leakage current.

Cleaning sets its own requirements. The AIUM (American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine) guideline classifies transducers by what they touch: external transducers on intact skin need low-level disinfection, internal (endocavity and transesophageal) transducers are semicritical and need high-level disinfection with a single-use cover, and users are told to follow the manufacturer's reprocessing instructions because some disinfectants damage transducers. For the designer, every outside material (lens, housing, seals, cable jacket) must be qualified against the labelled disinfectants and cycle count by soaking and repeated wiping, followed by acoustic, visual and leakage tests.

17.15Matrix probes and micro-beamforming: the build side

Roadmap branch · matrix probe: every step again, with an ASIC under the stack

A 2D matrix array steers and focuses in both azimuth and elevation, so it needs λ/2 pitch in both directions (17.1), and at 5 MHz that is a 0.154 mm square cell. A modest aperture of 32 × 32 already has 1024 elements; a published pediatric TEE prototype has exactly that, 32 × 32 PZT elements on an ASIC at 5.8 MHz with 67 % −6 dB bandwidth (Daeichin et al., 2018). The first change is electrical. A 0.134 × 0.134 mm element (20 µm kerf) of a 0.228 mm plate has \(C_0 \approx\) 1.0 pF by (17.2), an impedance of about 31 kΩ at 5 MHz. Connected to a cable it forms a capacitive divider:

The second change is mechanical. The stack is built on the ASIC, so there is no room for a thick lossy backing, and silicon is a poor backing anyway. Matrix stacks therefore often place a high-impedance dematching layer behind the piezo, a dense, stiff material such as tungsten carbide, whose \(\rho c\) is near 100 MRayl (a dematching-layer patent quotes "about 100 MRayl", US 9,808,830 B2). In this module's KLM chain, a λ/4 plate (0.228 mm) on a 100 MRayl rear face with the two Desilets layers resonates at 4.49 MHz with 91 % FBW, against 4.67 MHz for the λ/2 plate (0.456 mm) on 5 MRayl backing: the same band from half the thickness. Between the dematching layer and the ASIC sits either an interposer that redistributes the element grid to the ASIC pads, or a direct PZT-on-CMOS (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor) build in which a metal layer and a conductive glue connect each element to a bond pad on the chip (Chen et al., 2018).

The third change is the micro-beamformer. Even with a preamp under every element, thousands of channels cannot go down a cable, so the ASIC groups elements into subarrays, applies a short fine delay to each element so that the subarray points toward the current beam direction, and sums the group into one channel. A TEE probe adds the tightest constraints of all, in size and above all in heat.

TEE matrix probes: size, plane selection and tip heat

The whole array, ASIC and interconnect must fit in a tip small enough to pass the esophagus; conventional multiplane TEE probes rotate a 1D array mechanically through 0–180° to choose the image plane, while a matrix TEE selects planes electronically. The tip cannot shed its heat into the patient: with a contact radius of a few millimetres, (17.17) gives \(R_t\) of the order of 100 K/W, so a watt of ASIC power would be far too much if it had nowhere else to go. Heat is carried back along the shaft, the tip temperature is measured by a sensor, and the system reduces output or stops when it rises; the recall Z-1841-2020 of §17.14 shows what happens when that sensing is not configured in manufacturing.

17.19Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why does a transducer need a matching layer, and what impedance would you pick for one layer on PZT into tissue? · screen

PZT at ≈ 34 MRayl against tissue at 1.5 MRayl transmits only 16 % of the power per pass and reflects 92 % of the pressure, so the plate rings. A \(\lambda /4\) layer transforms the load to \(Z_1^2 /Z_L\); \(\sqrt{Z_p Z_L } \approx 7.2\) MRayl makes that equal to \(Z_p\) at \(f_0\). For a short pulse I would start lower, at the Desilets value \((Z_p Z_L^2)^{1/3} \approx 4.3\) MRayl, and refine with a KLM model. Follow-up: "Why lower?" Because the plate is already resonant; under-matching at \(f_0\) flattens the band.

I2Two matching layers: give the impedances and say where the numbers come from. · onsite

Desilets, Fraser and Kino (1978): \(Z_1 = Z_p^{4/7} Z_L^{3/7} \approx 9\) MRayl next to the plate and \(Z_2 = Z_p^{1/7} Z_L^{6/7} \approx 2.3\) MRayl outside, from a KLM analysis of the resonant plate. The microwave binomial transformer gives 15.7 / 3.3 MRayl, which maximizes flat transmission from a semi-infinite source and in a transducer gives a wider −6 dB band but a ringier pulse. Follow-up: "What would you actually build?" Whatever filled epoxy or ceramic gets near 9 MRayl with acceptable loss, then re-optimize the second layer in the model with the real first-layer properties.

I3What does the backing do, and why not always use a backing matched to the ceramic? · screen

It absorbs the rearward wave and damps the plate: \(Q \approx (\pi /2)Z_p /(Z_B + Z_L)\). A matched backing gives the shortest pulse but sends at least half of the energy backwards on transmit and loses as much on receive (half each way only if the layers present \(Z_p\) to the plate; a bare PZT face into tissue sends about 96 % backwards). Without matching layers, going from air to a matched backing adds about 28 dB of two-way loss in the reference design (−3.6 to −31.2 dB) for 16 % → 127 % bandwidth. With two good matching layers the band is already about 63 % on air backing, so designers use moderate backing (a few MRayl) and let the layers do the broadbanding. Follow-up: the backing must also be thick and lossy enough that its rear echo is below the dynamic range.

I4Silicone lens: convex or concave, and what radius for a 40 mm elevation focus? · screen

Convex, because silicone (≈ 1000 m/s) is slower than tissue: the thick centre delays the central rays. \(F = R/(c_t /c_l - 1)\), so \(R = 40 \times 0.54 = 21.6\) mm. Follow-up: "What happens in a patient with a thick fat layer?" With 1450 m/s the same lens focuses at 48 mm; the elevation focus is set by the lens and the tissue, not by the scanner.

I5What does lens attenuation do to the image besides costing sensitivity? · onsite

Two things. It low-pass filters the pulse (centre frequency down by about \(\beta \sigma^2\), 0.2–0.3 MHz is typical at 5 MHz), which lowers axial resolution and biases spectral QUS unless the reference has the same filter. And it weights the elevation aperture towards the edges because the convex lens is thickest at the centre: an inverse apodization that narrows the elevation main lobe slightly and raises sidelobes, more so at high frequency and wide aperture. Follow-up: "How would you reduce it?" Lower-loss lens materials, thinner lens with a shorter radius at the cost of more aberration, or elevation focusing in a 1.5D/1.75D array instead.

I6Your measured centre frequency is 10 % below the KLM design. List the suspects in order. · research

(1) The lens: it downshifts the spectrum and is often left out of the model. (2) Element geometry: a slender element resonates at the bar frequency, about 12 % below the plate value for \(\nu \approx 0.3\) (FDTD: 0.440 against 0.5). (3) Layer thickness or speed: a layer 10 % thick moves its band 10 % down. (4) Material constants: \(v^D\), \(k_t\) and \(\varepsilon^S\) differ from the datasheet after dicing and poling. (5) Bondlines at high frequency. The test that separates them: measure the electrical impedance of a bare element (\(f_r\), \(f_a\) give \(k_{\mathrm{eff}}\) and v), then of the stack without lens, then with lens, and fit each step.

I7What are lateral modes and how do you avoid them in an array? · onsite

Width resonances of the element bar, near \(f \approx c_p /(2w)\), that couple to the thickness mode through Poisson's ratio when w is comparable with t. They split the spectrum and add ringing and spurious beam lobes. The guideline keeps w/t below about 0.6; wider elements are sub-diced into two or more slender pieces wired in parallel. In composites the pillar aspect ratio and the lateral period play the same role. Follow-up: "How would you check?" A 2D/3D FE model of the element with kerf and filler, then laser vibrometry or the impedance spectrum of a diced sample.

I8When is a KLM model enough, and when do you need finite elements? · onsite

KLM/ABCD is exact for a laterally infinite plate with plane layers: it is the right tool to choose layer impedances and thicknesses, backing and electrical tuning, and it fits measured impedance well for large single elements. FE is needed for anything with edges: array elements with w/t near 1, kerf and sub-dicing, crosstalk through filler, backing and lens, curved lenses, element directivity, composites with non-trivial pillar geometry. Follow-up: "Explicit or implicit?" Explicit time-domain for broadband transient and large models; implicit frequency-domain for exact modes, admittance at a few frequencies and strongly coupled multiphysics.

I9You build an explicit FE model of a 7.5 MHz probe with a silicone lens. How do you choose the mesh and time step? · research

Find the shortest wavelength anywhere at the highest frequency of interest, including shear waves in the epoxies (treat the soft silicone lens as a lossy, nearly fluid medium) and the upper band edge (say 12 MHz). Use 15–20 elements per that wavelength for second-order schemes (−0.3 to −0.55 % phase speed error at \(S = 0.5\)), more if the waves travel many wavelengths. The time step follows from the fastest wave (ceramic longitudinal) and the smallest element through the CFL limit, \(c_{\mathrm{max}} \Delta t/\Delta x \le 1/\sqrt{d}\) for this stencil (codes add safety factors). Then check convergence by refining once and comparing impedance and pulse-echo. Follow-up: cost grows as \(\mathrm{ppw}^4\) in 3D, so exploit symmetry and model one element plus neighbours.

I10Design a matching stack for a 15 MHz probe on a 1-3 composite at 15 MRayl. What changes compared with PZT? · research

The mismatch is smaller (\(T_I = 0.33\) bare), so lower layer impedances: Desilets one layer \((15 \times 2.25)^{1/3} = 3.2\) MRayl, two layers \(15^{4/7}\,1.5^{3/7} = 5.6\) and \(15^{1/7}\,1.5^{6/7} = 2.1\) MRayl, all reachable with filled epoxies and parylene. At 15 MHz a 2500 m/s layer is 42 µm thick, so lapping tolerance and bondlines (keep them to 1–2 µm) dominate, and layer loss in dB stays the same as at 5 MHz for \(\alpha \propto f\). The composite's lower Z and higher \(k_t\) give bandwidth with lighter backing. Follow-up: check the composite's lateral (inter-pillar) resonances against 2 × 15 MHz if harmonic imaging is planned.

I11A 64-element Hann-apodized aperture has a 1 dB element sensitivity spread and two dead elements. Where is the error sidelobe background, and what would you change first? · onsite

With (17.14): \(\sigma_a \approx 0.115\) (so \(\sigma_a^2 = 0.0133\)), \(1 - q = 2/64 = 0.031\), \(\eta_w = 0.67\): \(\overline{B} \approx 0.0445/41.5\), about \(1.1 \times 10^{-3}\), about −30 dB one way, already at the level of the designed Hann sidelobes (−31.5 dB). The dead elements contribute more than the spread, so I would fix the dead elements (repair or tighten the dead count) before tightening the sensitivity window. Follow-up: "And if the two dead elements are adjacent?" Then the background is not flat: a two-element gap is a coherent defect with its own sidelobe structure, which is why acceptance rules forbid adjacent failures.

I12The first pilot lot shows centre frequencies 2 % below the KLM design with normal sensitivity and bandwidth. What is your first hypothesis and how do you check it? · onsite

A systematic, one-sided shift points at bond lines: a few micrometres of glue can only add thickness, and the Monte Carlo of §17.10 shows 3 ± 1.5 µm lowering the mean \(f_c\) by about 1.7 % at nearly unchanged FBW and loss. I would section a reject or measure bond lines by acoustic microscopy, then put the measured mean bond line into the nominal KLM model and re-centre the layer thicknesses. Lens downshift is the second suspect if the measurement included the lens and the model did not (§17.7). Follow-up: "Why not just thin the layers?" Because re-centring without knowing the cause hides a process drift that will move again.

I13Why do matrix probes put electronics under the elements, and what sets the delay range of the micro-beamformer? · research

An element at λ/2 pitch at 5 MHz is about 0.13 × 0.13 mm; on a λ/4 plate (0.228 mm) it has \(C_0 \approx 1\) pF (0.5 pF on a 0.456 mm λ/2 plate); a 2 m cable at ≈ 100 pF/m divides its signal by about 200 (−46 dB, (17.18)), and thousands of cables would not fit anyway. A preamp under each element and subarray summing cut the channel count by \(n_x n_y\) (9 for 3 × 3). The delay range follows from the largest pre-steering angle across one subarray: \(\tau_{\max} = (n - 1)p\sin\theta_{\max}/c\) = 117 ns each way for 3 × 150 µm at ±37°, matching the ≈ 233 ns range of a published 5 MHz ASIC (Chen et al., 2018). Follow-up: "What does the stack look like?" λ/4 piezo on a dematching layer, on an interposer or directly on the ASIC; the backing is gone, and the heat (≈ 1 mW per element) is now in the head.

I14Which imaging mode sets the thermal design of a linear probe, and how would you estimate the face temperature rise? · onsite

The one with the highest drive duty \(D = N_{\mathrm{act}}N_{\mathrm{cyc}}\mathrm{PRF}/f_0\): pulsed Doppler (8 cycles at 10 kHz is about seven times the duty of 2-cycle B-mode at 6 kHz) or shear-wave pushes, not B-mode. Heat = D times the element input power minus what leaves through the lens, plus any electronics (17.16). Then \(\Delta T \approx P(1/R_t + 1/R_{\mathrm{back}})^{-1}\) with \(R_t = 1/(4ka)\), about 77 K/W for a 19.2 × 7 mm aperture, and \(R_{\mathrm{back}}\) the path through backing and housing. In still air the face path is about 250 K/W, which is why the still-air test is hard and heat is routed backwards. Follow-up: "And the limits?" IEC 60601-2-37 clause 201.11 for the edition you test to.

I15An element profile shows one element with normal capacitance but no echo, and another with twice the normal capacitance and a weak echo. What happened to each? · screen

Normal capacitance means the ceramic and its electrodes are connected up to where the capacitance was measured; no echo then points beyond that point (a broken joint or trace between the test point and the cable, if capacitance was measured at the flex) or at a disbond in front of the element (void under a matching layer or lens). Twice the capacitance with a weak echo is the classic short to a neighbour from dicing (smeared electrode or a crack bridging the kerf): two elements now share one channel. Follow-up: "How do you tell a void from an open?" A void keeps the electrical resonance but loses the acoustic echo; an open loses both.

Q5The matching layer of a 5 MHz design came out 8 % too thick. Roughly where does its transmission peak move, and how much sensitivity do you lose at 5 MHz?

The peak moves to \(f_0 /1.08 = 4.63\) MHz. At 5 MHz the loss is small, a few tenths of a dB (0.14 dB at 5 %, 0.52 dB at 10 % for the ideal single layer). The symptom is a low centre frequency, not low sensitivity.

Q6Why does the −6 dB fractional bandwidth reward a matching design that rings?

The −6 dB band is measured between the outermost crossings; a passband with a dip that stays above −6 dB counts as one wide band. In time, a spectral ripple is a pair of echoes, so the pulse has a tail. Judge designs on both the band and the −20 dB (or −40 dB) pulse length.

Q7An element of a 5 MHz array is 0.25 mm wide and 0.40 mm thick. Is it in the safe range for lateral modes, and what thickness resonance should a plane-strain bar model predict relative to the plate value?

\(w/t = 0.63\), at the edge of the usual guideline (≈ 0.6); FDTD at \(w/t = 0.6\) still shows a single mode. Its resonance is near f \(t/c_L \approx 0.42\) (FDTD at 0.6) instead of 0.5, about 16 % below the plate value: design the plate thickness with the bar velocity, not the plate velocity.

Q8A 3 MHz array must steer to ±30° without grating lobes in view. What is the largest pitch?

\(\lambda = 1540/3 \times 10^{6} = 0.513\) mm; \(p \le \lambda /(1 + \sin 30°) = 0.513/1.5 = 0.342\) mm (17.1).

Q9Each element of a 128-element probe fails the acceptance test with probability 0.5 %. What fraction of probes pass if one failure is allowed? If none is?

\(\lambda = 128 \times 0.005 = 0.64\). One allowed: \(0.995^{128} = 0.527\) plus \(128 \times 0.005 \times 0.995^{127} = 0.339\), total 0.865, 86 %. None allowed: 53 % (17.15).

Q10In PW Doppler, 64 elements are driven with 8 cycles at 10 kHz at 5 MHz; each element takes 0.454 W during the burst, 74 % leaves the face and the lens passes 68 %. Estimate the heat in the head and the steady face rise if the tissue path is 77 K/W and the backward path 25 K/W.

\(D = 64 \times 8 \times 10^{4}/(5 \times 10^{6})\) = 1.024 element-s/s. Heat ≈ \(1.024 \times 0.454\) W \(\times (1 - 0.74 \times 0.68)\) = 0.23 W (17.16). The two paths in parallel give \((1/77 + 1/25)^{-1}\) = 18.9 K/W, so \(\Delta T \approx 4.3\) K (17.17), as in Demo 17.9.
