20.1The signal chain and where each number lives

A diagnostic system is two chains that meet at the element. The transmit chain converts a timing pattern into a high-voltage waveform on each element. The receive chain converts microvolt echoes into digital samples without adding much noise, and without clipping echoes that are 100 dB larger. The T/R switch is the junction: it must pass ±100 V outward and then, a fraction of a microsecond later, pass microvolts inward to an amplifier that would be destroyed by the transmit pulse.

The ranges below are generic for current integrated parts (pulser, T/R switch and AFE ICs) and research platforms. Use your own datasheets for a design; the link budget in 20.8 takes every one of these as an input.

20.2Transmit: pulser types, slew rate and harmonics

A switching pulser connects each element to one of a few supply rails through MOSFETs. A unipolar pulser switches between 0 and +V (or a single spike). A bipolar pulser switches between +V and −V, producing a square-wave burst. A 3-level (return-to-zero) pulser adds a clamp to ground; a 5-level pulser adds two intermediate rails ±V/2. A linear (arbitrary-waveform) transmitter is a high-voltage amplifier driven by a DAC: any shape, at much higher dissipation. Everything about the switching types follows from the Fourier series of their stepped waveforms.

\[ \pm V \text{ square wave:}\quad c_n = \frac{4V}{n\pi},\ \ n \text{ odd} \qquad \left( \frac{c_3}{c_1} = -9.54\ \mathrm{dB},\ \ \frac{c_5}{c_1} = -13.98\ \mathrm{dB} \right) \]

An amplitude mismatch \(+V \ne -V\) alone does not: it adds a step at the burst envelope, which is low-frequency.

Why multi-level matters for harmonic and contrast imaging

Tissue harmonic imaging and CEUS receive near \(2f_0\) (or at \(f_0\) after pulse inversion). Any transmit energy at \(2f_0\) is indistinguishable from the tissue's own harmonic, so the pulser must be clean there: tight duty symmetry (even harmonics) and a smooth envelope so the \(f_0\) band's spectral skirt does not spill into \(2f_0\). 5-level and linear drivers also allow amplitude shaping (tapered burst envelope, per-element apodization in transmit) and amplitude-modulation sequences, which a 2-level pulser can only approximate by pulse-width tricks. Pulse inversion cancels the fundamental only to the symmetry of the ±pulses: residual \(\approx \sqrt{\delta a^2 + (2\pi f_0 \Delta t)^2}\), so 1 % amplitude mismatch leaves −40 dB and 1 ns timing mismatch at 5 MHz leaves −30 dB.

Pitfall

Quoting a pulser's "second-harmonic suppression" without the load. Slew rate and edge symmetry depend on \(C_{\mathrm{load}}\), so a part that is clean into a test load can be dirtier on a long cable with a large element. Measure the \(2f_0\) content on the real probe, at every drive voltage used.

20.3T/R switch, protection, cable and element loading

T/R switch. During transmit the switch isolates the LNA and a limiter (diode clamp) holds the LNA input near a volt; during receive the switch connects the element through a few ohms. Switching transients, residual ring-down and LNA overload recovery make a short blind interval after each transmit; its depth equivalent is \(z_{\mathrm{dead}} = c\, t_{\mathrm{rec}}/2\) (1 µs → 0.77 mm). Integrated pulser ICs often include the switch; the LNA must also tolerate the leakage through it.

The element as a source. In receive the element is a voltage source with an internal impedance. Near resonance the Butterworth–Van Dyke (BVD) model is enough: a motional branch (\(R_m\), \(L_m\), \(C_m\)) in parallel with the clamped capacitance \(C_0\).

Purely capacitive limit: \(\lvert H \rvert = C_0/(C_0 + C_{\mathrm{cable}} + C_{\mathrm{in}}) = 30/195\) = −16.3 dB for a 150 pF cable and 15 pF LNA input. At resonance the motional branch lowers the source impedance, and the BVD model gives −12.0 dB. Either way the cable is a large signal loss.

Signal loss is not SNR loss. The element's own thermal noise, \(4kT \operatorname{Re} Z_e\), passes through the same divider as the signal. Only the LNA's noise is added after it. The cable therefore costs SNR only to the extent that it pushes the element's noise down toward the LNA's noise. With an 0.8 nV/√Hz, 1.5 pA/√Hz LNA, the 150 pF cable costs 12.0 dB of signal but only 2.8 dB of SNR (noise figure 0.88 → 3.72 dB). A shunt inductor that resonates the total capacitance, \(L = 1/(\omega_0^2 C_T)\), recovers both at \(f_0\) (\(\lvert H \rvert\) = +0.6 dB, NF 1.28 dB with \(Q_L = 30\)), but only over a narrow band.

Intuition

Think of the element's thermal noise as part of the signal: it is "the noise of the acoustic world", and it rides through the cable with the echo. The LNA's job is to add little on top. A long cable only hurts when it shrinks both below the LNA's own floor. That is why the same cable that is harmless for a 30 pF linear-array element is fatal for a 1 pF matrix element.

Pitfall

Power matching (\(R_{\mathrm{in}} = R_s\)) is not noise matching. A resistive termination equal to a 500 Ω element (the power match) halves the signal, and its own thermal noise alone sets \(F \ge 1 + R_s/R_{\mathrm{term}} = 2\) (3 dB) before the LNA adds anything; a 50 Ω termination on the same element divides the signal by 11 and gives \(F \ge 11\) (10.4 dB). When damping or a defined input impedance is needed, active (feedback) termination provides the impedance with a fraction of the noise, because the feedback resistor is \((1 + A)\) times larger.

20.5TGC, anti-aliasing and the ADC

Time-gain compensation. Echoes from equal scatterers fall by the two-way attenuation \(2\alpha f_0 z\) (dB). The VGA gain follows depth so the echo level at the ADC stays roughly constant. Since \(z = ct/2\):

\[ \frac{dG}{dz} = 2\alpha f_0 \ \ [\mathrm{dB/cm}], \qquad \frac{dG}{dt} = \alpha f_0 c \ \ [\mathrm{dB}/\mu\mathrm{s},\ \text{with } c = 0.154\ \mathrm{cm}/\mu\mathrm{s}] \]
(20.11)
\(G\)

\(f_0\)

\(\alpha = 0.5\) dB/cm/MHz at 5 MHz: 5 dB/cm, 0.385 dB/µs. At 20 cm depth that is 100 dB of attenuation, which no single VGA covers; in practice the gain saturates and deep echoes become noise-limited (Module 10 for the SNR-versus-depth view).

Dynamic range. Total input dynamic range spans the strongest near-field echo (up to the LNA's linear range, ≈ 1 Vpp = 0.354 V rms) to the input noise in band (2.06 µV rms with the default element, cable and LNA over 3.5 MHz): 104.7 dB. The ADC's instantaneous range is ≈ 70 dB. TGC maps the first onto the second by spending gain only where the echoes are small.

Anti-aliasing filter. Content above \(f_s/2\) folds into the band. For signals this means harmonics and out-of-band interference; for noise it means the LNA's wideband noise folds in too. Without a filter, an LNA noise bandwidth of 70 MHz sampled at 40 MHz raises the in-band noise by \(10 \log_{10}(70/20)\) = 5.4 dB. A 3rd-order Butterworth at 15 MHz attenuates 30 MHz (which aliases to 10 MHz at \(f_s\) = 40 MHz) by only 18.1 dB, which is why the filter order and cutoff are traded against the band you want to keep (\(2f_0\) for harmonic imaging).

Ultrasound link

Beamforming relaxes per-channel ADC requirements: N channels add the signal coherently and the uncorrelated quantization and thermal noise incoherently, a gain of \(10 \log_{10} N\) (21.1 dB for 128). This is why 12-bit channels make images with more than 74 dB of displayed dynamic range, and why ultrafast systems with 10-bit channels can still work. Per-channel jitter averages like noise. A shared clock averages only partly: after the focusing delays each channel uses a different clock edge, so only jitter slower than the spread of delays across the aperture (close-in phase noise, wander) errs together on all channels and adds coherently with the signal; fast edge-to-edge jitter decorrelates and averages.

Reading the waterfall

Pitfall

Adding the beamforming gain to a signal that is already ADC-limited, or to "noise" that is actually clutter or reverberation. The \(10 \log N\) gain applies only to noise that is uncorrelated across channels. Electronic interference coupled into all channels at once (switching-supply ripple, a common clock spur) is correlated and survives beamforming; reverberation and off-axis clutter are signals, not noise, and the link budget says nothing about them.

20.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Walk me through the receive chain of one channel. Where is the noise floor set? · screen

Element → cable → T/R switch → LNA → VGA (TGC) → anti-aliasing filter → ADC → serializer → beamformer. The noise floor is set at the LNA input: the element's thermal noise \(4kT \operatorname{Re} Z_e\) (divided by the cable together with the signal) plus the LNA's \(e_n\) and \(i_n \lvert Z_s \rvert\). With 12–24 dB of LNA gain, later stages are divided down; the VGA's job is to put the signal above the ADC noise without clipping. Typical numbers: 0.8 nV/√Hz LNA, 3.5 MHz band, ≈ 1.5–2 µV rms input noise. Follow-up: "How do you check in a running system that the ADC isn't limiting?" Disconnect the probe or mute transmit, set maximum gain, and compare the measured noise in LSB with the ADC's own noise; the analog noise should exceed it by ≥ 6–10 dB.

I2Why do modern systems use 3- or 5-level pulsers instead of simple bipolar ones? · screen

A bipolar square burst has odd harmonics at −9.5 dB (3rd) and −14 dB (5th) and only one degree of freedom. A 3-level pulse with 120° conduction nulls the 3rd for 1.25 dB of fundamental; 5-level switching at 12° and 48° nulls both 3rd and 5th. Extra levels also allow amplitude shaping (tapered envelopes, transmit apodization, amplitude-modulation contrast sequences) without a power-hungry linear amplifier. Harmonic imaging needs the \(2f_0\) band clean, which is about duty symmetry (−36 dBc per ns at 5 MHz) and envelope shape. Follow-up: "Why not always use a linear amplifier?" Dissipation: a linear stage drops (rail − output) × current continuously, which is expensive across 128–256 channels.

I3A 30 pF element drives 150 pF of cable into a high-impedance LNA. How much signal do you lose, and how much SNR? · onsite

Capacitive divider estimate: \(30/(30 + 150 + 15)\) = −16.3 dB; with the motional branch at resonance (BVD, \(R_m\) ≈ 800 Ω) −12.0 dB. SNR drops much less, because the element's thermal noise is divided equally; only the LNA's noise is added after the divider. With 0.8 nV/√Hz and 1.5 pA/√Hz the NF goes from 0.88 to 3.72 dB: 2.8 dB of SNR. Follow-up: "And with a 1 pF matrix element?" Then the divider is ≈ −41 dB, the element's noise falls far below the LNA, and the NF is above 10 dB: amplify in the handle.

I4The input dynamic range is about 100 dB. Why is a 12-bit (74 dB) ADC enough? · onsite

Three reasons. (1) TGC: the 100 dB is mostly a depth-dependent mean level (\(2\alpha f_0 z\)), which the VGA removes; the instantaneous range at a given depth is smaller. (2) Processing gain: quantization noise spreads over \(f_s/2\), the echo occupies \(B\), so filtering gains \(10 \log_{10}(f_s/2B)\), ≈ 7 dB at 40 MS/s and 4 MHz. (3) Beamforming: uncorrelated per-channel noise averages, \(+10 \log_{10} N\) (21 dB for 128). The displayed image then needs only ≈ 60 dB of log-compressed dynamic range. Follow-up: "When would you want 14 bits?" Strong near-field clutter next to weak signals (cardiac apical views), Doppler with large wall signals, and systems with less TGC flexibility (plane-wave acquisitions with fixed gain).

I5What clock jitter do you need for a 14-bit ADC digitizing a 15 MHz harmonic band? · onsite

Set \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{jitter}} \ge 6.02 \cdot 14 + 1.76\) = 86.0 dB: \(\sigma_t \le 10^{-86/20}/(2\pi \cdot 15\ \mathrm{MHz})\) = 0.53 ps rms. In practice the ADC's own SNR is ≈ 75–77 dBFS, so 1 ps (80.5 dB at 15 MHz) is acceptable; 5 ps (66.5 dB) is not. Follow-up: "Does beamforming average jitter?" Per-channel random jitter, yes. A shared clock only partly: its slow components (close-in phase noise, slower than the spread of focusing delays) err together on every channel and do not average, while its fast edge-to-edge jitter is sampled at different instants on different channels and does. The master clock therefore needs a close-in phase-noise spec, not only an rms jitter figure.

I6Size the data path for a 256-channel ultrafast research system, 62.5 MS/s, 14 bit, 10 cm, 5 kHz. · onsite

At the ADCs: 256 × 62.5 MHz × 14 bit = 224 Gb/s. Per event: \(2 \cdot 0.1/1540\) = 130 µs → 8117 samples × 256 × 2 B = 4.16 MB; at 5 kHz, 20.8 GB/s to the host, beyond PCIe Gen3 ×8 (7.9 GB/s) and within Gen4 ×16 (31.5 GB/s) with little margin. IQ at \(f_{\mathrm{IQ}}\) = 5 MHz complex cuts it by 62.5/10 = 6.25 to 3.3 GB/s. Follow-up: "Where would you beamform?" On the GPU for flexibility (ULM, adaptive beamforming), with IQ demodulation and decimation in the FPGA to fit the link.

I7Contrast images show tissue signal that should have been cancelled by pulse inversion. Diagnose. · research

Residual fundamental = \(\sqrt{\delta a^2 + (2\pi f_0 \Delta t)^2}\) plus motion. Check (1) pulser amplitude symmetry (1 % → −40 dB), (2) timing skew between the + and − pulses (1 ns at 5 MHz → −30 dB), including the slew-limited edges on the actual cable, (3) tissue motion between firings (axial displacement \(d\) gives \(\varphi = 4\pi f_0 d/c\)), (4) receive saturation that is polarity dependent. Measure with the probe in a static, linear phantom at low MI: whatever survives is the electronics. Follow-up: "How would amplitude modulation behave?" It cancels with half-amplitude pulses, so it depends on the pulser's amplitude linearity rather than polarity symmetry, and 5-level or linear drivers help.

I8Why can't you cable a 32 × 32 matrix probe element by element to the system? · research

Three budgets fail at once. Channel count: 1024 coax lines and 1024 system channels (492 Gb/s at 40 MS/s, 12 bit). Signal: a ≈ 0.2 mm element has ≈ 1 pF, so a 150 pF cable divider is ≈ −41 dB and the NF exceeds 10 dB. Cable bulk and cost for a TEE or handheld probe. The fix is in-probe electronics: preamplifiers and sub-array (micro-)beamformers that delay and sum groups of elements so only ≈ 100–200 partially beamformed channels travel down the cable (Savord and Solomon 2003; Module 19). Follow-up: "What do you lose?" Per-element channel data, so software beamforming, SoS estimation and aberration correction must work with sub-array sums.

I9What TGC slope do you program at 7.5 MHz, and how much VGA range do you need for 6 cm? · screen

\(2\alpha f_0 = 2 \times 0.5 \times 7.5\) = 7.5 dB/cm, i.e. \(\alpha f_0 c\) = 0.58 dB/µs. Over 6 cm: 45 dB, so a 40 dB attenuator plus some LNA/PGA gain margin is needed; deeper, the gain saturates and the image becomes noise-limited. Follow-up: "Why do users still need TGC sliders?" Attenuation varies (fluid, fat, liver), and the downshifted echo spectrum at depth reduces the effective slope.

I10You measure only 19 dB of beamforming SNR gain with 128 channels on a speckle phantom. Is the hardware broken? · research

Not necessarily. For diffuse scatterers the per-channel echoes are only partially coherent: with equal transmit and receive apertures the van Cittert–Zernike coherence is triangular and the gain is \((2N^2 + 1)/(3N)\) → 19.3 dB, not 21.1 dB. Check the noise side too: correlated interference (supply ripple, clock spurs) does not average. Test with a point target in water and with transmit off; a point should give ≈ 21 dB. Follow-up: "How does aberration show up?" Lower coherence, hence lower gain; that is the basis of coherence-based beamformers (SLSC) and sound-speed estimators.

I11What is active termination and why use it? · screen

A defined LNA input impedance made with feedback rather than a shunt resistor: \(R_{\mathrm{in}} = R_f/(1 + A)\). It damps the element and cable (shorter ring-down, flatter response), but the noise current of \(R_f\) is \((1 + A)\) times smaller than that of a physical \(R_{\mathrm{in}}\). In the model, a 200 Ω resistor gives NF 8.8 dB, a 200 Ω active termination with \(A = 10\) gives 5.9 dB. Follow-up: "When would you leave the input high-impedance?" When SNR matters most and the element is already well damped acoustically; high-Z gives the lowest NF (3.7 dB here).

20.14Self-check

Q1A bipolar pulser drives a 400 pF load at ±80 V with 2 A. How long is each full transition, and what fraction of a 7.5 MHz half period is that?

\(t = C\Delta V/I\) = 400 pF × 160 V / 2 A = 32 ns. Half period at 7.5 MHz = 66.7 ns, so 48 %: the waveform is closer to a trapezoid-triangle and the fundamental is reduced (\(\operatorname{sinc}(7.5\ \mathrm{MHz} \times 32\ \mathrm{ns}) = \operatorname{sinc}(0.24)\) → −0.84 dB).

Q2What conduction angle nulls the 5th harmonic of a 3-level pulse, and what does the fundamental lose?

\(\sin(5\beta/2) = 0 \to \beta = 72^\circ\) or \(144^\circ\). At \(144^\circ\): \(c_1 \propto \sin 72^\circ = 0.951\) (−0.44 dB), and \(c_3 \propto \sin(216^\circ)/3\), \(\lvert c_3/c_1 \rvert = 0.588/(3 \times 0.951) = 0.206\) (−13.7 dB).

Q3An LNA has \(e_n\) = 1 nV/√Hz and \(i_n\) = 2 pA/√Hz. What source resistance minimizes NF, and what is \(\mathrm{NF}_{\mathrm{min}}\)?

\(R_{\mathrm{opt}} = 1\ \mathrm{nV}/2\ \mathrm{pA}\) = 500 Ω. \(F_{\mathrm{min}} = 1 + e_n i_n/(2kT)\) \(= 1 + 2 \times 10^{-21}/(8.01 \times 10^{-21})\) \(= 1.25\) → 0.97 dB.

Q4Your 12-bit ADC (2 Vpp) samples at 50 MS/s. What is the LSB and the ideal quantization noise in a 5 MHz band?

\(\mathrm{LSB} = 2/4096\) = 488 µV; noise \(\mathrm{LSB}/\sqrt{12}\) = 141 µV rms over 25 MHz; in 5 MHz: \(141 \times \sqrt{5/25}\) = 63 µV rms. Relative to a full-scale sine (0.707 V rms): 74.0 + 7.0 = 81.0 dB.

Q5How much does doubling the drive voltage extend noise-limited penetration at 5 MHz and 0.5 dB/cm/MHz? At 10 MHz?

+6.02 dB of SNR divided by \(2\alpha f_0\): \(6.02/5\) = 1.2 cm at 5 MHz, 0.6 cm at 10 MHz. (Acoustic output limits usually forbid the doubling; Module 22.)

Q6Why does a resistive 200 Ω input termination hurt more than the cable in the default receive model?

The resistor adds its own thermal noise current \(4kT/R\) across the input, which, with \(\lvert Z_s \rvert\) of a few hundred ohms, is comparable to the element's noise; it also loads the divider (−14.9 dB). NF rises from 3.7 dB (high-Z) to 8.8 dB. An active termination with A = 10 reduces that noise current ×11 (NF 5.9 dB).

Q7With IQ at \(f_{\mathrm{IQ}} = f_0\) = 3 MHz, by how much does a 40 MS/s RF stream shrink?

RF: 40 M real words/s; IQ: 3 M complex = 6 M words/s. Ratio 40/6 = 6.7.
