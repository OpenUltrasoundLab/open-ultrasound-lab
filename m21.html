21.1Two families of simulators

Ultrasound simulators split along one line: do they assume the medium is linear and homogeneous, or do they solve the wave equation over a grid of the actual medium?

Linear field simulators (Field II, FOCUS) assume a linear, homogeneous medium with a single sound speed. The field of any aperture is then a superposition of free-space Green's functions over its surface, which collapses into one function of position and time: the spatial impulse response \(h(\mathbf{r}, t)\). Scattering is added as a sum of point scatterers in the Born approximation, so an image with 10⁵ to 10⁶ scatterers is routine. They cannot represent a sound-speed change, refraction, reverberation between structures, or harmonic generation.

Full-wave simulators (k-Wave, FDTD codes, explicit finite elements such as PZFlex) discretize the wave equations over the whole domain. They handle heterogeneity, multiple scattering, power-law absorption and nonlinearity, and finite elements also model the transducer structure itself. The price is a grid that must resolve the shortest wavelength everywhere, which in 3D grows as \(f^4\) in run time.

21.2The spatial impulse response

A planar aperture S in a rigid baffle vibrates with uniform normal velocity \(v(t)\). The Rayleigh integral gives the velocity potential at \(\mathbf{r}\) as a sum of delayed, spherically spreading copies of \(v\) from every surface point. Because every copy is the same \(v(t)\), the geometry factors out into one convolution:

\[ \begin{aligned} \varphi(\mathbf{r}, t) &= \int_S \frac{v\left( t - \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}' \rvert / c \right)}{2\pi \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}' \rvert}\, dS = v(t) * h(\mathbf{r}, t) \\ h(\mathbf{r}, t) &= \int_S \frac{\delta\left( t - \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}' \rvert / c \right)}{2\pi \lvert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}' \rvert}\, dS \\ p(\mathbf{r}, t) &= \rho_0 \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t} = \rho_0 \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} * h(\mathbf{r}, t) \end{aligned} \]
(21.1)

\(h\) has units of m/s. It is the potential produced by an impulsive velocity \(\delta(t)\): the aperture's arrival-time histogram, weighted by \(1/(2\pi R)\) per unit area. Pressure is the time derivative of the velocity convolved with h.

The geometric reading. Let \(z\) be the distance from the aperture plane and \(\sigma\) the radius in that plane around the projection of \(\mathbf{r}\). Then \(R^2 = \sigma^2 + z^2\), so \(R\, dR = \sigma\, d\sigma\), and \(dS = \sigma\, d\sigma\, d\theta = R\, dR\, d\theta\). The delta picks \(R = ct\), and \(\delta(t - R/c)\, dR\) integrates to \(c\):

\[ \begin{gathered} h(\mathbf{r}, t) = \frac{c}{2\pi}\, \Theta(t) \\ \Theta(t) = \text{angle of the circle of radius } \sigma(t) = \sqrt{c^2 t^2 - z^2} \text{ lying inside the aperture} \end{gathered} \]
(21.2)

The \(1/R\) spreading cancels exactly against the growth of the ring area. While the whole circle lies inside the aperture, \(h = c\), the plane-wave value.

Disc, on axis. The circle stays inside the disc of radius \(a\) from \(t = z/c\) to \(t = \sqrt{z^2 + a^2}/c\), so \(h\) is a rectangle of height \(c\), and the pressure is two copies of the source waveform: \(p = \rho_0 c\, [v(t - z/c) - v(t - \sqrt{z^2 + a^2}/c)]\). The first is the plane wave from the face, the second an inverted edge wave. Near the face they are separated in time; far away they overlap and the difference becomes a derivative, which is the far-field point-source limit.

Rectangle. With half-widths \(a \le b\) on axis, \(\Theta = 2\pi\) until \(\sigma\) reaches \(a\), then each pair of edges cuts off \(4 \arccos(\cdot/\sigma)\), and \(h\) ends at the corners:

\[ \Theta(\sigma) = 2\pi - 4 \arccos(a/\sigma) \cdot [\sigma > a] - 4 \arccos(b/\sigma) \cdot [\sigma > b], \qquad 0 \le \sigma \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \]

Intuition: the SIR is DAS geometry run in reverse

For a receive beamformer you ask "when does the echo from this pixel reach each element?" The SIR asks the same question for every patch of one element and histograms the answers. A field point that sees all patches at nearly the same delay (far field, on axis) gets a short, tall response; a point with a wide spread of delays (near the face, or far off axis) gets a long, low one that low-pass filters the pulse.

Pitfall: sampling a discontinuous function

Exact SIRs jump (the on-axis disc response is a rectangle). Sampling them directly at a typical simulation rate puts energy errors on every edge. Field II therefore tracks the exact break times and works with integrated responses; its user guide recommends sampling around 100 MHz with these approximations rather than GHz rates with exact expressions. If you write your own SIR code, integrate over each sample interval instead of point-sampling.

21.3From the impulse response to the field: Field II and FOCUS

The continuous-wave field is one Fourier coefficient of the same function: \(P(\mathbf{r}, f) = j2\pi f \rho_0 V(f) H(\mathbf{r}, f)\). Everything a linear code computes (CW beam plots, pulsed peak pressures, pulse-intensity integrals, pulse-echo signals) is a convolution or a Fourier transform of \(h\).

Field II's approximation. Exact \(h\) is known in closed form only for simple shapes, so Field II splits the aperture into small rectangles and uses a far-field response for each: a short pulse of area \(\Delta A/(2\pi R_i)\) at \(t = R_i/c\). The sum converges to the exact \(h\) when each sub-element is in its own far field, roughly \(R \gg d^2/\lambda\) for sub-element size \(d\). Demo 21.2 compares the subdivided field with the exact on-axis response so you can see where that breaks.

FOCUS's approach. The fast nearfield method rewrites the Rayleigh integral over a rectangle or disc as a one-dimensional integral along its edges with a smooth integrand, which removes the \(1/R\) singularity that makes direct sums inaccurate close to the face. The time-space decomposition gives transient fields at lower sampling rates, and the angular spectrum approach propagates a field from one plane to the next. Same physics as Field II, with better accuracy near the transducer per unit of computation.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{disc, on axis, CW:}\quad \lvert p(z) \rvert = 2\rho_0 c\, v_0 \left\lvert \sin\left[ \frac{\pi}{\lambda} \left( \sqrt{z^2 + a^2} - z \right) \right] \right\rvert \\ &\text{last maximum at } z = \frac{a^2}{\lambda} - \frac{\lambda}{4} \approx \frac{a^2}{\lambda} \\ &\text{square of side } 2a\text{: last maximum near } 1.36\, a^2/\lambda \\ &\text{(exact Rayleigh integral; the Fresnel two-slit approximation gives } 1.37\, a^2/\lambda\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} &= -\nabla p \\ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} &= -\rho_0 c^2\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} + \rho_0 c^2 q \end{aligned} \]
(21.6)

Heterogeneity enters through \(\rho_0(\mathbf{r})\) and \(c(\mathbf{r})\). k-Wave adds power-law absorption (a fractional Laplacian term, Treeby & Cox, JASA 2010) and nonlinearity through the coefficient \(\beta = 1 + B/2A\) (Treeby, Jaros, Rendell & Cox, JASA 2012).

FDTD. Pressure and velocity live on staggered grids and leapfrog in time. Every scheme of this type has a dispersion relation linking the numerical frequency \(\omega\) to the wavenumber \(k\). With Courant number \(\mathrm{CFL} = c\Delta t/\Delta x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sin(\omega \Delta t/2) = \mathrm{CFL} \cdot S(k \Delta x) \\ &\text{2nd-order:}\quad S = \sin(k\Delta x/2) \\ &\text{4th-order:}\quad S = (9/8) \sin(k\Delta x/2) - (1/24) \sin(3k\Delta x/2) \\ &\text{pseudospectral (leapfrog):}\quad S = k\Delta x/2 \\ &\text{k-space corrected:}\quad \partial/\partial x \to jk \cdot \operatorname{sinc}(c_{\mathrm{ref}} k \Delta t/2) \;\Rightarrow\; \omega = c_{\mathrm{ref}} k \text{ exactly} \end{aligned} \]
(21.7)

The numerical phase speed is \(\omega/k\). Second-order FD2 is slow by \(\approx (k\Delta x)^2 (1 - \mathrm{CFL}^2)/24\), so 1.5 % at 10 points per wavelength (PPW) and CFL 0.3; over 100 wavelengths that is 1.5 cycles of phase error. Stability needs \(\mathrm{CFL} \cdot \max S \le 1\) in 1D (\(\mathrm{CFL} \le 1\), 6/7 and \(2/\pi\) for the three schemes), divided by \(\sqrt{d}\) in \(d\) dimensions (for all three schemes).

k-space pseudospectral (k-Wave). Spatial derivatives are taken with FFTs, which are exact for band-limited fields, so the grid can approach the Nyquist limit of two points per wavelength (a few more in practice, to represent heterogeneities and sources). The temporal error of leapfrog is then cancelled by the k-space correction (Tabei, Mast & Waag, JASA 2002): in a homogeneous medium at \(c_{\mathrm{ref}}\) the time stepping is exact for any \(\Delta t\), and in heterogeneous media the error is small when \(c\) stays near \(c_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Perfectly matched layers absorb waves leaving the grid.

Explicit FE (PZFlex class). The same leapfrog idea on a mesh of elements, with piezoelectric coupling and an electrical port, so a kerfed, layered, lensed stack is modelled as it is built (Module 17). Mesh density is chosen by refinement studies; the step is limited by the smallest element.

Pitfall: numerical error that looks like physics

A focus that moves or widens in a simulation is exactly the signature of a sound-speed error or an aberration, so grid dispersion can masquerade as the effect you are studying. Before trusting any aberration or sound-speed result, rerun the homogeneous case on the same grid and confirm it matches a linear reference; then refine the grid until the quantity you report stops changing.

21.6Pulse-echo testing and electrical impedance

Setup. A pulser-receiver excites the transducer in degassed water; the echo from a flat, polished reflector (steel or fused quartz) at normal incidence and at the focal or natural-focus distance is recorded on a scope sampling well above the band (here 250 MS/s). The probe is tilted until the echo is maximal. The gated echo is the transducer's two-way impulse response times the reflector, water and diffraction losses.

What is reported. Peak frequency \(f_{\mathrm{pk}}\); the −6 dB points \(f_l\) and \(f_u\) of the echo amplitude spectrum; centre frequency \(f_c = (f_l + f_u)/2\) (the arithmetic mean, as in ASTM E1065; some datasheets use \(\sqrt{f_l f_u}\)); fractional bandwidth \((f_u - f_l)/f_c\); −6 dB and −20 dB pulse durations from the envelope; and loop sensitivity \(20 \log_{10}(V_{\mathrm{echo,pp}}/V_{\mathrm{tx,pp}})\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{loop sensitivity (dB)} &= 20 \log_{10}\left( V_{\mathrm{echo,pp}} / V_{\mathrm{tx,pp}} \right) \\ \text{two-way insertion loss} &\approx \text{loop sensitivity} - 20 \log_{10} R_{\mathrm{refl}} + 2\, \alpha_w z f_c^2 \\ R_{\mathrm{steel/water}} &= \frac{45.4 - 1.48}{45.4 + 1.48} = 0.937 \ \ (-0.57\ \mathrm{dB}) \\ \alpha_w &\approx 2.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{dB/(mm \cdot MHz^2)} \end{aligned} \]

The IEEE Std 176 relation gives the thickness coupling \(k_t^2 = (\pi/2)(f_r/f_a) \tan[(\pi/2)(f_a - f_r)/f_a]\) (Module 16); the capacitance well above resonance (or the low-frequency capacitance times \(1 - k_t^2\)) gives the clamped permittivity \(\varepsilon^S\); the peak sharpness gives the loss. Per element, an open (broken lead) shows almost no capacitance and no resonance, a short shows a near-zero \(\lvert Z \rvert\), and a depoled element keeps its capacitance but loses the resonance.

21.7Hydrophone measurements and acoustic output

Hydrophones. 21.10How experts think about it

An expert first decides which physics the question needs: if the medium can be treated as homogeneous and linear, the spatial impulse response answers everything quickly and exactly; if the answer depends on heterogeneity, nonlinearity or the transducer structure, only a full-wave code will do, and its grid must be proven converged. On the bench, every number is a ratio with a definition behind it (two-way or one-way, −6 dB or −3 dB, arithmetic or geometric centre, derated or not), and every measuring instrument (the hydrophone above all) filters what it measures. Expert habit: model the instrument, then compare.

The spatial impulse response is the whole linear field: everything else is a convolution or a Fourier transform of it.

21.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What is a spatial impulse response, and why is it the core of Field II? · screen

It is the velocity potential at a point when the aperture moves with an impulsive velocity: \(h(\mathbf{r}, t) = \int \delta(t - R/c)/(2\pi R)\, dS\). In a linear homogeneous medium every field quantity follows from it by convolution with the excitation (\(p = \rho_0 v' * h\)) and pulse-echo is \(h_t * h_r\). Field II splits the aperture into small rectangles and sums their far-field responses, so it never solves a wave equation. Follow-up: "What does it cost you?" No heterogeneity, no nonlinearity, no multiple scattering.

I2Sketch h(t) for a rectangular element at a point on axis and at a point beyond its edge. · onsite

On axis: zero until \(z/c\), a flat plateau at \(c\) until the circle reaches the nearer edge (\(\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}/c\)), a decay through the farther edge, and zero after the corners at \(\sqrt{z^2 + a^2 + b^2}/c\). Beyond the edge: starts when the circle touches the nearest edge, never reaches \(c\) (only part of the circle is inside), shows knees at the corner arrivals, and lasts much longer. For 4 × 6 mm at z = 15 mm: on axis 0.28 µs at height \(c\); at x = 6 mm, 1.13 µs with peak 0.20c. Follow-up: "What does a long h do to the pulse?" It low-pass filters it, so off-axis pulses are longer and weaker.

I3When would you use k-Wave instead of Field II for a sound-speed estimation study? · research

Whenever the estimator's behaviour depends on the heterogeneity: layered fat and muscle, refraction at oblique interfaces, aberration-induced coherence loss, reverberation clutter. Field II with a global c only tests the homogeneous mismatch problem. I would validate the k-Wave setup in homogeneous water against Field II first, choose PPW from a convergence test, and smooth sharp interfaces. Follow-up: "Cost?" A 2D study at 5 MHz is cheap; 3D needs a GPU and tens of GB at 3 PPW.

I4Your FDTD simulation of a homogeneous medium shows the focus 1.5 mm shallower than expected. What do you check? · onsite

Numerical dispersion first: at 7–8 PPW a second-order scheme has phase-speed errors of 2–5 % across the band and they depend on direction, which distorts focusing (Demo 21.4). Then CFL, source discretization, absorbing boundary reflections, and whether the delays use the same c as the grid. Refine \(\Delta x\) by 2 and see if the focus moves; switch to fourth order or pseudospectral. Follow-up: "What is the convergence rate?" \(\Delta x^2\) for FD2, so halving the grid should cut the error by about 4.

I5Define centre frequency and fractional bandwidth from a pulse-echo test. What mistakes are common? · screen

Take the amplitude spectrum of the gated echo, find the −6 dB points \(f_l\), \(f_u\); \(f_c = (f_l + f_u)/2\), \(\mathrm{FBW} = (f_u - f_l)/f_c\). Mistakes: reporting the peak as the centre (wrong by 10–20 % for double-humped spectra), mixing −3 dB one-way with −6 dB two-way, gates that include reverberations (ripple) or cut the echo (broadening), power versus amplitude dB, and not stating arithmetic versus geometric centre. Follow-up: "How long is a 5 MHz, 65 % pulse?" About 0.27 µs at −6 dB for a Gaussian.

I6What is loop sensitivity, and how would you compare it between two probes measured in different labs? · onsite

\(20 \log_{10}(V_{\mathrm{echo,pp}}/V_{\mathrm{tx,pp}})\) for a flat reflector. To compare, normalize the reflector (steel −0.57 dB, quartz differs), the water path (\(2.2 \times 10^{-4}\) dB/(mm·MHz²) round trip), diffraction to the reflector distance, the pulser source impedance and the receiver load (50 Ω versus high impedance changes the voltage), and the cable. Follow-up: "Is loop sensitivity a transducer property?" No, it is a property of transducer + cable + electronics + target geometry.

I7How large may a hydrophone be for a focused 10 MHz beam at F# 2? · research

\(\lambda F\# = 0.31\) mm. For under 0.5 dB peak loss the radius should be below about \(0.3\, \lambda F\#\): \(b \lesssim 0.09\) mm, a diameter under about 0.2 mm, and harmonics need smaller still. The loss law is \((\pi b/\lambda F\#)^2/16\). Then check the hydrophone's directivity, its effective (not geometric) size at the frequency, and its bandwidth for the harmonic content. IEC 62127-1 gives the formal size criterion and corrections. Follow-up: "And for \(I_{\mathrm{SPTA}}\)?" The intensity error is twice the pressure error in dB.

I8Walk me through how MI is computed for a 510(k). · onsite

Hydrophone scans in water under IEC 62127-1 conditions; deconvolve or correct for sensitivity, bandwidth and spatial averaging; derate the peak rarefactional pressure by 0.3 dB/cm/MHz; find the depth defined by IEC 62359 (via the derated pulse-intensity integral); \(\mathrm{MI} = p_{\mathrm{r.3}}/\sqrt{f_{\mathrm{awf}}}\) with p in MPa and f in MHz; search operating modes for the global maximum; report against the FDA Track 3 limit MI ≤ 1.9 with uncertainty. Follow-up: "Why \(\sqrt{f}\)?" The cavitation threshold data scale roughly as \(p \propto \sqrt{f}\), so MI approximates the cavitation likelihood.

I9A probe returns from the field with two dead elements. Is it still usable? · screen

Quantify: for 64 active elements, uniform weights, the main lobe drops \(20 \log(62/64)\) = 0.28 dB and a far-sidelobe floor appears near \(10 \log(2/62^2)\) = −32.8 dB; with an apodized aperture the floor is relative to a much lower sidelobe level and is more visible. Two adjacent dead elements produce structured lobes rather than a floor. Whether that fails depends on the application's contrast requirement and the QA limit; many services set a maximum number of dead elements and forbid adjacent ones. Follow-up: "How do you detect them?" Per-element pulse-echo or impedance; a phantom image shows vertical dropout streaks in linear arrays.

I10How do you turn an impedance measurement into a KLM model? · research

Start with the bare plate in air: \(f_r\) and \(f_a\) give \(k_t\) through the IEEE 176 relation, the capacitance well below resonance times \(1 - k_t^2\) (or the capacitance well above resonance) gives \(\varepsilon^S\), the \(Q\) of the peaks gives the losses; \(f_a\) fixes the plate's sound speed for its thickness. Then add layers and backing and fit their impedances and thicknesses to the finished element's impedance in air and water, and finally fit the electrical network to the pulse-echo spectrum. Peaks the 1D model cannot produce are lateral modes: hand those to FE. Follow-up: "Example?" \(f_r\) = 3.00, \(f_a\) = 3.45 MHz gives \(k_t\) = 0.53.

21.14Self-check

Q1What is the value of h on axis of a disc while the arrival circle is inside the aperture, and what pressure waveform results?

\(h = c\) (\(\Theta = 2\pi\)). The pressure is \(\rho_0 c\, [v(t - z/c) - v(t - \sqrt{z^2 + a^2}/c)]\): the plane wave plus an inverted edge wave.

Q2At what depth does a 4 × 6 mm element at 3 MHz enter its far field on axis by the "SIR shorter than a period" criterion?

\((a^2 + b^2)/(2zc) = 1/f_0\) gives \(z = (a^2 + b^2) f_0/(2c) = 13 \times 3/(2 \times 1.54)\) = 12.7 mm; the point-source formula is good (≥ 0.9) only beyond about 3 times that.

Q3How much phase error does FD2 accumulate over 100 wavelengths at 10 PPW, CFL 0.3?

The phase speed is 1.5 % low, so 0.015 × 100 = 1.5 cycles (540°). FD4 at the same grid accumulates under 0.1 cycle.

Q4A two-way echo spectrum has −6 dB points at 3.4 and 6.6 MHz. Give \(f_c\), FBW and the expected −6 dB envelope length if the pulse is Gaussian.

\(f_c\) = 5.0 MHz, \(\mathrm{FBW} = 3.2/5\) = 64 %; \(\tau_{-6} = 0.883/3.2\ \mathrm{MHz}\) = 0.28 µs.

Q5What peak loss does a 0.2 mm diameter hydrophone cause at a 7.5 MHz, F# 1.5 focus?

\(\lambda F\# = 0.205 \times 1.5\) = 0.308 mm, \(b\) = 0.1 mm, \(b/\lambda F\# = 0.325\); loss \(\approx (\pi \times 0.325)^2/16 = 0.065\), about −0.58 dB (exact disc average ≈ −0.57 dB).

Q6A 3 MHz beam has \(p_r\) = 2.4 MPa in water at 4 cm. What is the derated MI at that depth?

Derating 0.3 × 3 × 4 = 3.6 dB → factor 0.661, \(p_{\mathrm{r.3}}\) = 1.59 MPa, \(\mathrm{MI} = 1.59/\sqrt{3} = 0.92\).

Q7Which simulator would you pick for (a) a 10⁶-scatterer B-mode phantom, (b) a skull aberration study, (c) the lateral modes of a 1-3 composite element?

(a) Field II (linear, fast). (b) k-Wave or another full-wave code with the heterogeneous medium (elastic if shear conversion matters). (c) Explicit FE such as PZFlex/OnScale.

Q8Why does crosstalk hurt a phased array more than a linear array?

It multiplies the element pattern by \(1 + 2\kappa \cos(kp \sin\theta)\), which is largest at broadside and smallest near \(kp \sin\theta = \pi\). Phased arrays steer to ±45° and beyond, where that factor costs several dB at −20 dB crosstalk; linear arrays mostly fire near broadside.
