Digital filter design: FIR, IIR and ultrasound filters

An ultrasound chain is a cascade of filters: the RF band-pass after the ADC, the decimation and IQ low-pass, the Hilbert FIR, the Doppler wall filter on 8–16 slow-time samples, the smoothing on parametric maps. This module turns filter design into geometry (poles and zeros), into order formulas you can size from memory, and into the failure modes that matter for imaging: dispersion, edge transients, short-ensemble transients and fragile coefficients.

You will be able to

  • Read magnitude, phase and group delay off a pole-zero plot, and place poles and zeros to shape a response
  • Size an FIR from a spec in your head (Kaiser window, equiripple estimate, the A/22 rule) and check it
  • Design Butterworth, Chebyshev and elliptic filters by the bilinear transform with prewarping, and ship them as biquads
  • Predict the delay and the pulse distortion of a design, and say when zero-phase filtering is allowed
  • Build decimation chains (half-band, polyphase, CIC) and a Hilbert FIR
  • Choose and defend a Doppler wall filter for an 8–16-sample ensemble

In 60 seconds
  1. The response is z-plane geometry: A digital filter’s gain at a frequency is the product of distances to its zeros (numerator roots) over those to its poles (denominator roots), seen from the unit circle. A pole at radius r rings for about 1/(1 − r) samples: r = 0.95 rings about 20 pulses, longer than a colour-flow ensemble. §09.1
  2. Window FIRs: shape sets ripple, length sets edge: Window design tapers an ideal filter; the taper shape sets ripple and its length sets edge sharpness. A linear-phase FIR of order M delays the signal by M/2 samples. An 8-to-9 MHz edge at 40 MHz sampling with 60 dB stopband needs order 145–148, a delay worth 1.42 mm. §09.2
  3. Optimal FIRs need fewer taps: Equiripple design (Parks–McClellan) minimizes the peak error and spreads it evenly, so it meets a spec with fewer taps than a window. Allowing more passband than stopband ripple shortens it further: the same 8/9 MHz, 60 dB spec needs order 104 with passband ripple ten times stopband, against 148 by Kaiser window. §09.3
  4. IIRs: low order, nonlinear phase: Recursive (IIR) filters copy analog designs through the bilinear transform, which keeps stability but warps frequency, so band edges are prewarped. Elliptic designs need the lowest order: 8 where Butterworth needs 53. All ship as biquads (second-order sections), because high-order direct forms go unstable once coefficients are quantized. §09.4
  5. Uneven group delay distorts the pulse: If frequencies are delayed by different amounts, the pulse spreads and its envelope skews. A mismatch between carrier and envelope delay also rotates the phase, which phase-based estimators read as displacement. Filtering forward then backward gives zero phase, but it is non-causal and edge-sensitive, so it suits offline use with padding. §09.5
  6. Never compute a sample you will discard: When lowering the sample rate by a factor D, compute only the outputs you keep: polyphase decimation costs about (M + 1)/D multiplies per input sample for an order-M filter. First suppress everything from \(f_s/(2D)\) to \(f_s/2\), with \(f_s\) the input rate, because that band folds into the kept band. §09.6
  7. On short ensembles a wall filter is a matrix: Colour Doppler has only 8–16 pulses per pixel, so a wall filter, which removes slow tissue echoes, acts as a matrix on that vector. A zero-state IIR barely removes stationary tissue, due to its start-up transient. A degree-p regression filter cuts near \((0.44 + 0.41p)\,\mathrm{PRF}/N\), PRF the pulse rate, N the pulses. §09.7
From the field
  • Use linear-phase FIRs where phase or pulse shape matters; subtract their delay, half the order. An elliptic IIR needs far lower order, but its group delay peaks near band edges, smearing the pulse. The FIR delay matters: 74 samples, 1.42 mm, for the 8/9 MHz, 60 dB filter at 40 MHz. §09.4 §09.5
  • Never run a zero-state IIR wall filter (tissue-clutter filter) on an 8–16-pulse ensemble. Its start-up transient leaves stationary clutter in: DC drops by only 12.6 dB at 10 pulses. Initializing by projection reaches −43 dB at 0.01 times the pulse rate without losing output samples; regression or FIR filters also work. §09.7
  • Write filter specs in physical units (MHz, dB, cm/s) and normalize only at design time. A normalized cutoff means a different clinical cutoff on every preset. A degree-2 regression wall filter on 10 pulses cuts at 7.8 cm/s at 5 MHz and a 4 kHz pulse rate; change either and the velocity cutoff moves. §09.7
  • Ship IIR filters as cascaded biquads and test them with quantized coefficients. High-order direct forms cluster poles (denominator roots) whose positions are hypersensitive to rounding. An 8th-order Butterworth at 0.02 times the sample rate goes unstable in 16- or 24-bit direct form and stays exact as biquads, second-order sections. §09.4
Conventions in this module

M is the FIR order: M + 1 taps, linear-phase delay M/2 samples (Module 03 counts taps, so its (M − 1)/2 is the same number). \(\omega = 2\pi f/f_s\) in rad/sample; \(\Delta f\) is a transition width normalized to \(f_s\) and \(\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta f\). \(A = -20\log_{10}\delta\) in dB (amplitude). For IIR specs, \(A_p\) is the peak-to-peak passband ripple and \(A_s\) the minimum stopband attenuation, both in dB. Default sampling rate for RF examples: \(f_s\) = 40 MHz.

09.1The z-plane: the response is geometry

Recap from Module 03. Section 03.7 derives the transfer function \(H(z) = B(z)/A(z)\) from the difference equation (03.21), the vector picture of the response (03.23), and the stability condition (03.24). Factored as \(H(z) = g\prod_k (1 - z_k z^{-1})/\prod_k (1 - p_k z^{-1})\), the magnitude on the unit circle is the product of distances from \(e^{j\omega}\) to the zeros over the product of distances to the poles, and the phase is the sum of the corresponding angles. A pole close to the circle makes a peak where the circle passes it; a zero on the circle makes an exact null. This module uses that geometry as a design tool: where to put roots to meet a spec, and what each root costs in delay.

Group delay root by root. \(\tau_g(\omega) = -d\varphi/d\omega\) is additive over factors. For a root at \(r e^{j\theta}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{zero: } \tau_z(\omega) = \frac{r^2 - r\cos(\omega - \theta)}{1 - 2r\cos(\omega - \theta) + r^2}, \qquad \text{pole: } \tau_p(\omega) = -\tau_z(\omega) \\ &\text{pole at } \omega = \theta:\ \ \tau = \frac{r}{1 - r}\ \text{samples} \\ &\text{−3 dB full width} \approx 2(1 - r)\ \text{rad/sample}, \qquad \text{ringing} \approx \frac{1}{1 - r}\ \text{samples} \end{aligned} \]
(09.1)

For \(r = 1 - \varepsilon\), \(\lvert e^{j\omega} - p\rvert^2 \approx \varepsilon^2 + (\omega - \theta)^2\), so the peak falls 3 dB at \(\lvert\omega - \theta\rvert = \varepsilon\). The impulse response decays as \(r^n = e^{n\ln r}\), time constant \(-1/\ln r \approx 1/(1 - r)\). r = 0.95: width 0.1 rad/sample, delay 19 samples at the peak, ringing ≈ 20 samples. The one-pole formula (03.17) of Module 03 is the θ = 0 case.

Worked example (RF anti-alias low-pass). \(f_s\) = 40 MHz, pass ≤ 8 MHz, stop ≥ 9 MHz, A = 60 dB. \(\Delta\omega = 2\pi\cdot 1/40 = 0.1571\) rad/sample, β = 5.653, \(M = 52/(2.285 \times 0.1571) = 144.9 \to 145\) (09.3). Ask for A = 61: β = 5.76, M = 148, measured 60.9 dB (passband deviation 0.0009). Delay M/2 = 74 samples = 1.85 µs, which is 1.42 mm of depth at c/2 = 770 m/s.

Key takeaways
  • β = 0.1102(A − 8.7) above 50 dB and M = (A − 8)/(2.285 Δω), good to about ±0.5 dB.
  • The window shape (β) fixes the ripple floor (−59.8 dB at β = 5.65 at any length); the length fixes only the transition width.
  • Window designs have δp = δs and sit at half amplitude (−6 dB) at ωc = (ωp + ωs)/2.
  • 8/9 MHz at 40 MHz, 60 dB: M = 145 to 148, delay 74 samples = 1.85 µs = 1.42 mm of depth.

09.3Optimal FIRs: frequency sampling, least squares, equiripple

Write a type I linear-phase filter (M even, symmetric taps) as a delay times a real amplitude: \(H(e^{j\omega}) = e^{-j\omega M/2} A(\omega)\), with \(A(\omega) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{M/2} a_k \cos k\omega\) and \(h[M/2 \pm k] = a_k/2\). Every design method chooses the M/2 + 1 numbers \(a_k\) by a different criterion on the error \(E(\omega) = W(\omega)\left(A(\omega) - D(\omega)\right)\) over the bands, ignoring the transition ("don't care") band.

09.4IIR design: analog prototypes, the bilinear transform and biquads

IIR design borrows a century of analog filter theory. Design a normalized analog low-pass prototype \(H_a(s)\), transform it (low-pass to low-pass, high-pass or band-pass by substituting s), then map s to z. The bilinear transform is the standard map:

\[ \begin{aligned} s &= \frac{2}{T}\,\frac{1 - z^{-1}}{1 + z^{-1}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad z = \frac{1 + sT/2}{1 - sT/2} \\ s = j\Omega &\ \mapsto\ \lvert z\rvert = 1 \ \text{ with } \ \Omega = \frac{2}{T}\tan\frac{\omega}{2}, \qquad \text{prewarp: } \Omega_p = \frac{2}{T}\tan\frac{\omega_p}{2} \end{aligned} \]
(09.5)

\(\omega\)

The left half-plane maps inside the unit circle (stability preserved) and the whole jΩ axis maps once onto the circle, so there is no aliasing, but the frequency axis is compressed: Ω = ∞ lands at Nyquist. Prewarp each critical edge, design the analog filter at the warped edges, transform: the digital edges land exactly. Analog zeros at infinity map to z = −1. Low-pass to band-pass, \(s \to (s^2 + \Omega_0^2)/(B s)\) with \(\Omega_0^2 = \Omega_1\Omega_2\), \(B = \Omega_2 - \Omega_1\), doubles the order.
\[ \begin{aligned} \varepsilon_p &= \sqrt{10^{A_p/10} - 1}, \qquad \varepsilon_s = \sqrt{10^{A_s/10} - 1}, \qquad \text{selectivity } \Omega_s/\Omega_p \\ \text{Butterworth:}\quad N &\ge \frac{\ln(\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_p)}{\ln(\Omega_s/\Omega_p)} \\ \text{Chebyshev I and II:}\quad N &\ge \frac{\operatorname{acosh}(\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_p)}{\operatorname{acosh}(\Omega_s/\Omega_p)} \\ \text{Elliptic:}\quad N &\ge \frac{K(k)\,K'(k_1)}{K'(k)\,K(k_1)}, \qquad k = \frac{\Omega_p}{\Omega_s}, \quad k_1 = \frac{\varepsilon_p}{\varepsilon_s} \end{aligned} \]

Ship it as biquads. Factor H into second-order sections, pair each complex pole pair with the nearest zero pair, and order the sections by pole radius. The reason is root sensitivity: for a polynomial with roots \(p_i\), \(\partial p_i/\partial a_k = -p_i^{N-k} / \prod_{j \ne i}(p_i - p_j)\). Narrowband filters cluster their poles near z = 1, the denominators collapse, and tiny coefficient errors move poles across the circle. Each biquad has \(a_1 \in (-2, 2)\) and \(a_2 \in (-1, 1)\) and only two roots to protect (Figure 09.B).

09.5Phase, group delay, pulse distortion and zero-phase filtering

Recap from Module 03. Group delay \(\tau_g = -d\varphi/d\omega\) and phase delay \(\tau_\varphi = -\varphi/\omega\) are defined in Section 03.5, (03.16): a narrowband pulse \(a(t)\cos(\omega_0 t)\) through H comes out as approximately \(\lvert H(\omega_0)\rvert\, a(t - \tau_g)\cos\left(\omega_0 (t - \tau_\varphi)\right)\), and linear phase \(\varphi = -\omega M/2\) gives \(\tau_g = \tau_\varphi = M/2\) at every \(\omega\), so the shape is preserved. The design question here is what happens when a filter is not linear phase.

The approximation holds when \(\tau_g\) is nearly constant across the pulse band. When it varies (an IIR near its band edge), different frequencies arrive at different times: the pulse spreads and its envelope becomes asymmetric, with a tail on the side of the steeper edge. A mismatch \(\tau_\varphi \ne \tau_g\) rotates the carrier under the envelope, which a phase-based delay estimator (Module 11) reads as displacement.

09.6Multirate and special-purpose filters: decimation, half-band, polyphase, CIC, Hilbert

Decimation by D keeps every D-th output: \(y[m] = \sum_k h[k]\, x[mD - k]\). Everything between \(f_s/(2D)\) and \(f_s/2\) folds into the new band, so the low-pass must remove it first. If only \(0 \dots f_{\text{pass}}\) must stay clean, aliases may land in the transition band: \(f_{\text{stop}} = f_s/D - f_{\text{pass}}\). Polyphase splits h into D sub-filters \(h_i[m] = h[mD + i]\) that run at the output rate, so only kept samples are computed: \((M + 1)/D\) multiplies per input sample instead of M + 1.

09.9How experts think about it

A filter expert sees three things at once: the pole-zero map (where the response comes from), the error budget (\(\delta_p\), \(\delta_s\) and the transition width, which set the order before any design runs), and the implementation (delay, transient, word length). FIRs are chosen for linear phase, polyphase cost and robustness; IIRs for low order when phase does not matter or can be cancelled offline. On short records, such as Doppler ensembles, the steady-state frequency response is a fiction and the filter must be judged as a matrix.

09.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why are RF and IQ filters in a beamformer almost always FIR? · screen

Linear phase: a constant, known delay \(M/2\) that is identical on every channel, so filtering never looks like an aberration and the delay is subtracted once. Polyphase decimation divides the cost by D, which erases most of an IIR's per-output advantage (for the 8/9 MHz spec, 53 folded multiplies vs about 20 for an 8th-order elliptic, before decimation). No stability, limit-cycle or coefficient-sensitivity problems in fixed point. Follow-up: how many taps for 3–7 MHz at 40 MHz with 1 MHz transitions and 40 dB? The low-pass estimate says about 61; two transitions push the true equiripple minimum to 72.

I2A 129-tap linear-phase FIR runs at 50 MHz on RF. What delay do you remove, in samples, µs and mm? · screen

Order 128, delay 64 samples = 1.28 µs. In pulse-echo depth that is \(c\,t/2\) = 770 m/s × 1.28 µs = 0.99 mm. Follow-up: what if the filter has an even number of taps? Then the delay is a half sample; either pad to odd length or account for the half-sample shift in the delay law, otherwise IQ phase is biased by \(\pi f_0/f_s\).

I3Derive the bandwidth and the peak group delay of a pole at \(r e^{j\theta}\). · onsite

\(\lvert 1 - r e^{j\theta}e^{-j\omega}\rvert^2\) \(= 1 - 2r\cos(\omega - \theta) + r^2\) \(\approx (1 - r)^2 + r(\omega - \theta)^2\) near θ, so half power at \(\lvert\omega - \theta\rvert \approx 1 - r\): full width \(2(1 - r)\) rad/sample. The phase of \(1/(1 - re^{-j\Delta})\) is \(-\operatorname{atan}\left(r\sin\Delta/(1 - r\cos\Delta)\right)\); differentiating at Δ = 0 gives the group delay \(r/(1 - r)\). r = 0.95: 0.1 rad/sample and 19 samples. Follow-up: convert to a color-flow PRF of 4 kHz: 64 Hz wide, 4.75 ms delay, longer than a 12-pulse ensemble (3 ms).

I4Size a Kaiser-window low-pass: 50 dB, 0.5 MHz transition at \(f_s\) = 20 MHz. Then estimate equiripple with \(\delta_p = 0.01\). · onsite

\(\Delta f = 0.025\), \(\Delta\omega = 0.157\) rad/sample. \(\beta = 0.5842 \times 29^{0.4} + 0.07886 \times 29 = 4.53\). \(M = 42/(2.285 \times 0.157) = 117\), round to 118 for an integer delay. Equiripple: \(\delta_s = 0.00316\), \(-20\log_{10}\sqrt{0.01 \times 0.00316} = 45.0\) dB, \(M \approx (45.0 - 13)/(14.6 \times 0.025) = 88\), so budget about 90 after the few-percent underestimate. Follow-up: why can the window design not take advantage of the 0.01 passband? Because its \(\delta_p\) equals \(\delta_s\) by construction.

I5Why does the bilinear transform need prewarping, and why not use impulse invariance instead? · onsite

The bilinear map sends Ω to \(\omega = 2\operatorname{atan}(\Omega T/2)\), so analog edges land at lower digital frequencies (15 MHz lands at 11.0 MHz for \(f_s\) = 40 MHz). Prewarping designs the prototype at \((2/T)\tan(\omega_p/2)\) so the edges land where specified. Impulse invariance samples \(h_a(t)\), which aliases its spectrum: fine for steep low-pass prototypes, impossible for high-pass and band-stop, whose response does not fall off. Follow-up: what does the bilinear transform do to an analog zero at infinity? It maps to z = −1, a null at Nyquist.

I6Your 8th-order elliptic low-pass works in MATLAB but oscillates on the FPGA. Diagnose. · onsite

Almost certainly direct-form coefficients: the \(a_k\) of a narrowband 8th-order filter span tens, so after quantization the clustered poles move outside the unit circle (16-bit direct form moves an 8th-order Butterworth pole at \(0.02\,f_s\) to radius 1.30). Re-implement as four biquads with poles paired to nearest zeros, scale each section against overflow, put the highest-Q section last. If it still hums with zero input, it is a limit cycle: add more state bits or use magnitude truncation / error feedback. Follow-up: why are FIRs immune? No feedback: quantization moves zeros, which changes the stopband floor but cannot destabilize.

I7Color flow shows strong clutter bleeding into the velocity map near vessel walls. The wall filter is a 2nd-order Butterworth high-pass on 10 pulses. What do you check first? · research

The initialization. With zero initial state, the clutter step at the first pulse rings through the ensemble: on N = 10 at \(f_c\) = 0.1 PRF the DC suppression is only 12.6 dB instead of infinite. Step initialization nulls DC exactly but gives only −32 dB at 0.01 PRF; projection initialization gives −43 dB with all 10 outputs kept; a degree-2 regression filter −63 dB. Follow-up: what does projection initialization compute? \(\mathbf{s}_0 = -\mathbf{G}^{+}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{x}\), minimizing output energy over the initial state, so \(\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{+})\mathbf{F}\).

I8When is a polynomial regression wall filter enough, and when do you need an SVD filter? · research

Regression assumes clutter is a low-order polynomial in slow time at every pixel independently: fine for slowly moving tissue and short ensembles where its cutoff (\(\approx (0.44 + 0.41p)\,\mathrm{PRF}/N\)) sits below the slowest flow of interest. When tissue moves at velocities overlapping blood (cardiac, breathing, handheld probe) or when slow microvascular flow must be kept, frequency separation fails; an SVD filter on a spatiotemporal Casorati matrix separates by spatial coherence (Module 06, Demené et al. 2015) and needs long ensembles (ultrafast Doppler, ULM). Follow-up: what is the regression filter's matrix? \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^H\) with \(\mathbf{Q}\) orthonormal polynomials; an SVD filter is the same with \(\mathbf{Q}\) from the data.

I9You smooth shear-wave displacement vs time with filtfilt and a 4th-order Butterworth at 1 kHz. What did you actually apply? · onsite

\(\lvert H\rvert^2\): zero phase (so arrival times are not biased, which matters for time-of-flight SWE), but −6 dB at 1 kHz, the −3 dB point at 0.896 kHz, and an 8th-order magnitude roll-off. The first and last few periods of the cutoff are affected by edge transients unless the record is reflection-padded and the filter started from steady state. Follow-up: could you do this in real time? Only block-wise with overlap and a latency of at least the block length.

I10You need envelopes from RF on an FPGA at 40 MHz. FFT Hilbert or FIR Hilbert, and how big? · research

FIR: streaming, no block edges, no circular wrap. A 31-tap Hamming-windowed type III Hilbert is within −0.19/+0.05 dB from 2 to 18 MHz; only 16 taps are nonzero and they are antisymmetric, so 8 multiplies per output. Delay the in-phase path by 15 samples to match. Better still, demodulate to IQ and decimate (Module 08), which also cuts the data rate. Follow-up: why not an even-length Hilbert? Type IV has a half-sample delay, awkward to match in the real path.

09.13Self-check

Q1A pole pair sits at radius 0.9 and angle 0.3π, \(f_s\) = 40 MHz. Roughly how wide (−3 dB) is the resonance, and how long does it ring?

\(2(1 - r) = 0.2\) rad/sample → \(0.2/(2\pi) \times 40\) MHz = 1.27 MHz. Ringing \(\approx 1/(1 - r) = 10\) samples (exactly \(-1/\ln 0.9 = 9.5\)) = 0.25 µs. At θ = 0.3π the conjugate pole is far enough that the approximation holds.

Q2Why can you not build a linear-phase high-pass with an even number of symmetric taps?

Even length means odd order M; symmetry gives \(H(-1) = \sum_n h[n](-1)^n = -H(-1)\), so \(H(-1) = 0\): a forced zero at Nyquist. Use odd length (type I) or antisymmetric taps with even length (type IV, zero at DC is fine for a high-pass).

Q3Kaiser design for 70 dB with a transition of \(0.02\,f_s\): β and M?

\(\beta = 0.1102 \times (70 - 8.7) = 6.76\). \(\Delta\omega = 2\pi \times 0.02 = 0.1257\) rad/sample, \(M = 62/(2.285 \times 0.1257) = 216\). Expect about ±0.5 dB from the formula; add 1 dB of margin if the spec is hard.

Q4Analog spec: 1 dB ripple up to \(\Omega_p\), 40 dB from \(2\Omega_p\). Butterworth and Chebyshev orders?

\(\varepsilon_p = 0.509\), \(\varepsilon_s = 99.99\), ratio 196.5. Butterworth: \(\ln 196.5/\ln 2 = 7.62 \to 8\). Chebyshev: \(\operatorname{acosh} 196.5/\operatorname{acosh} 2 = 5.97/1.317 = 4.54 \to 5\). Elliptic needs 4.

Q5You run a 2nd-order Butterworth low-pass (\(f_c\) = 1 MHz) forward and backward. Attenuation at 1 MHz? Delay?

−6 dB (\(\lvert H\rvert^2 = 0.5\) at the original −3 dB point), zero delay at all frequencies. The new −3 dB point is \((\sqrt{2} - 1)^{1/4} = 0.80\) of 1 MHz.

Q6Decimate RF from 40 to 10 MHz while keeping 0–3 MHz (after mixing to baseband) alias-free at 60 dB. Stopband edge, rough taps, cost?

\(f_{\text{stop}} = f_s/D - f_{\text{pass}}\) = 10 − 3 = 7 MHz, so \(\Delta f = 4/40 = 0.1\). Taps \(\approx 60/(22 \times 0.1) \approx 27\). Polyphase: 27/4 ≈ 7 multiplies per input sample (per real channel). Aliases landing in 3–7 MHz are removed later or ignored.

Q7Color flow with N = 12, PRF = 2 kHz, \(f_0\) = 3 MHz and a degree-1 regression wall filter. Where is the −3 dB cutoff, in Hz and cm/s?

\(0.86\,\mathrm{PRF}/N = 0.86 \times 2000/12\) = 143 Hz. \(v = c f/(2 f_0) = 1540 \times 143/(6 \times 10^6)\) = 3.7 cm/s at zero beam-to-flow angle.
