Convolution, LTI systems and filters, from superposition

Every linear step in the ultrasound chain (transducer, analog front end, RF and IQ filters, the pulse-echo model of an RF line, wall filters, persistence) is a convolution with an impulse response. This module builds that from two assumptions, linearity and time invariance, then shows why the same operation is a plain multiplication in frequency, what the real and the imaginary parts of the frequency response do to a sinusoid, and why undoing a filter is harder than applying it.

You will be able to

  • Test a system for linearity and time invariance, and name the ultrasound blocks that fail each test
  • Derive the convolution sum from superposition and compute a convolution by hand, as a table and as polynomial multiplication
  • Prove that \(e^{j\omega n}\) is an eigenfunction of every LTI system and read \(H(e^{j\omega})\) as a gain and a rotation
  • Say what \(\mathrm{Re}\,H\) and \(\mathrm{Im}\,H\) do to a cosine, and read phase delay and group delay off \(\angle H\)
  • Derive the convolution theorem and check it numerically at one frequency
  • Run difference equations by hand, read \(\lvert H\rvert\) from pole and zero distances, and state the stability condition
  • Explain why band-pass filtering an RF line helps SNR and why inverse filtering amplifies noise

In 60 seconds
  1. One impulse response describes the system: If a system is linear (scaled, summed inputs give scaled, summed outputs) and time-invariant (delayed input, delayed output), its impulse response h predicts everything: output \(y = x \ast h\), input x convolved with h. Time-varying gain and log compression break these properties, so filtering and beamforming come before them. §03.1 §03.2
  2. Filters in a chain combine into one: Convolution can be regrouped and reordered, so a pulse-echo response is the excitation, transmit and receive responses convolved together. L input samples through an M-tap filter give L + M − 1 outputs. FFT-based convolution wraps around, so pad to at least that length or the tail lands on the start. §03.3
  3. Sinusoids keep their frequency: A complex sinusoid entering a linear, time-invariant system comes out at the same frequency, only scaled and rotated. The factor is the frequency response \(H(e^{j\omega}) = \sum_k h[k]e^{-j\omega k}\), with h the impulse response and ω the frequency in radians per sample. Such a system never creates a frequency; harmonics need a nonlinearity or time variation. §03.4
  4. The phase of H is delay: A filter’s phase response shifts each frequency in time. A pulse envelope moves by the group delay \(-d\varphi/d\omega\), the slope of phase φ against frequency ω. A symmetric FIR (finite impulse response) filter of M taps delays all frequencies by (M − 1)/2 samples, which must be removed before depths are assigned. §03.5
  5. Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency: Filtering multiplies spectra frequency by frequency: \(Y = XH\), with X, H and Y the spectra of input, filter and output, so magnitudes multiply and phases add. The reverse also holds: multiplying in time smears in frequency, which is exactly what spectral leakage and apodization (tapering the aperture weights) are. §03.6
  6. Poles set stability and resonance: A recursive filter is shaped by the roots of its denominator, called poles. A causal filter is stable only if every pole lies strictly inside the unit circle |z| = 1. Poles near the circle give sharp peaks and long ringing, and turn fragile when coefficients are rounded for fixed point. §03.7
  7. Deconvolution is limited by noise: Undoing the pulse (deconvolution) divides by its spectrum P, which amplifies noise wherever P is weak. The Wiener filter \(G = P^*/(\lvert P\rvert^2 + \varepsilon)\) adds a small constant ε, with \(P^*\) the conjugate of P, that limits this gain. Smaller ε gives a shorter pulse and more noise, and nothing recovers frequencies the pulse never contained. §03.8
From the field
  • Remove every filter’s delay before assigning depth, and match it across channels. A linear-phase FIR filter of M taps delays the echo by (M − 1)/2 samples, and a mismatch between channels is a focusing error indistinguishable from tissue aberration. A 41-tap filter at 40 MHz sampling shifts depth by 0.385 mm. §03.5
  • Use one rule everywhere for which output samples you keep. An M-tap filter on L samples gives L + M − 1 outputs; mixing ‘full’, ‘same’ and causal indexing shifts echoes by up to (M − 1)/2 samples. No error is thrown; it surfaces later as an unexplained depth offset. §03.2
  • Match the receive band-pass to the pulse band. A band-pass filter gains signal-to-noise ratio by rejecting out-of-band noise; cutting into the pulse band lengthens the pulse. For a 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth pulse sampled at 40 MHz, 3–7 MHz gives +7.3 dB and 4–6 MHz only +8.5 dB, with a visibly longer pulse. §03.8
  • Simulate fixed-point IIR filters with rounded coefficients before committing hardware. Recursive (infinite impulse response, IIR) filters with poles, the denominator roots, near the unit circle are stable on paper but fragile in fixed point: rounding can push a pole outside or cause limit cycles. Cascaded second-order sections (biquads) keep each pole pair well conditioned. §03.10
  • Use filters without linear phase only where pulse shape does not matter. IIR and minimum-phase filters delay different frequencies by different amounts, so the pulse spreads. Offline, filtering forward and then backward (filtfilt) gives zero phase at the price of squaring the magnitude response. §03.5

Key takeaways
  • Linearity (\(T\{ax_1 + bx_2\} = aT\{x_1\} + bT\{x_2\}\), so zero in gives zero out) and time invariance (a delayed input only delays the output) are separate tests; one failing case is a proof, one passing case proves nothing.

  • Filter, beamform and deconvolve before the envelope, never after.

03.2The impulse response, and the convolution sum from superposition

Four small steps take you from the two definitions to the formula that describes every LTI system.

  1. Any sequence is a stack of scaled, shifted impulses, (03.3). For \(x = [1, 2, 3]\): \(x = 1\cdot\delta[n] + 2\cdot\delta[n-1] + 3\cdot\delta[n-2]\).
  2. Name the response to one impulse: \(h[n] = T\{\delta[n]\}\), the impulse response.
  3. Time invariance moves it: \(T\{\delta[n-k]\} = h[n-k]\).

Pitfall: FFT convolution is circular

Multiplying two N-point DFTs and inverting gives circular convolution (03.8): output samples past \(N - 1\) wrap to the start. Key takeaways
  • Gather view: y[n] is the inner product of x with h flipped and shifted by n; the indices in each product add up to n.
  • Convolution is commutative, associative and distributive: a cascade is one filter (pulse-echo = excitation ∗ transmit ∗ receive), and a delay is convolution with δ[n − d].
  • FFT convolution is circular: with N = 4 the by-hand result becomes [−1, 5, 7, 1]; pad to N ≥ L + M − 1.

The factor \(e^{j\omega n}\) does not depend on \(k\), so it leaves the sum. What remains is one complex number per frequency: the frequency response, the correlation of \(h\) with a probe phasor (Module 02).

"Eigenfunction" means the same thing as eigenvector of a matrix: the output is the input times a number. For a convolution that number is \(H(e^{j\omega})\), and the only things it can do to the phasor are scale it by \(\lvert H\rvert\) and rotate it by \(\angle H\). So an LTI system never creates a frequency that was not in the input. New frequencies need a nonlinearity (the squarer turns \(\cos\omega n\) into \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos 2\omega n\)) or time variation (TGC multiplies, which convolves spectra).

A real cosine is two counter-rotating phasors, \(\cos\omega n = (e^{j\omega n} + e^{-j\omega n})/2\). Each passes through, scaled by its own eigenvalue. For real \(h\), \(H(e^{-j\omega}) = H(e^{j\omega})^{*}\), the two outputs are conjugates and their sum is real:

Ultrasound link: a delay is a phase ramp

A delay \(\tau\) is the LTI system \(h(t) = \delta(t - \tau)\) with \(H(f) = e^{-j2\pi f\tau}\): unit gain, phase \(-2\pi f\tau\). Applying a beamforming delay in the frequency domain is exactly this multiplication, and the narrowband shortcut of rotating IQ data by \(-2\pi f_0\tau\) keeps only the value at \(f_0\). Aberration correction estimates the same per-channel phase ramps.

Key takeaways
  • \(e^{j\omega n} \to H(e^{j\omega})\,e^{j\omega n}\) with \(H = \sum_k h[k]e^{-j\omega k}\): the only possible effects are a gain |H| and a rotation ∠H.
  • An LTI system never creates a frequency; new frequencies need a nonlinearity (squarer: 0 and 2ω₀) or time variation (TGC).
  • A real cosine leaves as \(\lvert H\rvert\cos(\omega n + \angle H)\): h = [½, ½] at ω = π/2 gives ×0.707 and −45°, half a sample of delay.

03.10How experts think about it

An expert treats any linear, time-invariant block as one sequence \(h\), or equivalently one complex number per frequency, \(H(e^{j\omega})\). 03.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Is time-gain compensation an LTI operation? Is envelope detection? · screen

TGC, \(y[n] = g[n]\,x[n]\), is linear (scaling and adding inputs scales and adds outputs) but time-varying: an echo at a later time gets a larger gain, so delaying the input does not just delay the output. Envelope detection is time-invariant but nonlinear (the envelope of \(-x\) equals the envelope of \(x\)). Neither has a transfer function. Follow-up: "So where may you filter?" Anywhere before the envelope; and TGC commutes with an LTI filter only approximately, when \(g\) varies slowly over the filter length.

I2Convolve [1, 2, 3] with [2, 1, −1] by hand and give two checks. · screen

\([2, 5, 7, 1, -3]\). Checks: length \(3 + 3 - 1 = 5\); \(\sum y = 12 = \sum x \cdot \sum h = 6 \cdot 2\); and the alternating sum (\(\omega = \pi\)): \(2 - 5 + 7 - 1 - 3 = 0\) \(= (1 - 2 + 3)(2 - 1 - 1) = 2 \cdot 0\). Follow-up: "Do it with a 4-point FFT." You get \([-1, 5, 7, 1]\) because the −3 wraps onto \(n = 0\); pad to 5 or more.

I3Why are complex exponentials the eigenfunctions of LTI systems? · screen

\(y[n] = \sum_k h[k]\,e^{j\omega(n-k)}\) \(= e^{j\omega n}\sum_k h[k]\,e^{-j\omega k}\) \(= H(e^{j\omega})\,e^{j\omega n}\). The shift-invariance turns a delay into multiplication by \(e^{-j\omega k}\), and exponentials are the functions whose shifts are scalar multiples of themselves. Matrix view: convolution matrices are Toeplitz (circulant with padding), and the DFT vectors diagonalize every circulant. Follow-up: "Are sines eigenfunctions?" Not individually: a sine comes out as a scaled sine plus a cosine unless \(H\) is real at that \(\omega\).

I4What does the imaginary part of a frequency response mean physically? · onsite

For a cosine input, \(y = \mathrm{Re}\,H\cos\omega n - \mathrm{Im}\,H\sin\omega n\). \(\mathrm{Re}\,H\) is the gain of the in-phase copy, \(-\mathrm{Im}\,H\) the gain of a copy shifted 90° (a quarter period late if \(\mathrm{Im}\,H < 0\)). Example: \(h = [\tfrac12, \tfrac12]\) at \(\omega = \pi/2\) has \(H = 0.5 - 0.5j\), so the output is half in-phase plus half lagging quadrature, net 0.707 at −45°, a half-sample delay. \(\mathrm{Im}\,H\) comes from the odd part of \(h\) about \(n = 0\). Follow-up: "Which filter has \(\mathrm{Re}\,H = 0\) everywhere?" Any odd \(h\), e.g. the Hilbert transformer (\(-j\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\omega\)) or the centered difference (\(j\sin\omega\)).

I5Phase delay versus group delay: which one tells you where the echo envelope lands? · onsite

Group delay \(\tau_g = -d\varphi/d\omega\) at the pulse center frequency gives the envelope delay; phase delay \(-\varphi/\omega\) gives the carrier delay. For linear phase they coincide; for a 41-tap symmetric FIR at 40 MHz both are 20 samples = 0.5 µs, 0.385 mm of depth. Follow-up: "Why does this matter for beamforming?" A phase-delay mismatch between channel filters looks like a time-delay error: 10° at 5 MHz is 5.6 ns.

I6Derive the convolution theorem and say what changes for the DFT. · onsite

\(Y = \sum_n\sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]\,e^{-j\omega n}\); write \(e^{-j\omega n} = e^{-j\omega k}\,e^{-j\omega(n-k)}\) and set \(m = n - k\): \(Y = X H\). For the N-point DFT the indices are mod \(N\), so DFT products give circular convolution; linear convolution needs \(N \ge L + M - 1\). Follow-up: "And the dual?" Multiplication in time is circular convolution of the DFTs divided by \(N\): windowing smears lines (leakage).

I7A one-pole smoother has a = 0.9. Give its impulse response, DC gain, −3 dB frequency, delay and stability. · onsite

\(h[n] = 0.1 \cdot 0.9^n\), \(\sum h = 1\) (DC gain 1). \(\lvert H\rvert^2 = 0.01/(1 - 1.8\cos\omega + 0.81) = \tfrac12\) at \(\cos\omega = 1 - 0.01/1.8\), \(\omega = 0.105\) rad/sample (\(\approx 1 - a\)). Time constant \(-1/\ln 0.9 = 9.5\) samples; group delay at DC \(a/(1-a) = 9\) samples. Pole at 0.9, inside the circle: stable. Noise variance gain \(0.1/1.9 = 1/19\). Follow-up: "Frame rate 50 Hz?" Time constant 0.19 s, cutoff \(0.105 \times 50/(2\pi) = 0.84\) Hz.

I8Why can't you deconvolve an RF line by dividing spectra, and what do you do instead? · research

\(\hat S = Y/P = S + W/P\): outside the transducer band \(\lvert P\rvert\) is tiny (for a 5 MHz, 60 % pulse, −67 dB at 10 MHz), so noise is amplified enormously. Use Wiener \(G = P^{*}/(\lvert P\rvert^2 + \varepsilon)\) with \(\varepsilon\) the noise-to-signal ratio, whose gain is capped at \(1/(2\sqrt\varepsilon)\), or sparse or Bayesian inversion with priors on \(\rho\). Follow-up: "Limits?" No method restores frequencies with no signal, \(P\) must be known (it changes with depth through attenuation, so blind or depth-adaptive estimates are used), and phase errors in \(P\) bias the result.

I9Is the pulse-echo RF line really an LTI convolution of a pulse with reflectivity? · research

Only locally. Frequency-dependent attenuation low-passes the pulse progressively with depth (center frequency downshift), diffraction changes the pulse off focus, TGC adds a time-varying gain, and multiple scattering and nonlinear propagation add non-convolutional terms. Practice: assume shift invariance within short windows (a few pulse lengths), estimate a local pulse, and compensate attenuation. Follow-up: "How does this affect sound-speed estimation?" Methods that compare phase or delays between windows or channels assume the same local pulse on both sides; depth-dependent spectral change biases them unless both are measured over matched windows.

I10Can a causal filter have a purely real frequency response? · research

Real \(H\) for all \(\omega\) requires \(h\) even about \(n = 0\) (for real \(h\)); a causal \(h\) is zero for \(n < 0\), so evenness forces \(h = c\,\delta[n]\). Any useful causal filter has phase. The best you can do causally is linear phase (symmetric FIR, pure delay). Offline, filter forward and then backward: \(H H^{*} = \lvert H\rvert^2\), zero phase, magnitude squared. Follow-up: "Price of zero phase?" Doubled dB figures, non-causal transients at both ends, and it cannot run in real time.

I11An IIR filter has a pole at z = 1.02. What do you see, and how do you fix it? · onsite

The impulse response contains \(1.02^n\), doubling every \(\ln 2/\ln 1.02 = 35\) samples: the output eventually overflows whatever the input. Fix: redesign so all poles are inside the circle, or if the pole moved because of coefficient quantization, implement as cascaded biquads (second-order sections) where each pole pair depends on two coefficients. Follow-up: "Pole exactly at 1?" An integrator: a bounded input of constant sign (DC) makes the output grow linearly without bound, so it is not BIBO stable; zero-mean noise makes it wander like a random walk.

03.14Self-check

Q1Is y[n] = n·x[n] linear? Time-invariant?

Linear (a gain applied sample by sample), not time-invariant (the gain depends on \(n\)). It is a TGC with \(g[n] = n\).

Q2Compute [1, −1] ∗ [1, 1, 1, 1]. What does the result tell you about differencing a rect?

\([1, 0, 0, 0, -1]\): length \(2 + 4 - 1 = 5\). Differencing a rect leaves only its two edges, +1 at the rising edge and −1 at the falling one; \(\sum y = 0 = \sum x \cdot \sum h = 0 \cdot 4\).

Q3For h = [½, ½], find Re H and Im H at ω = π/3 and the output to cos(πn/3).

\(H = \tfrac12(1 + e^{-j\pi/3})\) \(= \tfrac12(1 + 0.5 - 0.866j)\) \(= 0.75 - 0.433j\), \(\lvert H\rvert = 0.866\), \(\angle H = -30^\circ\) (half a sample at 60° per sample). \(y = 0.75\cos(\pi n/3) + 0.433\sin(\pi n/3)\) \(= 0.866\cos(\pi n/3 - 30^\circ)\).

Q4Which of these has a purely imaginary H: (a) h = [1, −1] at n = 0, 1; (b) h[−1] = 1, h[1] = −1; (c) h = [1, 2, 1] at n = −1, 0, 1?

(b): it is odd about \(n = 0\), \(H = e^{j\omega} - e^{-j\omega} = 2j\sin\omega\). (c) is even, \(H = 2 + 2\cos\omega\), real. (a) is the causal first difference, \(2j\sin(\omega/2)\,e^{-j\omega/2}\): imaginary only after removing the half-sample delay.

Q5You convolve a 2000-sample RF line with a 49-tap filter by FFT. What is the smallest FFT length, and the smallest power of two?

\(N \ge 2000 + 49 - 1 = 2048\), which is already a power of two. One sample less and the last output would wrap onto the first.

Q6A causal filter has poles at 0.95e±j0.3π and zeros at ±1. Where is |H| largest, where is it zero, and is it stable?

Peak near \(\omega = 0.3\pi\) (pole distance about 0.05), nulls at \(\omega = 0\) and \(\omega = \pi\) (zeros on the circle at \(z = 1\) and \(z = -1\)): a band-pass resonator. Poles have radius \(0.95 < 1\): stable, ringing time about \(1/(1 - 0.95) = 20\) samples.

Q7Why does a Wiener deconvolution filter look like a matched filter at frequencies where the pulse is weak?

\(G = P^{*}/(\lvert P\rvert^2 + \varepsilon) \approx P^{*}/\varepsilon\) when \(\lvert P\rvert^2 \ll \varepsilon\). \(P^{*}\) is the matched filter, so where the data are mostly noise the Wiener filter correlates with the pulse and attenuates instead of inverting.
