Wave physics, nonlinear propagation and harmonic imaging

Every beamformer assumes a wave equation, a sound speed and a linear medium. This module derives the linear acoustic wave equation, follows the wave through interfaces, scatterers, absorbers and apertures, and then drops the linear assumption: B/A, the shock distance, Fubini's harmonics and the Burgers equation explain why tissue harmonic imaging gives narrower, cleaner beams, and how pulse inversion and amplitude modulation isolate the nonlinear echo.

You will be able to

  • Derive the linear wave equation from mass, momentum and state, and state exactly which terms linearization drops
  • Compute reflection, transmission, critical angles and shear mode conversion at tissue, fat, bone and air interfaces, and the image errors refraction causes
  • Place a scatterer in the specular, Rayleigh or stochastic regime, and connect power-law attenuation to dispersion (Kramers–Kronig) and to spectral downshift
  • Estimate focal gain, beam width and depth of focus from the aperture
  • Compute β, the shock distance, Fubini harmonic amplitudes and the Gol'dberg number for a given tissue, pressure and frequency
  • Quantify why tissue harmonic beams are narrower with lower sidelobes and less clutter, and choose between filtering, pulse inversion and amplitude modulation

15.1The linear wave equation from three conservation laws

Write density, pressure and particle velocity as a rest state plus a small acoustic perturbation: \(\rho = \rho_0 + \rho'\), \(p = p_0 + p'\), \(\mathbf{u} = 0 + \mathbf{u}\). Three physical statements, each kept to first order in the perturbation, give the wave equation.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{mass:} && \frac{\partial \rho'}{\partial t} + \rho_0 \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 && \text{(drops } \nabla \cdot (\rho' \mathbf{u})\text{)} \\ &\text{momentum:} && \rho_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} = -\nabla p' && \text{(drops } \rho'\, \partial \mathbf{u}/\partial t \text{ and } \rho_0 (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{u}\text{)} \\ &\text{state:} && p' = c_0^2 \rho', \quad c_0^2 = \left( \frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho} \right)_s = \frac{1}{\rho_0 \kappa_s} && \text{(drops the } (B/2)(\rho'/\rho_0)^2 \text{ term)} \end{aligned} \]

15.2Reflection, transmission and refraction

At a plane boundary pressure and normal particle velocity are continuous. For normal incidence of a plane wave from medium 1 onto medium 2:

\[ \begin{aligned} R &= \frac{p_r}{p_i} = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}, \qquad T = \frac{p_t}{p_i} = 1 + R = \frac{2Z_2}{Z_2 + Z_1} \\ \text{energy:}\ \ R_I &= R^2, \qquad T_I = \frac{Z_1}{Z_2}\, T^2 = \frac{4 Z_1 Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}, \qquad R_I + T_I = 1 \end{aligned} \]
(15.3)

Fat → muscle: R = 0.093 (−20.7 dB); liver → muscle: R = −0.004 (−48 dB); soft tissue → skull: R = 0.54, 29 % of the energy; soft tissue → air: R = −0.9995, so only 0.1 % (−30 dB) of the energy crosses. Soft-tissue contrast in B-mode comes from interfaces 20–50 dB below a perfect reflector and from diffuse scattering, not from large impedance jumps.

Oblique incidence. Phase matching along the boundary gives Snell's law; the fluid–fluid coefficients follow by projecting the velocity onto the normal:

Beyond \(\theta_c\), \(\cos\theta_2\) is imaginary, \(\lvert R \rvert = 1\) and the transmitted field is evanescent. Soft tissue → skull (\(c_L\) = 2820 m/s): \(\theta_{\mathrm{c,L}}\) = 33.1°. Fat → muscle: \(\theta_c\) = 66.6°.

Mode conversion. A solid also supports shear waves, and a fluid–solid boundary has three conditions (normal displacement, normal stress, zero shear stress), so an incident longitudinal wave launches a reflected wave, a transmitted longitudinal wave and a transmitted shear wave. Demo 15.2 solves that 3 × 3 system exactly. With the skull values, beyond 33° the longitudinal wave is gone and between about 37° and 73° more than 90 % of the incident energy enters the bone as shear. Because \(c_S\) (1500 m/s) is below the tissue speed, the shear wave has no critical angle and is refracted toward the normal; shear attenuation in bone is high, which is why transcranial imaging normally stays near normal incidence.

Refraction errors in imaging. The beamformer assumes straight rays at \(c_0\) = 1540 m/s. Three consequences: (1) an axial shift, because a layer of thickness d and speed \(c_L\) is displayed as \(d \cdot c_0/c_L\) (fat: +0.62 mm per cm); (2) a lateral shift, because a wedge-shaped layer bends the beam (a fat wedge of 20° in muscle deviates the beam by 1.9°, i.e. 2.0 mm at 6 cm, the mechanism behind duplicated images below the rectus muscles); (3) defocus, because the delays no longer match the true arrival times. These are the aberrations that sound-speed estimation and aberration correction try to undo.

Pitfall: dB, nepers, one-way and round trip

Attenuation coefficients are quoted in dB/cm/MHz for amplitude, one-way. Simulators often want Np/m (multiply dB/cm by 100/8.686 = 11.5). B/A measures how much the sound speed rises with pressure. It is measured from the pressure (or temperature) dependence of c, or from finite-amplitude harmonic growth. Water 5.0, soft tissue 6–8, fat about 10; a sensitive fat marker, which is why B/A imaging has been proposed for fatty liver.

Two effects make high-pressure parts of the wave faster. The local sound speed rises (the B/2A part), and the medium itself moves with the wave (convection, the "1"). A point of the waveform with particle velocity \(u\) therefore travels at

Why less clutter. What it costs. 15.9How experts think about it

An expert keeps three small parameters in view and asks which one is not small. Impedance contrast \(\Delta Z/Z\) sets what reflects and how much; \(ka\) sets how it scatters; the Mach number \(\varepsilon\), multiplied by \(kx\) and \(\beta\), sets whether the wave is still linear (\(\sigma = \beta\varepsilon k x\)). Everything else is a ratio of lengths: the path relative to \(1/\alpha\) (attenuation), to \(a^2/\lambda\) (diffraction) and to \(\bar{x}\) (nonlinearity), and \(\Gamma = 1/(\alpha\bar{x})\) says whether absorption or nonlinearity wins. Harmonic imaging is the \(p^2\) law applied to beams: anything weak gets weaker by twice as many decibels.

Mass, momentum and an equation of state, each linearized, give the wave equation; \(c^2 = 1/(\rho\kappa)\).
\(\nabla^2 p = c^{-2}\, \partial^2 p/\partial t^2\)\(p = f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)\)\(\varepsilon \approx 4 \times 10^{-4}\) at 1 MPa

Sound speed is stiffness over inertia: water's adiabatic compressibility \(4.5 \times 10^{-10}\) Pa⁻¹ and density 998 kg/m³ give 1490 m/s. The neglected terms are of relative order \(\varepsilon = u_0/c_0 = p_0/(\rho_0 c_0^2)\), \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) at 1 MPa in tissue.

15.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Derive the linear acoustic wave equation. Which terms does linearization drop, and how big are they? · screen

Linearized mass (\(\partial\rho'/\partial t + \rho_0 \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0\)), momentum (\(\rho_0\, \partial\mathbf{u}/\partial t = -\nabla p\)) and state (\(p = c_0^2 \rho'\)); take \(\partial/\partial t\) of the first, the divergence of the second and eliminate: \(\nabla^2 p = c_0^{-2} \partial^2 p/\partial t^2\). Dropped: \(\nabla \cdot (\rho' \mathbf{u})\), \(\rho_0 (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{u}\), \(\rho'\, \partial \mathbf{u}/\partial t\) and \((B/2) s^2\), all of relative order \(\varepsilon = p_0/(\rho_0 c_0^2)\), about \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) at 1 MPa. Follow-up: "Then why do harmonics matter?" Because the error accumulates with distance: the relevant parameter is \(\sigma = \beta\varepsilon k x\), which reaches 0.5 in 2–3 cm at diagnostic pressures.

I2Why is soft-tissue contrast weak in B-mode, and what amplitude does a fat–muscle boundary return? · screen

Soft-tissue impedances are all within about 10 % of 1.6 MRayl. Fat → muscle (1.38 → 1.66 MRayl): R = 0.093, −20.7 dB; liver → muscle about −48 dB. Most of the image is diffuse scattering from sub-resolution structure, 40–60 dB below a specular reflector, displayed after log compression. Follow-up: "And air?" R = −0.9995, the polarity inverts and 0.1 % of the energy crosses: gel, and acoustic shadows behind gas.

I3A beam hits the skull at 45°. What happens to its energy? · onsite

The longitudinal critical angle is \(\arcsin(1540/2820)\) = 33.1°, so no longitudinal wave enters. The boundary still transmits by mode conversion: solving the three boundary conditions gives about 93 % of the energy as a shear wave and 7 % reflected (\(c_S\) = 1500 m/s, ρ = 1900 kg/m³). The shear wave is refracted toward the normal (\(c_S < c_1\)) and attenuates strongly in bone; at the inner table it converts back to longitudinal. Follow-up: "Why do transcranial studies avoid this regime?" Shear attenuation and the extra mode conversions; normal incidence keeps 71 % in the longitudinal wave.

I4How does a subcutaneous fat layer bias an image and a sound-speed estimate, and how would you correct it? · research

Axially, \(d(c_0/c_f - 1)\): 2 cm of fat at 1450 m/s puts everything 1.24 mm deeper. Laterally, oblique fat–muscle boundaries refract (a 20° wedge deviates 1.9°, 2 mm at 6 cm) and duplicate structures. Across the aperture, path lengths in fat differ, so delays are wrong and the focus loses coherence. A global sound-speed estimator returns a path-averaged speed biased by the layer; local estimators (CUTE-style phase-shift tomography, layered inversion) model the layer explicitly, and aberration correction applies the residual delays. Follow-up: "What does the FDTD/k-Wave check add?" Reflections and mode conversion that ray models miss.

I5Rayleigh scattering goes as \(f^4\). Why are measured backscatter exponents in tissue usually below 4? · onsite

\(f^4\) holds for \(ka \ll 1\). Real scatterers have finite size and correlated structure; the form factor rolls the BSC off. With the Gaussian form factor \(\exp(-0.827 k^2 a_{\mathrm{eff}}^2)\) the local exponent is \(4 - 1.654 (k a_{\mathrm{eff}})^2\), equal to 2 at \(ka = 1.10\) (54 µm at 5 MHz). Fitting that exponent is how QUS estimates effective scatterer size (Module 27). Follow-up: "What else biases the slope?" Uncorrected attenuation and diffraction, and gating, which is why reference-phantom normalization is used.

I6Why must frequency-dependent attenuation come with dispersion? Estimate it for liver. · research

Causality makes the complex wavenumber analytic in the upper half-plane, so its real (phase) and imaginary (loss) parts are a Kramers–Kronig pair. For \(\alpha = \alpha_1 \lvert \omega \rvert\) the nearly local result is \(\Delta(1/c) = -(2/\pi)\alpha_1 \ln(\omega/\omega_0)\). With 0.5 dB/cm/MHz, c rises 1.38 m/s per neper of ln f, 3.2 m/s from 1 to 10 MHz (3.6 m/s for y = 1.1). Follow-up: "Where does this matter?" Comparing sound speeds measured at different frequencies, and time-domain simulators (fractional Laplacian terms in k-Wave) that must include it to be causal.

I7Define B/A and β, and compute the shock distance in liver at 2 MHz, 1 MPa. · onsite

\(B/A = 2\rho_0 c_0 (\partial c/\partial p)_s\), the second-order coefficient of the equation of state relative to the first; \(\beta = 1 + B/2A\) adds convection. Liver: B/A ≈ 7, β = 4.5, ρ = 1060 kg/m³, c = 1578 m/s: \(\bar{x} = \rho c^3/(\beta\omega p_0)\) \(= 1060 \times 1578^3/(4.5 \times 2\pi \times 2 \times 10^6 \times 10^6)\) = 73.7 mm (29.5 mm at 5 MHz). Follow-up: "Will it shock?" No: α at 2 MHz ≈ 12.4 Np/m gives \(\Gamma \approx 1.1\), so absorption balances nonlinearity and the harmonic saturates near \(\sigma \approx 0.5\).

I8Why is the tissue-harmonic beam narrower, with lower sidelobes? Give numbers. · onsite

The harmonic source density is \(\propto p_1^2\), so the transmit profile is squared: for a line aperture the −6 dB width goes from 1.207 to 0.886 \(\lambda_0 F/D\) and the first sidelobe from −13.3 to −26.5 dB. Received at \(2f_0\) the two-way pattern is \(\operatorname{sinc}^2(u) \operatorname{sinc}(2u)\): 0.511 \(\lambda_0 F/D\) (0.58 × the fundamental two-way) with −27.9 dB first sidelobe. Grating lobes of the \(2f_0\) receive pattern are suppressed by the squared transmit. Follow-up: "Why not just transmit at \(2f_0\)?" Attenuation doubles in dB on the way down, and the \(f_0\)-designed array would have transmit grating lobes at \(2f_0\).

I9Why does THI reduce clutter in difficult patients? · screen

\(p^2\) dependence: clutter at −20 dB (reverberation, sidelobes, off-axis scatter) produces harmonic at −40 dB. The harmonic is absent at the skin and builds up with depth, so near-field reverberations in the body wall contribute little, and the harmonic is generated after part of the aberrating path. Follow-up: "Cost?" 15–25 dB less signal, twice the receive attenuation in dB, narrower usable bandwidth with filtering, or half the frame rate with PI.

I10Compare pulse inversion and amplitude modulation. What limits each in vivo? · onsite

With \(e(a) = \sum a^k e_k\): PI, \(e(a) + e(-a)\), keeps even orders (+6 dB on \(2f_0\)) and removes the fundamental at full bandwidth; AM, \(e(a) - 2e(a/2)\), removes the linear term but keeps ½ of the quadratic and ¾ of the cubic terms, including the nonlinear fundamental, useful for bubbles. Both need two transmits (half frame rate) and identical pulses; motion leaves \(2 \lvert \sin(\pi f_0 \delta) \rvert\): −26 dB at 10 mm/s, 5 kHz, 3 MHz. Follow-up: "How do you reduce the motion residual?" Higher PRF, motion compensation, or three-pulse sequences that cancel the linear residual to higher order.

I11How would you simulate harmonic imaging for a new probe, and what would you check first? · research

Linear codes (Field II) cannot make harmonics. For a homogeneous focused beam, a KZK solver (axisymmetric or 3D) with tissue power-law absorption and B/A; for heterogeneous media and aberration, a nonlinear full-wave code (k-Wave with β and power-law absorption). First checks: plane-wave limit against Fubini (\(\sigma \le 1\)) and the Gol'dberg behaviour, the on-axis fundamental against O'Neil, grid convergence with at least the third or fourth harmonic resolved (PPW counted at the highest harmonic). Then compare the simulated \(2f_0\) focal width with the \(p^2\) prediction. Follow-up: "Measurement?" Hydrophone with bandwidth to 3–5 \(f_0\) (Module 21).
