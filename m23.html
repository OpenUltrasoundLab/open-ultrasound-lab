Beam patterns, k-space and compounding

Module 04 established that the beam is the Fourier transform of the aperture. This module makes that statement quantitative where it gets hard: at low Fresnel number and low F-number, with broadband pulses, sparse apertures and transmit ≠ receive, and finally for coherent plane-wave compounding, where the transmit focus is synthesized in k-space one angle at a time. Module 19 covers the hardware side of the same patterns (element directivity, element factor × array factor, grating-lobe pitch rules, elevation, 2D, sparse, micro-beamformed and row-column layouts); this module uses those results and treats the beam as the imaging operator they produce.

You will be able to

  • Say when the focal plane is a Fourier plane, where the intensity peak really is, and how wrong the Fourier picture is at F# 1.5
  • Choose transmit and receive apodizations by PSL, ISLR and their separate SNR and energy prices
  • Predict broadband grating-lobe and far-sidelobe levels from the pulse length and element count
  • Read any transmit/receive scheme (focused, sparse, vernier, synthetic aperture, plane waves) as a coarray and predict its two-way PSF
  • Place grating lobes for any pitch, steering and bandwidth, separately on transmit and receive, and tell peak from energy patterns
  • Derive the plane-wave angle span and step rules, and budget angles against SNR, frame rate and motion
  • Predict the steered-plane-wave transmit grating lobe and the lateral resolution of compounding from span and receive F#

In 60 seconds
  1. Where focusing works: focusing works only within the depth where the unfocused aperture still converges, \(D^2/(4\lambda)\) for aperture width D and wavelength λ: 299 mm for 19.2 mm at 5 MHz, so focusing deeper buys nothing. Focused near that limit, the intensity peak sits well short of the set focus, and below F-number 1.5 Fourier sidelobe models fail. §23.1
  2. Apodization trades sidelobes for signal: tapering element weights lowers sidelobes but costs signal. Peak sidelobe level predicts one bright point's ghost; the integrated sidelobe ratio (ISLR, all sidelobe energy over the main lobe) predicts the clutter filling a cyst. Hann costs 1.76 dB of signal-to-noise ratio on receive but 6.0 dB of focal amplitude on transmit: apodize on receive first. §23.2
  3. Broadband grating lobes hit speckle harder than points: with short pulses, element echoes at a grating-lobe angle arrive a period apart and only a few overlap, so a bright point's grating lobe is weak, falling with element count and bandwidth. Speckle sees total energy: −20.3 dB against −40.1 dB peak for 128 elements, 1.5 λ pitch, 75 % bandwidth. §23.3
  4. The coarray sets the two-way beam: the two-way lateral response is the Fourier transform of the coarray, the transmit aperture convolved with the receive aperture. Its extent sets resolution and its gaps make grating lobes. Every second element on both sides leaves gaps; transmitting on every p-th and receiving on every (p − 1)-th element fills them, removing the two-way grating lobe. §23.4
  5. Plane-wave compounding synthesizes the transmit focus: summing plane waves at several angles builds a transmit focus afterwards. The span sets the transmit F-number, \(F\#_{tx}=1/(2\tan\theta_{\max})\) with \(\theta_{\max}\) the largest angle: 1.5 for ±18.4°. The angle step only places the transmit grating lobe, so extra angles inside the span lower clutter without sharpening the beam. §23.5
  6. Plane-wave transmit and receive lobes land apart: one plane wave has no transmit focus, so the receive beam sets lateral resolution. A steered plane wave launches a second, grating-lobe wave once the pitch exceeds \(\lambda/(1+|\sin\theta_{tx}|)\), \(\theta_{tx}\) the steering angle; it arrives late as a near-field streak below targets. Because the lobes fall apart, their product suppresses each. §23.6
  7. Every angle has a price: each extra angle adds signal against electronic noise but lengthens the sequence, so frame rate falls and motion matters more. Compounding N angles gains \(10\log_{10}N\) dB of signal-to-noise ratio but divides frame rate by N, and axial motion of a quarter wavelength over the sequence loses 3.9 dB. Choose the angle count from the expected motion. §23.7
From the field
  • Set angle span for focus, angle step for replicas, then budget frame rate and motion. The span sets the transmit focus; the step only moves replicas, so extra angles buy clutter suppression, not resolution. At 40 mm depth, 31 angles give +14.9 dB at 621 Hz frame rate, but quarter-wavelength axial motion loses 3.9 dB. §23.7
  • Limit plane-wave steering by the pitch; check image edges first. A steered plane wave launches a second wave once element pitch exceeds \(\lambda/(1+|\sin\theta|)\) for steering angle θ; a 0.3 mm array at 5 MHz and 15° shows it as a −24.3 dB near-field streak. Receive lobes appear first at the truncated, steered image edges. §23.6
  • Judge acquisition changes by their effect on the coarray. The two-way point-spread function is the Fourier transform of the coarray, the transmit aperture convolved with the receive aperture: gaps are grating lobes, lost extent is lost resolution. Schemes with the same coarray give the same narrowband response however many firings they use. §23.4
  • Do not expect coherent compounding to smooth speckle. The \(10\log_{10}N\) gain for N angles is against electronic noise; adding angles within a fixed span lowers clutter but leaves speckle signal-to-noise ratio (mean over standard deviation) at about 1.8–1.9 for every N. Speckle reduction needs incoherent looks, summed after envelope detection. §23.5
  • Judge apodization for cysts by integrated, not peak, sidelobe level. Diffuse clutter integrates all sidelobes, so a window with a low peak but slowly decaying far sidelobes fills cysts more: Hamming peaks at −42.7 dB but integrates to only −34.4 dB. Apodize on receive first, where Hann costs 1.76 dB, not 6.0 dB. §23.2

23.1From Fresnel to Fraunhofer: where the focus is a Fourier plane

Module 04 used the paraxial integral to show that a focusing law cancels the quadratic phase, so the focal plane is a Fraunhofer plane at any depth. Three questions remain open: how far an unfocused aperture keeps converging, where a focused beam actually peaks, and how accurate the Fourier picture is at the F-numbers used in imaging. To answer them without the paraxial approximation, sum point sources directly. For a line of point sources across \(\xi \in [-D/2, D/2]\) with apodization \(a(\xi)\) and focus \(F\):

Transmit versus receive apodization. The two are paid for in different currencies. On receive, the price is SNR: \(G_{\mathrm{SNR}} = -1.76\) dB for Hann. On transmit, the peak drive per element is fixed by the pulser and by the mechanical index, so weights below 1 simply radiate less: the focal amplitude scales with \(\sum w/N\) (Hann: −6.0 dB) and the radiated energy with \(\sum w^2/N\) (Hann: −4.3 dB). This is why systems that are SNR-limited apodize on receive first.

23.3Grating lobes and broadband beams: where the copies land, peak versus energy, edge waves

Where the copies land. Sampling the aperture at pitch d multiplies it by a comb, so its far-field (or focal-plane) pattern is periodic in \(u = \sin\theta\) with period \(\lambda/d\). For an array steered to \(\theta_0\) by true time delays, with element factor \(E\):

Intuition

Two-way is a product, so a grating lobe survives only where transmit and receive both have one. Every way of beating grating lobes with a coarse pitch exploits this: different steering on transmit and receive (plane-wave transmit, parallel receive beams), different effective pitches (vernier, Section 23.4), the element factor (common to both, so it is squared), and bandwidth (the copies move with frequency, the main lobe does not). Consider three directions (Demo 23.4 draws each case from (23.8)):

  1. Main lobe, \(u = 0\). \(\tau_1 = 0\): every element pulse arrives at the same time, they stack, and the sum peaks at \(\sum c_n\) (N for uniform weights). Pulse length does not matter.
  2. Grating lobe, \(u = \lambda_0/d\). \(\tau_1 = T_0\) exactly: each pulse is one period later than its neighbor. They are in phase (CW sees a full lobe), but they no longer coincide: the sum is a train of N pulses, \(N T_0\) long, and at any instant only the \(\approx M\) pulses within one pulse length overlap. The peak is \(\approx M\) instead of N.
  3. A sidelobe angle, \(0 < \tau_1 < T_0\). The pulses overlap partially with phases spread over \(2\pi N\tau_1/T_0\); neighbors cancel, and only the uncancelled ends (the edge waves below) survive once the spread \(N\tau_1\) exceeds the pulse length.

23.4The coarray: two-way k-space is the convolution of the apertures

Collecting pairs with the same sum gives the coarray \(c = a * b\) (a convolution), so \(P_2 = \mathcal{F}\{a * b\}\). Walker & Trahey (1998) develop this k-space view for pulse-echo; m23_kspace_coarray.m checks the identity to \(2\times 10^{-15}\).

Everything about the two-way lateral response can be read off the coarray:

  • Extent \(L_T + L_R\) sets the resolution. Equal apertures give a triangle, hence \(\mathrm{sinc}^2\).
  • Gaps are grating lobes. Transmit and receive both on every 2nd element leave every other lag empty; the coarray is periodic with 2d and the PSF repeats at \(\lambda z/(2d)\).
  • Vernier pairs (Lockwood et al., 1996): transmit every p-th, receive every (p−1)-th element. Each factor has grating lobes, but each lands near the other's nulls, in its low-sidelobe region; the cleaner statement is that the coarray has no periodic gaps (only a single missing lag next to each end, which makes no grating lobe).
  • Synthetic aperture: firing each element alone and summing all firings gives the coarray of the full aperture with itself, the same triangle as a transmit focus, at every pixel. One firing alone (or one plane wave) gives only the receive aperture: receive-only resolution.
  • Random thinning keeps the extent but replaces grating lobes with a floor. For K of N positions drawn at random, the mean one-way sidelobe power relative to the peak is \(\frac{N-K}{K(N-1)} \approx 1/K\), and the peak sidelobe sits about 7 dB above that mean (N = 64). Module 19 (Section 19.4) applies the same floor to sparse 2D layouts.

Plane-wave imaging separates the two beams that a focused system multiplies at one depth. No transmit focus. What the second wave does in the image follows from where and when it arrives:

  • Where. It occupies its own strip, leaving the aperture at \(\theta_g\).   • When. Along that ray it arrives late by \(\Delta = z\,[1 - \cos(\theta_{\mathrm{tx}} - \theta_g)]/\cos\theta_g\) of path (13.6 mm at z = 15 mm in the 5 MHz case, 7.8 mm at 30 mm for 7.5 MHz), and it is a pulse train: successive elements arrive one period apart, case (b) of Section 23.3. Receive F-number. A dynamic receive aperture \(L_{\mathrm{rx}}(z) = z/F_\#\) keeps the lateral width \(\approx 1.2\,\lambda F_\#\) constant with depth until the aperture saturates at \(z = L\cdot F_\#\) (57.6 mm for L = 38.4 mm, F# 1.5); beyond that the width grows ∝ z. The F# cannot be lowered freely, because each element only accepts echoes within its directivity. Module 19 derives the element directivity \(E(\theta) = \mathrm{sinc}(w\sin\theta/\lambda)\) (rigid baffle; times \(\cos\theta\) for a soft baffle), (19.1), and the resulting floor \(F_{\#,\min} \approx 1/(2\tan\alpha)\) with \(\lvert E(\alpha)\rvert = 0.5\), (19.2). For the reference array (5 MHz, element width w = 0.27 mm) the −6 dB acceptance half-angle is \(\alpha\) = 43.5° in a rigid baffle (\(F_{\#,\min}\) = 0.53) and 36.0° in a soft baffle (0.69); at 7.5 MHz the floor is 0.97–1.05. Elements beyond \(\alpha\) add more noise than signal; Perrot et al. (2021) discuss this choice for DAS.

Diverging waves. A phased array is too narrow for plane waves to cover a sector. Instead each transmit uses a circular wavefront from a virtual source behind the array; the transmit delay to a pixel is its distance to the virtual source minus a constant, and tilting the virtual source position steers the wave. Compounding over virtual sources synthesizes the transmit aperture exactly as plane waves do, with the angle span now measured as seen from each pixel. Papadacci et al. (2014) used this for ultrafast cardiac imaging.

Key takeaways
  • Compounding N angles gains 10 log10 N in SNR against electronic noise (14.9 dB for 31) and divides the frame rate by N (19.25 kHz PRF at 40 mm → 621 Hz for 31 angles).
  • Axial motion over the whole sequence costs −0.9 dB at λ/8 and −3.9 dB at λ/4, with a null at λ/2.
  • A constant receive F# holds the width until the aperture fills the array at z = L·F# (57.6 mm for 38.4 mm, F# 1.5); element directivity limits F#min to 0.53–0.69 for 0.27 mm elements at 5 MHz.

23.9How experts think about it

An array expert reduces every transmit/receive scheme to two objects: the coarray, which says which lateral spatial frequencies the scheme measures and with what weight, and the pulse, which sets how coherently those measurements add away from the main lobe. Focused beams, synthetic aperture, sparse arrays and plane-wave compounding are then just different ways of filling the same k-space, priced in firings, SNR, frame rate and motion sensitivity. The Fourier picture is the default model, and the expert knows its error bars: low Fresnel number, low F#, broadband pulses, and the 2D structure of the PSF.

23.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Why can't you focus a 19.2 mm aperture at 400 mm at 5 MHz? · screen

The near-field length \(D^2/(4\lambda)\) is 299 mm, so 400 mm is beyond the last axial maximum: the Fresnel number \(N_F = D^2/(4\lambda F) = 0.75\) is below 1, there is less than one Fresnel zone of phase to cancel and the focusing law changes almost nothing. The intensity peak would sit well short of 400 mm (peak/F ≈ 0.6 already at \(N_F = 1\)). Fix: a larger aperture or a higher frequency (\(N_F \propto D^2 f\)). Follow-up: "What is the far field then?" \(D^2/\lambda\) = 1.2 m or \(2D^2/\lambda\) = 2.4 m, depending on the convention; say which.

I2Your paraxial simulation at F# 1.2 shows first sidelobes at −13.3 dB; a full field simulation shows −12.5 dB. Which is wrong? · onsite

Neither is buggy; the paraxial model is the approximation. The Fourier relation drops non-paraxial path terms and the 1/r amplitude variation across a wide aperture; at F# 1.56 the direct point-source sum already gives −12.7 dB and a 3.5 % wider main lobe, and the error grows as F# drops. For Hann the gap is larger (−28.8 vs −31.5 dB), because the error is a small additive field that matters more under low sidelobes. Follow-up: "Would element directivity change it?" Yes, it acts as an extra taper and usually lowers the far sidelobes; include it in the reference model.

I3You want lower sidelobes. Do you apodize on transmit or on receive, and what does each cost? · onsite

The two-way pattern is the same either way (product of factors), but the price differs. Receive apodization costs SNR against channel noise: \((\sum w)^2/(N\sum w^2)\), −1.76 dB for Hann. Transmit apodization at fixed peak drive costs radiated field: focal amplitude \(\sum w/N\), −6.0 dB for Hann, energy −4.3 dB. If SNR-limited, apodize on receive (or not at all); if MI-limited at shallow depth with SNR to spare, transmit apodization is acceptable. Follow-up: "What do you report for clutter?" ISLR or 2D integrated clutter and cyst gCNR, not just PSL.

I4A 128-element array at 7.5 MHz has pitch 1.46λ. The CW pattern has a 0 dB grating lobe at 43°. Why is the artifact in the image so faint? · onsite

Three factors. (1) Broadband: at the lobe the element pulses arrive one period apart and form a train; only \(\approx M\) of N overlap, so the peak is \(\approx 1.064\,M/N\): with 60 % band (M = 1.47) that is −38 dB one-way and about the same two-way. (2) Element factor: a 0.9d-wide element (w = 1.31λ) is about −19 dB at 43° one-way, −38 dB two-way. (3) The lobe must hit a strong reflector to show. Follow-up: "Energy pattern?" \(1.505\,M/N\), about −17.6 dB (power): hydrophone energy plots look much worse than the image.

I5Explain the coarray and use it to design a sparse array with fewer channels but no grating lobes. · research

Each Tx/Rx pair measures spatial frequency \((\xi_t + \xi_r)/(\lambda z)\); the two-way PSF is the Fourier transform of the coarray \(a * b\). Design goal: a filled, smooth coarray. Example: vernier, Tx every 3rd and Rx every 2nd element of 64: 22 + 32 elements, coarray without periodic gaps (only the lag next to each end is missing), two-way PSL −26.5 dB like the full aperture, whereas Tx = Rx every 2nd has a 0 dB lobe at \(\lambda z/(2d)\). Alternatives: random thinning (floor \(\approx 1/K\), peak ≈ 7 dB higher), or synthetic aperture over sub-apertures. Follow-up: "Cost?" SNR (fewer elements), and the vernier null alignment is exact only at \(f_0\).

I6What does a single plane wave give you laterally, and what exactly does compounding add? · screen

One plane wave is one virtual transmit element: the coarray is the receive aperture, so the lateral width is receive-only, \(1.21\,\lambda F_\#\) (0.56 mm at 5 MHz, F# 1.5), and the 2D PSF has X-shaped edge arms (PSL ≈ −17 dB). Compounding N angles builds a virtual transmit array: span → transmit \(F_\# = 1/(2\tan\theta_{\max})\) and narrower main lobe; step → replica position \(\lambda/\Delta(\sin\theta)\); count → clutter and SNR (\(10\log_{10} N\)). Follow-up: "Does more angles in the same span improve resolution?" No; it lowers clutter and moves replicas out.

I7Derive the plane-wave angle-step condition. Why is Δθ ≤ λ/L conservative? · onsite

The transmit factor \(T_N(x) = \frac{1}{N}\sum e^{j2\pi x\sin\theta_n/\lambda}\) is periodic in x with period \(\lambda/\Delta(\sin\theta)\), independent of depth. Requiring the replica of any target to fall outside an image of width W gives \(\Delta\theta \approx \Delta(\sin\theta) \le \lambda/W\); with W = L = 38.4 mm at 5 MHz, 0.46°, and with span \(1/F_\#\) the count \(N \approx L/(\lambda F_\#) = 83\) (Montaldo et al., 2009). Conservative because the ghost is also multiplied by the receive pattern at \(x_{\mathrm{rep}}\) and by the broadband factor \(\approx 1.06\,M/N\): 11 angles at 3° (replica 5.95 mm) leave a −53 dB ghost in simulation. Follow-up: "When is it not conservative?" Very bright specular targets and narrowband pulses.

I8With 11 angles at 3° you see a faint copy of a bright wire 6 mm to its side, at every depth. Diagnose and fix. · onsite

A transmit replica: \(\lambda/\Delta(\sin\theta) = 0.308/0.0523\) = 5.9 mm at 5 MHz, and its depth independence identifies it (a focused-array grating lobe would scale with z, a receive grating lobe would follow \(\lambda z/d\)). Fixes: a smaller step (more angles in the same span), receive apodization or a lower receive F# so that \(R(x_{\mathrm{rep}})\) is lower there (a higher F# makes it worse), or a nonuniform angle set that spreads the replica into a floor. Follow-up: "Cost of halving the step at fixed span?" Twice the angles: half the frame rate, +3 dB SNR, and a sequence twice as long, so twice the displacement for the same velocity.

I9Compounding 31 angles: what happens to SNR, cyst contrast and speckle, and why? · screen

SNR against electronic noise: \(+10\log_{10} 31\) = 14.9 dB (coherent signal, incoherent noise; simulated 14.86 dB). Cyst contrast: much better (−15.8 → −37.7 dB in the simulation, gCNR 0.87 → 0.995) because the synthesized transmit focus removes the edge arms and diffuse sidelobe energy. Speckle: unchanged statistics, \(\mathrm{SNR}_0 \approx\) 1.8–1.9, because coherent summation only changes the PSF; speckle reduction needs incoherent compounding of decorrelated looks. Follow-up: "Why does CNR improve only from 1.53 to 1.78?" CNR is bounded by background speckle variance (≈ 1.91 for a perfectly black cyst on the envelope); gCNR saturates near 1.

I10In cardiac plane-wave imaging the compounded image loses signal on the moving wall. Quantify and propose mitigations. · research

Axial motion \(\delta\) per firing gives a phase ramp \(\varphi \approx 4\pi\delta/\lambda\) per firing; at the true position the loss is \(\lvert\sin(N\varphi/2)/(N\sin(\varphi/2))\rvert\): λ/4 over the sequence costs 3.9 dB, λ/2 nulls it. At 5 MHz, 31 angles at 10 kHz, λ/4 is 25 mm/s. With sequential angle order part of the ramp turns into a lateral mislocation (0.12 mm, −2.2 dB peak loss in simulation). Mitigations: fewer angles or a higher PRF (shorter sequence), lower frequency, motion estimation and phase compensation per angle before summation, or diverging waves with few virtual sources for the heart. Follow-up: "Why does angle order matter?" A ramp linear in \(\sin\theta\) is equivalent to a lateral shift of the transmit factor.

I11Why use diverging waves on a phased array, and what changes in the k-space picture? · research

A phased array is narrow (≈ 20 mm), so a plane wave insonifies only a strip as wide as the probe, not a sector. A diverging wave from a virtual source behind the array covers the sector. Transmit delay to a pixel = distance to the virtual source (minus a constant); moving the virtual source tilts the wave. Each virtual source contributes the transmit direction from source to pixel, so the synthetic transmit aperture is the set of those directions as seen from the pixel, and its span shrinks with depth, unlike a plane-wave set. The same coarray reasoning applies: span sets \(F_{\#,\mathrm{tx}}\), spacing sets replicas, SNR is lower than plane waves because the energy spreads over the sector. Follow-up: Papadacci et al. (2014) for cardiac use.

I12Your 0.3 mm linear array shows a comet-tail streak under a wire only when you steer plane waves to 15° at 7.5 MHz. Explain and quantify. · onsite

A transmit grating lobe of the steered plane wave. At 7.5 MHz, \(d = 1.46\lambda\), above the threshold \(\lambda/(1+\sin 15^\circ) = 0.794\lambda\); the second wave leaves at \(\sin\theta_g = 0.259 - 0.685\), −25.2°. It reaches the wire late (7.8 mm of path along its ray at 30 mm) and as a pulse train, so DAS images it as a streak below the wire, about −23 dB for a single angle with 60 % band. Not \(M/N\): in the near field only about one Fresnel zone of elements builds the main arrival. Fixes: steer less, lower the transmit frequency, compound more angles (−35 dB with 11), finer pitch. Follow-up: "Why not at 5 MHz?" The lobe goes to −50° and its ray misses a 30 mm target (it comes from 36 mm off axis); it shows at 15 mm depth instead.

I13Derive the lateral resolution of coherent plane-wave compounding in terms of the angle span and the receive F-number. · research

Each angle contributes \(e^{j2\pi x\sin\theta/\lambda}\), so dense angles fill transmit k-space uniformly on \(\pm\sin\theta_{\max}/\lambda\); the receive aperture fills \(\pm 1/(2F_\#\lambda)\). The PSF is the product of the two sincs, support the trapezoid convolution: \(w_{-6} \approx \kappa\lambda/(\sin\theta_{\max} + 1/(2F_\#))\), κ from 0.603 (one support dominates, e.g. one angle: \(1.207\lambda F_\#\)) to 0.886 (equal supports, focused two-way). 5 MHz, F# 1.5, ±15°: 0.456 mm. Follow-up: "And the number of angles?" It sets the replica position \(\lambda/\Delta(\sin\theta)\), not the width; three angles over ±15° even give a narrower fringe (0.42 mm) with a replica shoulder.

I14Explain the plane-wave angle-step rule as a grating-lobe condition. · screen

Angle \(\theta_n\) acts as a virtual element at \(\xi = z\sin\theta_n\), so a uniform angle set is an array of pitch \(d_v = z\Delta(\sin\theta)\). Its grating lobe sits at \(\lambda z/d_v = \lambda/\Delta(\sin\theta)\), independent of depth. If \(d_v \le z/F_\#\) the coarray is gap-free and the replica falls beyond the first receive null, under receive sidelobes; \(\Delta\theta \le \lambda/W\) moves it out of the image (0.46° for 38.4 mm at 5 MHz). Follow-up: "Diagnose a ghost 6 mm to the side at every depth with 3° steps." \(0.308/0.0523 = 5.9\) mm: the angle-domain replica.

23.13Self-check

Q1A 38.4 mm aperture at 5 MHz: what are the near-field length and the antenna far-field distance? Is unfocused imaging at 40 mm "far field"?

λ = 0.308 mm. \(D^2/(4\lambda)\) = 1.20 m; \(2D^2/\lambda\) = 9.57 m (\(D^2/\lambda\) = 4.79 m). At 40 mm the aperture is deep in its near field: \(N_F = D^2/(4\lambda z) = 30\). A plane wave's transmit profile there is the footprint, not a beam.

Q2A 2.5 MHz phased array with D = 20 mm is focused at 100 mm. Where is the intensity peak, roughly?

λ = 0.616 mm, \(N_F = 400/(4 \times 0.616 \times 100) = 1.62\). From the table, peak/F is 0.735 at \(N_F = 1.5\) and 0.815 at 2.0, so about 0.75F ≈ 75 mm, with a depth of field well below \(7\lambda F_\#^2\).

Q3You switch receive apodization from rect to Hann on an SNR-limited deep scan. What do you gain and lose?

Gain: one-way PSL −13.3 → −31.5 dB, ISLR −9.7 → −32.9 dB on the receive factor, so less clutter in cysts. Lose: −1.76 dB SNR against channel noise and a 1.66× wider receive main lobe (1.21 → 2.00 λ/D at −6 dB). If the image is noise-limited rather than clutter-limited, the trade is negative.

Q4192 elements, 7.5 MHz, 70 % bandwidth. Estimate the peak level of a grating lobe in the two-way image.

\(M = 0.883/0.70 = 1.26\) cycles. Peak \(\approx 1.064\,M/N = 1.064 \times 1.26/192 = 0.0070\), i.e. −43.1 dB relative to the main lobe (array factor only; the element factor lowers it further).

Q521 plane waves over ±10° at 5 MHz. What are the transmit F#, the replica distance and the SNR gain?

\(F_{\#,\mathrm{tx}} = 1/(2\tan 10^\circ) = 2.84\). Step 1°, \(\Delta(\sin\theta) = 2\sin 10^\circ/20 = 0.0174\), \(x_{\mathrm{rep}} = 0.308/0.0174\) = 17.7 mm. SNR gain \(10\log_{10} 21\) = 13.2 dB. With \(F_{\#,\mathrm{rx}} = 1.5\) the lateral width sits closer to receive-only than with ±15°.

Q6Imaging to 50 mm with 15 angles: maximum compounded frame rate?

\(\mathrm{PRF} \le c/(2z_{\max}) = 1540/(2 \times 0.05)\) = 15.4 kHz; \(f_{\text{frame}}\) = 15.4 kHz/15 = 1.03 kHz, before any reverberation margin.

Q7Why does a vernier pair (Tx every 3rd, Rx every 2nd element) have no two-way grating lobe although both one-way patterns do?

Its coarray has no periodic gaps: sums of multiples of 3d and 2d reach every lag except the one next to each end (Figure 23.B), and two isolated holes make no grating lobe. In the pattern picture, the transmit grating lobes (period \(\lambda z/(3d)\)) land near receive nulls, where the receive pattern is low, and the receive lobes (\(\lambda z/(2d)\)) near transmit nulls. The narrowband two-way PSL is −26.5 dB, the same as the full aperture, with 22 + 32 elements.

Q8Up to what steering angle is a 0.25 mm pitch free of transmit grating lobes at 5 MHz and at 7.5 MHz?

\(d/\lambda\) = 0.25/0.308 = 0.812 and 0.25/0.205 = 1.218. From \(\sin\theta_{\max} < \lambda/d - 1\): 0.232 → 13.4° at 5 MHz; at 7.5 MHz \(\lambda/d - 1 = -0.18 < 0\), so a lobe is visible even unsteered (at \(\pm\)55.2°).

Q9Plane waves over ±10° with receive F# 2 at 5 MHz: estimate the lateral −6 dB width, and the width with a single plane wave.

\(\sin 10^\circ = 0.174\), \(1/(2F_\#) = 0.25\), ratio 0.69, so κ ≈ 0.87 (numerically 0.867): \(w \approx 0.867 \times 0.308/0.424\) = 0.63 mm. One plane wave: \(1.207\lambda F_\#\) = 0.74 mm.
