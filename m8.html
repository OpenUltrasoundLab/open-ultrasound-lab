Analytic signal & IQ demodulation

Most ULM localizers, Doppler estimators and phase-based sound-speed methods you will read about run on complex baseband (IQ) data rather than RF (coherence factors are computed on either). This module rebuilds the analytic signal and the IQ pipeline from the spectrum up, and shows exactly what changes in your DAS beamformer when its input is IQ.

You will be able to

  • Explain why a real signal's negative frequencies are redundant and build the analytic signal with an FFT, including its edge failures
  • Design an IQ chain (mix, low-pass, decimate) and pick \(f_d\), cutoff and decimation factor from the signal bandwidth
  • Convert a phase shift to a delay and displacement, and state the wrap limit
  • Beamform IQ data correctly (envelope interpolation plus phase rotation) and predict the loss if the rotation is omitted
  • Log-compress an envelope and estimate the attenuation-induced center-frequency downshift from IQ

In 60 seconds
  1. A real signal carries its spectrum twice: A real signal’s negative frequencies mirror its positive ones, so half the spectrum is redundant. A real sample also cannot tell a positive angle from a negative one: cos 60° equals cos(−60°). Keeping one rotating arrow (phasor) instead of the mirrored pair resolves the sign, which flow direction needs. §08.1
  2. The analytic signal keeps the positive half: Deleting negative frequencies and doubling the rest gives the analytic signal \(x_a = x + j\mathcal{H}\{x\}\), \(\mathcal{H}\) being the Hilbert transform (a 90° phase shifter). Its magnitude is the envelope, its angle the phase. The envelope is exact only while it varies slower than the carrier: error 4 × 10⁻⁵ at 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth. §08.2
  3. IQ is the analytic signal minus the carrier: Demodulation shifts the analytic signal down by \(f_d\), the demodulation frequency, so IQ needs sampling only for the bandwidth. The complex rate must exceed \(B + 2\lvert\Delta\rvert\), with B the bandwidth and Δ the echo-centre offset from \(f_d\): 8–10 MHz for a 5 MHz, 80 % echo, against 17 MHz real. §08.3
  4. Phase carries delay: A shift in echo arrival rotates the IQ phase, so phase measures sub-sample delay: \(\hat\tau = -\Delta\varphi/(2\pi f_0)\), with Δφ the phase change and f₀ the echo centre frequency. At 5 MHz one radian is 31.8 ns, or 24.5 µm of depth. An error in f₀ scales every delay by the same fraction. §08.4
  5. Beamforming on IQ needs a phase rotation: Delaying IQ data moves the envelope but not the removed carrier, so each channel must be rotated back by \(e^{+j2\pi f_d\tau_n}\), with τₙ its delay and \(f_d\) the demodulation frequency. This is exact, not a narrowband approximation. Without it, 32 elements focused at 20 mm lose 13.4 dB. §08.5
  6. Attenuation lowers the centre frequency with depth: Tissue absorbs high frequencies faster, so the echo spectrum slides down with depth. For a Gaussian spectrum the shift grows with attenuation, with bandwidth squared and with depth: about −0.187 MHz/cm at 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth and 0.5 dB/cm/MHz. The phase of the lag-one autocorrelation of IQ samples measures it. §08.6
  7. Two frequencies, two jobs: Rotate IQ data with the demodulation frequency, exactly what the demodulator removed. Convert phase to delay with the local echo centre frequency, which attenuation lowers with depth. Swapping them either defocuses the beamformed sum or biases every displacement and velocity by the ratio of the two frequencies. §08.7
From the field
  • Convert phase to delay or velocity with the received centre frequency at that depth, not the demodulation frequency. Attenuation pulls the echo centre down with depth, and a frequency mismatch scales every delay by the same fraction. Using a 5.3 MHz demodulation frequency for a 5.0 MHz echo biases delays by −5.7 %. §08.6 §08.7
  • Keep the demodulation frequency and its time origin identical in every frame of an ensemble. Phases compared across the repeated frames (the ensemble) are unbiased only because the leftover carrier rotation is the same in each frame at a given depth. A per-frame change appears as a phase step that looks like motion. §08.4
  • Size the low-pass filter and IQ rate for the whole band in use, harmonic modes and offset included. Aliasing during decimation (sample-rate reduction) leaves kept samples looking fine and corrupts everything between them, irreversibly. Take bandwidth at −20 to −40 dB; decimating a 5 MHz, 60 % echo to 5 MHz already overlaps the replicas. §08.3
  • Rotate IQ data with the demodulation frequency stored with the data, never the nominal transmit frequency. The rotation must undo exactly what the demodulator removed; any other frequency leaves a depth-dependent phase error across the aperture. The symptom is gain oscillating with depth; with no rotation, 32 elements focused at 20 mm lose 13.4 dB. §08.5

08.1Real signals carry their spectrum twice

Take any real signal \(x(t)\) and its Fourier transform \(X(f) = \int x(t)\, e^{-j2\pi f t}\, dt\). Conjugating both sides and using \(x^* = x\) gives the Hermitian symmetry that drives this whole module:

\[ \begin{gathered} X(-f) = X^*(f) \\ \Longleftrightarrow\quad \lvert X(-f)\rvert = \lvert X(f)\rvert, \qquad \angle X(-f) = -\angle X(f) \end{gathered} \]
(08.1)

The negative-frequency half is the mirror-conjugate of the positive half. It contains no information that the positive half does not already contain.

The elementary case is a cosine. Euler's formula splits it into two counter-rotating phasors of half amplitude:

\[ \cos(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{\,j(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi)} + \tfrac{1}{2}\, e^{-j(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi)} \]
(08.2)
\(f_0\)

One phasor at \(+f_0\), one at \(-f_0\). Their imaginary parts cancel at every instant, so the sum stays on the real axis. The single phasor \(e^{j(2\pi f_0 t+\varphi)}\) carries the same information: its real part is the cosine and its angle is the phase, available at every instant.

A phasor tells you amplitude and phase at one instant. A real sample does not: the value \(\cos\theta = 0.5\) is consistent with \(\theta = +60°\) and \(\theta = -60°\). This sign ambiguity is exactly the redundancy in the spectrum. Discarding the negative-frequency phasor resolves it.

Intuition

Think of the real RF trace as the shadow of a rotating phasor on the real axis. The shadow alone does not reveal which way the phasor turns or where it is on the circle. Key takeaways
  • For real x, X(−f) = X*(f): the negative-frequency half carries no information the positive half lacks.
  • cos θ = ½ejθ + ½e−jθ: cos 60° = cos(−60°), so a real sample has no sign of phase; keeping one phasor resolves it.

\[ X_a(f) = 2\,u(f)\, X(f) = \bigl[1 + \operatorname{sgn}(f)\bigr]\, X(f) \]

Write \(1 + \operatorname{sgn} f = 1 + j\,(-j\operatorname{sgn} f)\). The imaginary part of \(x_a\) is \(x\) filtered by \(H(f)\): an all-pass filter that shifts every positive frequency by −90° and every negative frequency by +90°.

Check on the cosine: \(\mathcal{H}\{\cos(2\pi f_0 t)\} = \sin(2\pi f_0 t)\), so \(x_a = \cos + j\sin = e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\), the single phasor of Demo 08.1. Three quantities follow from the polar form \(x_a = A(t)\,e^{j\varphi(t)}\):

Is the envelope the "true" envelope? For \(x(t) = a(t)\cos(2\pi f_0 t)\) the analytic signal equals \(a(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\) only if the spectrum of \(a(t)\) lies inside \(\lvert f\rvert < f_0\) (Bedrosian's product theorem). A Gaussian envelope has infinite support in frequency, so the identity is approximate: the error is governed by how much of the positive lobe spills below 0 Hz, a fraction \(\Phi(-f_0/\sigma_f)\) of the Gaussian. At 5 MHz with 60 % bandwidth, \(\sigma_f = 1.27\) MHz and \(f_0/\sigma_f = 3.9\), so the spill is \(4\times10^{-5}\): negligible. At 2 MHz with 120 % bandwidth (\(f_0/\sigma_f = 1.96\)) it is 2.5 %, and the envelope departs from the Gaussian by 2.5 % rms (MATLAB lab, m08_analytic_signal.m).

Mean frequency identity. Weighting the instantaneous frequency by the instantaneous power gives the spectral centroid of the one-sided power spectrum:

\[ \frac{\int f_i(t)\, A^2(t)\, dt}{\int A^2(t)\, dt} = \frac{\int_0^\infty f\, \lvert X_a(f)\rvert^2\, df}{\int_0^\infty \lvert X_a(f)\rvert^2\, df} \]
(08.6)

Proof: \(\int x_a^* \tfrac{1}{2\pi j}\dot{x}_a\, dt\) \(= \int A^2 \varphi'/2\pi\, dt + \tfrac{1}{2\pi j}\int A A'\, dt\); the last term is a boundary term (\(A^2\) vanishes at \(\pm\infty\)). In frequency, Parseval turns the left side into \(\int f \lvert X_a\rvert^2 df\). This is why the lag-one autocorrelation of IQ data estimates mean frequency (Section 08.6, Module 11).

Discrete implementation (FFT method)

For an \(N\)-point record (\(N\) even), take the DFT, multiply by a mask and invert:

\[ \begin{gathered} h[k] = \begin{cases} 1, & k = 0 \text{ and } k = N/2\\ 2, & 1 \le k \le N/2 - 1\\ 0, & N/2 + 1 \le k \le N - 1 \end{cases} \\[4pt] x_a[n] = \mathrm{IDFT}\bigl\{ h[k]\, X[k] \bigr\} \end{gathered} \]

DC and Nyquist bins are their own mirror images (real-valued for real \(x\)), so they are kept with weight 1, not doubled and not zeroed. With this mask \(\mathrm{Re}\{x_a\} = x\) exactly (Marple, 1999). This is MATLAB's hilbert() and SciPy's signal.hilbert.

The mask is multiplication in the DFT domain, hence circular convolution in time with the discrete Hilbert kernel \(h_{\mathcal{H}}[n] = 2/(\pi n)\) for odd \(n\), 0 for even \(n\). That kernel decays only as \(1/n\), so an echo truncated by the record edge rings across the record, and energy at one end leaks into the other end.

Pitfall

Instantaneous frequency is undefined at envelope nulls. In speckle, the phase jumps by up to π where \(\lvert x_a\rvert\) passes near zero, so \(f_i\) spikes far outside the transducer band (even negative). Never average \(f_i\) unweighted; weight by \(A^2\) (which gives the spectral centroid) or use the lag-one autocorrelation estimator of Section 08.6. The same applies to phase-based displacement: phase is unreliable where the echo is weak.

Pitfall

Applying hilbert() to a beamformed frame along the wrong dimension (lateral instead of axial) or to a record that cuts an echo at the edge corrupts the first and last few hundred nanoseconds. Pad with zeros (at least the pulse length) or taper the record before the FFT, then discard the edges.

Ultrasound link

The B-mode envelope is \(\lvert x_a\rvert\); ULM localizes bubbles on \(\lvert \mathrm{IQ}\rvert\), which is the same quantity (Section 08.3). The axial Hilbert transform is also the reason a beamformed RF image and a beamformed IQ image give the same envelope: both are the same analytic signal, one built after beamforming and one before (Section 08.5).

Key takeaways
  • Xa(f) = 2u(f)X(f) and xa = x + jℋ{x}, with H(f) = −j sgn f: −90° for every positive frequency.
  • A = |xa| is the envelope; the A²-weighted instantaneous frequency equals the spectral centroid of the one-sided spectrum.
  • Bedrosian: |xa| equals a(t) only if the envelope spectrum stays below f0; the spill is 4×10−5 at 5 MHz, 60 %, but 2.5 % at 2 MHz, 120 %.
  • The FFT mask is circular convolution with the 2/(πn) kernel: pad or taper the record and discard its edges.

With an ideal LPF (one that passes the whole positive-frequency lobe and none of the image, possible when the echo spectrum lies inside \((0, 2f_d)\)), IQ is the analytic signal with its carrier removed. Hence \(\lvert\mathrm{IQ}\rvert = \lvert x_a\rvert\) exactly, and the IQ phase is the analytic phase minus \(2\pi f_d t\).

Decimation limit. After the LPF the complex signal occupies roughly \([\Delta - B/2,\ \Delta + B/2]\), where \(B\) is its (two-sided, complex) bandwidth. A complex sample stream at rate \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}\) represents frequencies in \([-f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2,\ +f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2)\) without ambiguity, because there is no mirror image to collide with. So the requirement is

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}} = \frac{f_s}{M} &> B + 2\lvert\Delta\rvert && \text{(complex samples)} \\ f_s &> 2 f_{\max} && \text{(real RF samples)} \end{aligned} \]
(08.11)

5 MHz, 80 % (−6 dB) bandwidth: \(B_{-6} = 4\) MHz, but the Gaussian −20 dB width is 7.3 MHz and the −40 dB width 10.3 MHz. A practical IQ rate is 8 to 10 MHz (2 reals per sample → 16 to 20 Mreal/s), versus ≥ 17 MHz real for RF at the −20 dB edge (\(f_{\max} = 8.6\) MHz). \(M = 4\) from 40 MHz is the common choice.

LPF cutoff tradeoff. The cutoff \(f_c\) must pass \(\lvert\Delta\rvert + B/2\) (or the envelope is smoothed and the phase biased) and must reject the image at \(-(f_0+f_d)\) and the noise above \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2\) (or it aliases into the band after decimation). With a 49-tap Hamming FIR at 40 MHz the transition band is \(\approx 3.3 f_s/L \approx 2.7\) MHz wide, which is why \(f_c\) needs margin on both sides.

Pitfall

Aliasing at decimation does not change the samples you keep. It corrupts everything you compute between them: fractional-delay interpolation in the beamformer, upsampling for display, axial phase-based delay estimation and any spectral estimate. Ultrasound link

IQ exports and research pipelines usually fix \(f_d\) per acquisition, commonly at or near the nominal transmit frequency rather than the received center frequency (Section 08.7 covers concrete pipelines). Because attenuation lowers the received center frequency with depth (Section 08.6), real data always has some depth-dependent \(\Delta\). The envelope is unaffected; anything converting phase to time must account for it (Section 08.4).

  • The LPF must pass |Δ| + B/2 and reject the image at −(f0 + fd); decimation aliasing leaves the kept samples intact and corrupts everything computed between them.

IQ rate versus bandwidth. Two reals per complex sample means IQ at \(f_s/4\) carries the same number of reals as RF at \(f_s/2\). The saving is relative to oversampled RF, not to RF at its Nyquist rate. The real advantage of channel IQ is that the carrier phase is restored analytically by \(e^{j2\pi f_d \tau}\), so the interpolator only has to follow the slow envelope. RF DAS must interpolate the carrier itself: with nearest-sample delays the phase error is uniform in \(\pm\pi f_0/f_s\) and the coherent gain is \(\sin(a)/a\) with \(a = \pi f_0/f_s\), −0.22 dB at 40 MHz and 5 MHz, plus quantization sidelobes. Hence RF beamformers oversample or interpolate.

Choosing \(f_d\) when the spectrum downshifts. Attenuation moves the echo center from \(f_0\) toward \(f_0 - 2\alpha_0\sigma_f^2 z\) (08.19), 0.75 MHz at 4 cm in liver at 5 MHz, 60 %. A fixed \(f_d = f_0\) then leaves a depth-growing \(\Delta\), which must fit under the LPF and inside \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}} > B + 2\lvert\Delta\rvert\) (08.11). Either put \(f_d\) at the center frequency expected at mid-depth of interest, or let \(f_d\) track depth. If \(f_d\) varies, the demodulator phase is \(\theta_d(t) = 2\pi\int_0^t f_d(t')\,dt'\), and the IQ beamformer must rotate by \(e^{j\theta_d(\tau)}\), which equals \(e^{j2\pi f_d \tau}\) only for constant \(f_d\). In every case, rotate with what the demodulator removed and convert phase to delay with the local echo frequency \(\hat{f}_c\).

RF or IQ beamforming? Beamform RF when you need the full real spectrum after beamforming (harmonic or spectral QUS processing that wants the band untouched by a demodulation LPF) or when the hardware already does it. Beamform IQ when data rate and GPU memory dominate (ultrafast plane-wave, ULM, functional ultrasound), when the outputs feed slow-time phase processing, or when you will re-beamform the same channel data many times (sound-speed sweeps, autofocus): the rotation makes re-delaying exact at a low sample rate.

08.9How experts think about it

An expert sees every RF echo as a slow complex envelope riding a known carrier. The envelope (magnitude) is a property of the echo; the phase is a property of the echo and of the bookkeeping (which \(f_d\), which sign, which time origin). Every IQ bug is a bookkeeping bug, so the reflex is to ask which frequency was removed, which frequency the echo actually has, and whether the two are being confused.

The envelope does not care about \(f_d\); the phase does.

08.11Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1What is \(H(f)\) for the Hilbert transform, what is \(\mathcal{H}\{\cos\}\) and \(\mathcal{H}\{\sin\}\), and what is \(\mathcal{H}\) applied twice? · screen

\(H(f) = -j\operatorname{sgn}(f)\): −90° on positive frequencies, +90° on negative, unit magnitude. \(\mathcal{H}\{\cos\omega_0 t\} = \sin\omega_0 t\), \(\mathcal{H}\{\sin\omega_0 t\} = -\cos\omega_0 t\). Twice: \(H^2 = (-j\operatorname{sgn} f)^2 = -1\) for \(f \ne 0\), so \(\mathcal{H}\{\mathcal{H}\{x\}\} = -x\) (for \(x\) with no DC). The analytic signal \(x + j\mathcal{H}\{x\}\) has spectrum \(2u(f)X(f)\). Follow-up: why is the time-domain kernel \(1/(\pi t)\) a problem in practice? It decays slowly, so FIR Hilbert transformers need many taps and FFT implementations are circular.

I2Why can complex IQ be sampled at a rate set by bandwidth while RF needs twice its highest frequency? Give numbers for a 5 MHz, 80 % probe. · screen

A real signal's spectrum has a mirror at negative frequencies; sampling must keep the positive band from overlapping the mirror's replicas, hence \(f_s > 2f_{\max}\). IQ has only one band, so \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}} > B\) (plus \(2\lvert\Delta\rvert\) for a demodulation offset). \(\sigma_f = 4/2.355 = 1.70\) MHz; the −20 dB width is 7.3 MHz, so 8 to 10 MHz complex; RF needs ≥ 17.3 MHz at the −20 dB edge, 30 to 40 MHz in practice. Follow-up: is IQ cheaper in bits? At \(f_s/4\) complex it carries the same number of reals as RF at \(f_s/2\); the gain is against oversampled RF and in interpolation accuracy, not against Nyquist-rate RF.

I3Three ways to get a B-mode envelope from RF. Which are exact? · screen

(1) \(\lvert x_a\rvert\) via Hilbert/FFT: exact by definition (subject to edge effects). (2) IQ demodulation then \(\lvert\mathrm{IQ}\rvert\): equal to (1) for an ideal LPF that passes the band and rejects the image; a real FIR adds sub-percent error (0.6 % in the MATLAB lab). (3) Rectify and low-pass: an approximation; \(\lvert\cos\rvert\) has mean \(2/\pi\) and harmonics at \(2f_0\), so the result depends on the LPF and is scaled by \(2/\pi\). Follow-up: do (1) and (2) differ in phase? Yes, by \(2\pi f_d t\).

I4A colleague's IQ beamformer gives a PSF that is 13 dB weak and smeared over several millimetres at 20 mm, but fine B-mode speckle texture. Diagnose. · onsite

Missing (or wrong-sign) phase rotation. Each channel carries \(e^{-j2\pi f_d\tau_n}\) after envelope alignment; summing without \(e^{+j2\pi f_d\tau_n}\) gives a Fresnel-integral loss, −13.4 dB for 32 elements, 0.3 mm pitch, 5 MHz at 20 mm, oscillating with depth. Speckle still looks like speckle because random-phase sums have the same statistics. Test: beamform a point target at several depths; a loss that oscillates with depth and tracks \(\bigl\lvert\frac{1}{N}\sum e^{-j2\pi f_d\tau_n}\bigr\rvert\) confirms it. A wrong-sign rotation doubles the phase error instead of cancelling it. Follow-up: which \(f\) to use in the rotation? The stored \(f_d\), not the nominal \(f_0\).

I5With 5 MHz IQ, what axial displacement corresponds to 1° of phase, and what is the largest inter-frame motion you can track by phase? · onsite

\(d\) per radian \(= \lambda/(4\pi)\) = 24.5 µm, so 1° = 0.43 µm. Phase wraps at \(\pm\pi\), i.e. \(\pm\lambda/4\) = ±77 µm between frames; at PRF 5 kHz that is 0.385 m/s (Kasai Nyquist \(c\cdot\mathrm{PRF}/(4f_0)\)). Follow-up: what limits precision? Phase noise ~ 1/SNR per sample, reduced by averaging over a window; and the conversion frequency, which must be the local \(\hat{f}_c\).

I6Derive the attenuation-induced downshift for a Gaussian pulse. Which \(\sigma\), which \(z\), which units? · onsite

Amplitude spectrum \(G(f) = \exp\bigl(-(f-f_0)^2/2\sigma_f^2\bigr)\); round trip to one-way depth \(z\) multiplies by \(\exp(-2\alpha_0 f z)\) with \(\alpha_0\) the amplitude coefficient in Np/cm/MHz. Completing the square: center \(f_0 - 2\alpha_0\sigma_f^2 z\), same \(\sigma_f\). With the power-spectrum \(\sigma_p = \sigma_f/\sqrt{2}\) the same result reads \(f_0 - 4\alpha_0\sigma_p^2 z\). 5 MHz, 60 %, 0.5 dB/cm/MHz (0.0576 Np): −0.187 MHz/cm. Follow-up: turn this into an estimator: \(\hat{\alpha}_0 = -\text{slope}/(2\sigma_f^2)\), with \(\sigma_f\) measured from a reference; then list the confounders (diffraction, non-Gaussian spectra, frequency-dependent backscatter, LPF clipping of the shifted band).

I7For a CUTE-type sound-speed method that measures phase differences between steered IQ images, what biases the delay maps and how do you fix each? · research

(1) Conversion frequency: \(\Delta\tau = -\Delta\varphi/(2\pi f)\), and \(f\) must be the local echo frequency, which decreases with depth (−0.19 MHz/cm in the example above); using \(f_d\) or nominal \(f_0\) scales delays by \(\hat{f}_c/f\). Fix: estimate \(\hat{f}_c\) from fast-time \(R(1)\). (2) Phase wrapping when \(\lvert\Delta\tau\rvert > 1/(2f_c)\): limit the angle step or unwrap. (3) Low-coherence pixels (low envelope, decorrelation between angles) give random phase: weight by \(\lvert R\rvert\) or coherence. (4) Wrong or inconsistent IQ bookkeeping (sign, \(f_d\) in the rotation) between beamforming and phase estimation. Follow-up: why does a constant \(\Delta\) not matter for the phase difference itself? Both images carry the same residual rotation at the same pixel, so it cancels in the product.

I8You are designing a ULM acquisition: 5 MHz probe, 128 channels, 40 mm depth, 10 kHz plane-wave rate. RF or IQ channel data? Quantify. · research

Round trip to 40 mm: 2 × 40/1.54 = 52 µs. RF at 40 MHz, 16-bit: 2078 samples × 128 × 2 B = 532 kB per transmit, 5.3 GB/s at 10 kHz. IQ at 10 MHz: 520 complex = 1040 reals, 266 kB per transmit, 2.7 GB/s, the same as RF at 20 MHz (4 samples per wavelength). IQ is the natural choice: downstream SVD clutter filtering and Doppler need IQ anyway, and IQ DAS with rotation is accurate at 10 MHz (−0.4 dB with linear interpolation). Follow-up: what do you lose? The band outside the LPF; if you want harmonics or full-band spectral analysis, keep RF.

I9How would you estimate the local center frequency robustly from IQ, and why not average the instantaneous frequency? · onsite

Average \(R(1) = \sum \mathrm{IQ}^*[k]\,\mathrm{IQ}[k+1]\) over a depth window and over lines, then \(\hat{f}_c = f_d + (f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2\pi)\arg R(1)\) (08.20). This is equivalent to the \(A^2\)-weighted mean of \(f_i\), which equals the spectral centroid; unweighted \(f_i\) is dominated by spikes at envelope nulls. In the MATLAB lab, 64 lines and 4 mm windows give 0.02 MHz rms scatter and recover \(\alpha_0\) within 3 %. Follow-up: what aliasing limit? \(\lvert f_c - f_d\rvert < f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2\).

I10Your system samples at exactly \(4f_0\) and forms IQ by taking I and Q from neighboring samples. When is that acceptable? · onsite

When the echo is narrowband relative to the \(1/(4f_0)\) offset between the I and Q samples (50 ns at 5 MHz). For a 60 % Gaussian echo the pairwise IQ has 18 % rms complex error averaged over echo positions; averaging the two Q neighbors ([¼ ½ ¼] after the 1, −j, −1, +j mixer) brings it to about 4 %, and a longer interpolating LPF lower still. Follow-up: what does the 3-tap filter do spectrally? \(\cos^2(\pi f/f_s)\): a null at \(f_s/2 = 2f_0\), where the image sits, and 17 % droop at the 2.7 MHz band edge.

I11Why is IQ delay-and-sum exact even though it uses a single frequency \(f_d\) for the rotation? · research

Because the rotation is not a model of the echo; it undoes the demodulator. \(\mathrm{IQ}_n(t) = x_{a,n}(t)\,e^{-j2\pi f_d t}\) exactly, so \(\mathrm{IQ}_n(\tau)\,e^{j2\pi f_d\tau} = x_{a,n}(\tau)\) for any \(\tau\) and all frequencies in the band. The single frequency is the one that was removed, not an approximation of the echo spectrum. Error sources are only interpolation of the baseband and LPF imperfections. Follow-up: what if \(f_d\) varied with depth during demodulation? Rotate by the accumulated demodulator phase \(\theta_d(\tau) = 2\pi\int_0^\tau f_d\,dt\), not \(f_d(\tau)\cdot\tau\).

I12Your phase-based displacement estimates are 15 % low at 4 cm depth in liver and correct near the surface. What is the likely cause? · research

The phase-to-displacement conversion uses the nominal \(f_0\), but the echo is downshifted: at 5 MHz, 60 %, 0.5 dB/cm/MHz, \(f_c(4\ \mathrm{cm}) = 5 - 0.75 = 4.25\) MHz. The phase is \(-2\pi(4.25\ \mathrm{MHz})\tau\), so \(\hat{\tau} = \tau \times 4.25/5 = 0.85\tau\): 15 % low. Fix: convert with the local \(\hat{f}_c\) from fast-time \(R(1)\) (Loupas-style 2-D autocorrelation does this jointly). Follow-up: could the demodulation LPF make it worse? Yes: if the shifted band is clipped, \(\hat{f}_c\) is biased toward \(f_d\).

08.13Self-check

Q1A 5 MHz pulse has 80 % (−6 dB) fractional bandwidth and a Gaussian spectrum. What is the minimum complex IQ sampling rate, and how does it compare with real RF sampling?

\(B_{-6} = 0.8 \times 5 = 4\) MHz, so the bare minimum (−6 dB support) is \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}} > 4\) MHz with \(f_d = f_0\). A Gaussian is not band-limited: \(\sigma_f = 4/2.355 = 1.70\) MHz, the −20 dB full width is \(2\sigma_f\sqrt{2\ln 10}\) = 7.3 MHz and the −40 dB width is 10.3 MHz. A practical choice is 8 to 10 MHz complex (16 to 20 million real numbers per second). Real sampling needs \(f_s > 2f_{\max}\) with \(f_{\max} = 5 + 3.65 = 8.65\) MHz at −20 dB, i.e. ≥ 17.3 MHz, and 30 to 40 MHz in practice for good interpolation. The IQ rate is set by bandwidth, the RF rate by the highest frequency.

Q2An echo is delayed by 10 ns at 5 MHz. What phase rotation does its IQ signal show, and what axial displacement does that represent?

\(\Delta\varphi = -2\pi f_0\tau\) \(= -2\pi \times 5\times10^{6} \times 10\times10^{-9}\) \(= -0.314\) rad = −18°. Displacement \(d = c\tau/2 = 1540 \times 10\times10^{-9}/2\) = 7.7 µm. The envelope shift of 10 ns is 0.4 samples at 40 MHz and invisible in the magnitude; the phase measures it directly.

Q3Your system demodulates with \(f_d\) = 5.3 MHz but the echo is centered at 5.0 MHz. What happens to the baseband spectrum, the envelope and phase-based estimates?

\(\Delta = f_0 - f_d = -0.3\) MHz, so the baseband lobe is centered at −0.3 MHz instead of 0 and the image at \(-(f_0+f_d) = -10.3\) MHz. In time, \(\mathrm{IQ}(t) = b(t)\,e^{-j2\pi\cdot 0.3 t}\): a residual rotation of 0.3 cycles per µs (one turn every 3.3 µs, ≈ 2.6 mm of depth). The envelope is unchanged as long as the LPF passes \(\lvert\Delta\rvert + B/2\) and \(f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}} > B + 0.6\) MHz. Slow-time Doppler phase is unaffected (the residual rotation is identical in every frame at a given depth), but converting phase to delay or velocity with \(f_d\) instead of the true 5.0 MHz biases it: the measured \(\Delta\varphi = -2\pi(5.0\ \mathrm{MHz})\tau\), so \(\hat{\tau} = -\Delta\varphi/(2\pi f_d) = \tau \times 5.0/5.3\), an underestimate of 5.7 %. An IQ beamformer must still rotate with \(f_d\) = 5.3 MHz, the frequency actually removed.

Q4Why does the FFT Hilbert mask keep bins 0 and \(N/2\) at weight 1 instead of doubling or zeroing them?

For real \(x\), \(X[0]\) and \(X[N/2]\) are real and each is its own mirror (\(-0 = 0\) and \(-N/2 \equiv N/2 \bmod N\)). They are shared between the positive and negative halves, so they are neither redundant nor to be doubled. Weight 1 preserves \(\mathrm{Re}\{x_a\} = x\) exactly, which is the defining property of the analytic signal. Doubling DC would add a constant to \(x\); zeroing it would remove the mean from the real part. Bandpass RF has negligible energy there, so the effect is small in practice, but the mask should be right.

Q5You compute the envelope of a 2 MHz, 120 %-bandwidth pulse with hilbert() and it does not match the Gaussian you transmitted. Is the code wrong?

No. \(\sigma_f = 2.4/2.355 = 1.02\) MHz and \(f_0/\sigma_f = 1.96\), so about \(\Phi(-1.96) \approx 2.5\) % of the Gaussian lobe lies below 0 Hz. The analytic signal discards it and reflects the conjugate of the negative lobe's tail onto the positive side, so \(x_a \ne a(t)\,e^{j2\pi f_0 t}\) (Bedrosian's condition fails). The "true envelope" of such a pulse is not uniquely defined; \(\lvert x_a\rvert\) is the standard, well-defined choice. Demo 08.2 shows the error directly.

Q6An IQ beamformer forgets the phase rotation. Estimate the peak loss at \(z\) = 20 mm for 32 elements, 0.3 mm pitch, 5 MHz.

\(x_{\max} = 15.5 \times 0.3 = 4.65\) mm, \(\lambda\) = 0.308 mm. \(\psi = \pi x_{\max}^2/(\lambda z)\) \(= \pi \times 21.6/(0.308 \times 20)\) = 11.0 rad, so the aperture spans ≈ 1.75 cycles of quadratic phase (3.5 Fresnel zones). \(\bigl\lvert\int_0^1 e^{-j\psi s^2}ds\bigr\rvert \approx 0.24\) → −12.5 dB; the exact 32-element sum gives −13.4 dB (Demo 08.5 reproduces this). The PSF also broadens because the uncompensated phase acts like a strong defocus.

Q7At 5 MHz, 60 % bandwidth, the fast-time centroid falls by 0.20 MHz per cm of depth. What attenuation coefficient does this imply?

\(\sigma_f = 3/2.355 = 1.274\) MHz, \(\sigma_f^2 = 1.623\) MHz². \(\alpha_0 = -\text{slope}/(2\sigma_f^2)\) = 0.20/3.246 = 0.0616 Np/cm/MHz = 0.535 dB/cm/MHz (× 8.686). This is in the range of normal-to-fatty liver. Using the power-spectrum \(\sigma_p\) = 0.901 MHz with the form \(4\alpha_0\sigma_p^2\) gives the same answer; mixing the two \(\sigma\) definitions is the classic factor-of-2 error.

Q8You decimate IQ by \(M\) = 8 from 40 MHz and compare \(2\lvert\mathrm{IQ}[k]\rvert\) with \(\lvert x_a[8k]\rvert\): they match to 0.1 %. Is the decimation safe?

Not necessarily. Decimation picks samples without altering them, so the kept samples always match the reference regardless of aliasing. Aliasing shows up only in what you compute between samples. The test is whether the LPF output is band-limited to \(\pm f_{s,\mathrm{IQ}}/2\) = ±2.5 MHz: for a 5 MHz, 60 % pulse the −20 dB half-width is 2.7 MHz, so it is not, and band-limited reconstruction (and therefore beamforming interpolation) is wrong by several percent. Demo 08.3 reports both errors.

Centroid downshift with depth, measured in human liver

K. J. Parker, R. M. Lerner, R. C. Waag, "Comparison of techniques for in vivo attenuation measurements," IEEE Trans. Biomed. Eng. 35(12):1064–1068, 1988. Full text (author site). Fig. 5(a), 5(c), 5(d) and Fig. 2.

Fig. 2 compares the centroid-shift attenuation with amplitude decay across 49 subjects. Look at 5(c) as the measured version of (08.19): a linear downshift whose slope, divided by \(2\sigma_f^2\), is the attenuation. Then look at 5(d): the Gaussian model says \(\sigma_f\) stays constant with depth, so any trend there is the departure from that model that a real transducer spectrum and diffraction produce. The paper reports centroid-shift values of 0.18 to 1.55 dB/MHz·cm with about 0.2 dB/MHz·cm intraliver scatter, wider than amplitude decay (0.25 to 0.90), the noise price of a spectral estimator.

Echo phase between steered complex images, in vivo sound speed

P. Stähli, M. Kuriakose, M. Frenz, M. Jaeger, "Forward model for quantitative pulse-echo speed-of-sound imaging," arXiv:1902.10639v2 (2019); published as "Improved forward model for quantitative pulse-echo speed-of-sound imaging," Ultrasonics 108, 2020. The method is this module's phase-to-delay step applied to images: each RF signal is made analytic with the Hilbert transform, complex images are beamformed per transmit angle, and the local echo phase shift is the angle of the kernel-averaged Hermitian product of two images (2 × 2 mm kernel, 2° angle steps to avoid phase aliasing). Read the angle-step choice as the \(\lvert\tau\rvert < 1/(2f_0)\) wrap limit of (08.13), and the kernel as the averaging that tames phase noise in low-envelope pixels.
