Doppler and flow imaging

Every flow mode measures one thing: how the phase of a backscattered echo changes from one transmit to the next. Spectral, colour, power, ultrafast and vector Doppler differ in how they sample that slow-time signal, how they remove the tissue that sits 40 dB above it, and which estimator they apply. The same slow-time signal, filtered by SVD, is what ULM tracks and what sound-speed and aberration estimators must not corrupt.

You will be able to

  • Derive the Doppler equation, the pulsed-Doppler Nyquist limit and the range–velocity product \(v_{\max} z_{\max} = c^{2}/(8 f_0\cos\theta)\), and quote the numbers at 3, 5 and 15 MHz
  • Place high-PRF sample volumes, choose burst, gate and STFT window for a spectral display, and explain the mirror artifact and the cost of duplex time sharing
  • Trace a maximum-velocity envelope, compute PI, RI, S/D, TAMAX, VTI and volume flow, and state which errors cancel in the indices and which dominate the flow error budget
  • Follow a spectral Doppler examination from gate placement to a graded result: SRU carotid velocity criteria, mean and peak valve gradients, stroke volume and valve area, and the system-dependent bias that published thresholds inherit
  • Predict the shape of a Doppler spectrum from the velocity profile, the gate, the elevation beam and the aperture (geometric broadening)
  • Write the Kasai and Loupas autocorrelators, their bias and variance, and the noise bias of the variance (turbulence) estimate
  • Design a wall filter (regression or SVD) for a given ensemble and tissue motion, and explain why ultrafast power Doppler gains sensitivity as \(\sqrt{N}\)
  • Estimate 2D velocity vectors from multi-angle Doppler or transverse oscillation, and predict the lateral precision penalty \(\cot\gamma\)

  2. Nyquist and the range–velocity trade-off: Pulsed Doppler samples once per pulse, so velocities above a limit set by the pulse repetition frequency (PRF) alias, and echo return time caps the PRF.   3. What shapes the spectrum: The Doppler spectrum is a beam- and gate-weighted histogram of velocities in the sample volume, broadened by transit time and by the aperture's range of angles.   4. Spectrogram and clinical numbers: Each spectrogram column is a windowed spectrum, trading time against frequency resolution.   6. Power and ultrafast Doppler: Power Doppler displays signal power, a blood-volume map that never aliases. In colour, an alias jumps from brightest red to brightest blue while a true reversal passes through black. Unwrap before measuring, and allow for geometric broadening and noise before calling a wide spectrum turbulence. §29.11 §29.12
From the field
  • Keep the angle θ at or below 60° and verify velocities on a string or flow phantom. Angle error grows with tan θ and aperture broadening inflates peaks by a system-dependent 20–40 %, so only a known-velocity test object (IEC 61685) shows what a scanner reports. Scanners have read string-phantom peaks 0–29 % high. §29.1 §29.3 §29.7
  • Standardize pulse repetition frequency, wall filter, gain and envelope tracer before comparing indices. Indices cancel multiplicative errors, not additive ones such as gain lifting a tracer's peak. A second-order regression filter on 12 pulses at 2 kHz pulse rate removes all flow below 6.5 cm/s at 5 MHz and 60°. §29.6 §29.9
  • Check how close a velocity sits to a diagnostic threshold before grading. Thresholds carry angle, broadening and offset errors in full, so settings alone can change a borderline category. A true 200 cm/s carotid peak systolic velocity crosses the 230 cm/s criterion at 15 % broadening or with a 5° cursor error at 60°. §29.7
  • In high pulse-rate mode, confirm which depth the signal comes from. With several pulses in flight the receiver hears several depths at once, shallow ones less attenuated. At 2.5 MHz, 10 kHz pulse rate and a 12 cm gate, volumes sit at 4.3, 12.0 and 19.7 cm; the shallowest is 19.3 dB stronger. §29.2
  • Prefer indices to volume flow when the decision allows. Volume flow is mean velocity times area, so it keeps every angle error and doubles the relative diameter error. ±5° at 60° plus ±0.3 mm on a 6 mm vessel already gives 18.1 %, and a small central gate on a parabolic profile doubles the flow. §29.6

29.1The Doppler equation and why the angle dominates the error budget

A scatterer moving with velocity component \(v\cos\theta\) along the beam shortens (or lengthens) the round-trip path by \(2v\cos\theta\) per second. The echo phase \(\varphi = -4\pi f_0 z/c\) therefore rotates at the Doppler frequency

\[ f_D = \frac{2\,v\,f_0\cos\theta}{c} \]
(29.1)
\(f_D\)
Doppler shift, Hz (positive for flow toward the probe)
\(v\)
scatterer speed, m/s
\(f_0\)
transmit (centre) frequency, Hz
\(\theta\)
angle between the beam and the velocity vector
\(c\)
speed of sound, 1540 m/s
The factor 2 is the round trip. At 5 MHz, 1 m/s along the beam gives 6.49 kHz: Doppler shifts of blood are audio frequencies, \(v/c \approx 10^{-3}\) of the carrier.

The equation is exact to first order in \(v/c\); the second-order term \((v/c)^{2}\) is of order \(10^{-6}\) and never matters. What matters is \(\cos\theta\). The scanner measures \(f_D\), the operator sets \(\theta\), and the velocity is \(v = c f_D/(2 f_0\cos\theta)\). An angle error \(\delta\theta\) produces

\[ \frac{\delta v}{v} \approx \tan\theta\;\delta\theta, \qquad \frac{v_{\mathrm{est}}}{v} = \frac{\cos\theta_{\mathrm{true}}}{\cos\theta_{\mathrm{set}}} \]
(29.2)
\(\theta\)
beam-to-flow angle, rad
\(\delta v,\ \delta\theta\)
velocity error (m/s) and angle error (rad)
\(v_{\mathrm{est}},\ v\)
estimated and true speed, m/s
\(\theta_{\mathrm{true}},\ \theta_{\mathrm{set}}\)
true beam-to-flow angle and the angle-correction setting on the scanner, rad
At 60° a 5° error is 15 % (linear) or 18.3 % (exact, \(\cos 60°/\cos 65°\)); at 30° it is 5.7 %. Because the error scales as \(\tan\theta\), it grows steeply above 60° and diverges as \(\theta \to 90°\), which is why clinical protocols cap the insonation angle at 60°.

Angle correction in practice. The operator aligns an angle cursor with the vessel axis on the B-mode image and the scanner divides by \(\cos\theta_{\mathrm{set}}\). Three consequences of (29.2) matter at the bedside. First, the error is asymmetric: at 60° a cursor set 5° too steep (65°) reads 18.3 % high and one set 5° too shallow (55°) reads 12.8 % low; at 45° the same ±5° gives +10.0 / −7.7 %, at 70° +32.1 / −19.1 %. Second, the cursor encodes an assumption, that the flow is parallel to the vessel wall. That holds for straight laminar segments; in curves, bifurcations and post-stenotic jets the true direction is unknown and only vector methods (Section 29.11) measure it. Third, even a perfect correction does not make the displayed peak velocity angle independent: the aperture inflates the spectral envelope by \(\cos(\theta - \alpha)/\cos\theta\) (29.7), which at F# 2 is 1.11 at 30°, 1.21 at 45°, 1.39 at 60° and 1.64 at 70°.

Why 60° is a convention. Nothing in the physics singles out 60°: \(\tan 60° = 1.73\) is simply where the growth of the angle error becomes steep. What the convention buys is reproducibility. Because both the angle error and the broadening of the envelope depend on \(\theta\), the same artery measured at 40° and at 60° returns different peak velocities, so velocity criteria only transfer between laboratories and between follow-up studies if the insonation angle is held to the same range (vascular protocols typically ask for 60° or less and for the same angle at follow-up). Echocardiography takes the opposite route: no angle correction at all, the beam is aligned with the jet (\(\theta \approx 0\)), and the residual misalignment underestimates the velocity by \(1 - \cos\theta\): 1.5 % at 10°, 6.0 % at 20°, and 11.7 % in a pressure gradient that scales with \(v^{2}\) (29.19).

CW versus PW. A continuous-wave probe transmits on one half of the aperture and receives on the other. The Doppler signal comes from everything in the overlap of the two beams: no range resolution, but no sampling either, so no aliasing (the limit is the analog bandwidth). Pulsed-wave Doppler transmits a short burst every \(T = 1/\mathrm{PRF}\) and samples the demodulated echo once per pulse at the delay \(2z/c\): this range gate buys depth selectivity at the price of sampling the Doppler signal at the PRF. Everything in 29.2 follows from that sampling.

Key takeaways
  • fD = 2vf₀cosθ/c: 6.49 kHz per m/s at 5 MHz, about 10⁻³ of the carrier; the second-order term (≈ 10⁻⁶) never matters.
  • Angle error δv/v ≈ tanθ·δθ: a 5° error is 5.7 % at 30° and 18.3 % at 60° (exact), and asymmetric (+18.3 / −12.8 % at 60°).
  • The 60° cap is a reproducibility convention; CW has no range gate and no aliasing, PW buys depth selectivity by sampling at the PRF.

29.2Pulsed Doppler: the PRF, the Nyquist limit and the range–velocity trade-off

Between two pulses the scatterer moves \(vT\cos\theta\) toward the probe and the gated IQ sample rotates by \(\Delta\varphi = 4\pi f_0 v T\cos\theta/c = 2\pi f_D T\). The slow-time sequence \(z_n\) is a complex sinusoid at \(f_D\) sampled at the PRF, so it is unambiguous only while \(\lvert\Delta\varphi\rvert < \pi\):

\[ \lvert f_D\rvert < \frac{\mathrm{PRF}}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lvert v\rvert < v_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{c\,\mathrm{PRF}}{4 f_0\cos\theta} \]
(29.3)
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)
pulse repetition frequency (slow-time sampling rate), Hz
\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\)
Nyquist (aliasing) velocity, m/s
\(f_D,\ v,\ f_0,\ \theta,\ c\)
as in (29.1)
5 MHz, PRF 4 kHz, \(\theta\) = 0: \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.308 m/s. A complex (IQ) sequence distinguishes ±\(f_D\), so the full band is one PRF wide, from −PRF/2 to +PRF/2.

The next pulse cannot be fired until the echo from the gate has returned, \(T \ge 2z/c\). Combining \(\mathrm{PRF} \le c/(2z_{\max})\) with (29.3) removes the PRF:

\[ z_{\max} = \frac{c}{2\,\mathrm{PRF}}, \qquad v_{\max}\, z_{\max} = \frac{c^{2}}{8 f_0\cos\theta} \]
(29.4)
\(z_{\max}\)
deepest unambiguous gate depth, m
\(v_{\max}\)
largest unaliased speed at that depth, m/s
\(c,\ f_0,\ \theta\)
as in (29.1)
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)
pulse repetition frequency, Hz
The range–velocity product depends only on \(f_0\) and the angle: 0.0593 m²/s at 5 MHz and \(\theta\) = 0 (0.59 m/s at 10 cm), 0.0988 m²/s at 3 MHz (0.99 m/s at 10 cm). Halving \(f_0\) doubles it; that is why cardiac Doppler of fast jets uses 2–2.5 MHz and still needs CW.

When the flow exceeds \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) the spectrum wraps: the peak of a systolic waveform reappears at the opposite edge of the display. The remedies, in order of preference:

  1. Shift the baseline. The band is still one PRF wide but can be placed asymmetrically, up to nearly \(2v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) in one direction.
  2. Raise the PRF, until \(z_{\max}\) reaches the gate; beyond that, "high-PRF" mode accepts several gates in range.
  3. Lower \(f_0\) or increase \(\theta\); the second trades aliasing for angle error.

High-PRF Doppler. Raising the PRF above \(c/(2z_g)\) means the next burst leaves before the echo from the gate has returned. The receiver still takes one sample per pulse at a fixed delay \(\tau\) after the latest transmit, and that sample now holds the echo of the latest pulse from depth \(c\tau/2\) together with the echoes of earlier pulses from deeper depths:

\[ z_m = \frac{c}{2}\,(\tau + mT), \quad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \qquad \tau = \frac{2 z_g}{c} - m_g T, \qquad m_g = \left\lfloor \frac{2 z_g}{c\,T} \right\rfloor \]
(29.5)
\(z_m\)
depth of the \(m\)-th sample volume (the echo of the pulse fired \(m\) periods before the latest one), m
\(z_g\)
gate depth chosen by the operator; it is one of the \(z_m\) (\(m = m_g\)), m
\(\tau\)
sampling delay after the latest transmit, \(0 \le \tau < T\), s
\(T\)
pulse interval 1/PRF, s
\(m_g\)
number of pulses fired between the gate pulse and the sampling instant (0 in conventional PW)
\(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\)
floor (largest integer not above the argument)
\(c\)
speed of sound, m/s
2.5 MHz, gate at 12 cm, \(\theta\) = 0: conventional PW allows PRF ≤ 6.42 kHz and \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.99 m/s. At PRF 10 kHz, \(\tau\) = 55.8 µs and the sample volumes sit at 4.3, 12.0 and 19.7 cm; \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 1.54 m/s. With 0.5 dB/cm/MHz along the path, the 4.3 cm volume is 19.3 dB less attenuated than the gate (m29_hprf_ambiguity.m).

The Nyquist limit of (29.3) now applies to the higher PRF, but the spectrum is the sum of every sample volume along the line, weighted by its sensitivity and attenuation. HPRF works when the target is the fastest flow on the line (a valve jet) and the other sample volumes fall in tissue or slow flow; the scanner marks all \(z_m\) on the image so that the operator can check this. If another volume crosses a vessel, its spectrum appears with its own sign and, being shallower, often brighter. Two further conditions restrict the usable PRFs: the sampling instant must stay clear of the next transmit (the script lists the margin \(\min(\tau, T - \tau)\), which shrinks to 5.8 µs at 20 kHz), and no volume may land in a second vessel. Continuous-wave Doppler is the limit in which every depth along the overlap of the beams contributes, which is why CW measures any velocity but cannot say where it came from.

29.3Spectral Doppler: what shapes the spectrum

The Doppler power spectrum is, to first order, the histogram of Doppler frequencies of the scatterers in the sample volume, weighted by how strongly the beam and gate insonate each one:

\[ S(f) \propto \int_{\mathrm{lumen}} W(\mathbf{r})\; \delta\!\left(f - \frac{2 f_0\cos\theta}{c}\, v(\mathbf{r})\right) d^{2}\mathbf{r} \]
(29.6)
\(W(\mathbf{r})\)
two-way beam and gate weight at position \(\mathbf{r}\) in the vessel cross-section
\(v(\mathbf{r})\)
local speed along the vessel axis, m/s
\(S(f)\)
Doppler power spectrum at frequency \(f\), arbitrary units
\(\propto\)
proportional to
\(\delta\)
Dirac delta function
\(f_0,\ \theta,\ c\)
as in (29.1)
\(d^{2}\mathbf{r}\)
area element of the vessel cross-section, m²
For laminar Poiseuille flow, \(v = v_{\max}(1 - r^{2}/a^{2})\), and uniform insonation of the whole lumen, the area between radii \(r\) and \(r + dr\) maps linearly onto \(v\): the spectrum is flat from 0 to \(f_{D,\max}\) and its mean is \(f_{D,\max}/2\).

Three things change that shape. The first is the velocity profile: plug flow gives a narrow line at \(f_{D,\max}\); a blunted profile, \(1 - (r/a)^{4}\), puts the mean at \(\tfrac23 f_{D,\max}\). The second is the gate and the elevation beam: a small gate on the axis keeps only the fast core; a thin elevation beam samples one diameter rather than the disk, which tilts the spectrum toward \(f_{D,\max}\) (mean \(\tfrac23\) for the full diameter). The third is the aperture: each element sees the scatterer at a slightly different angle, so a single velocity produces a band of frequencies (geometric or "intrinsic" spectral broadening). With transmit and receive angles spread over \(\theta \pm \alpha\), where \(\alpha = \arctan(1/(2F_{\#}))\),

\[ f \in f_D\left[\frac{\cos(\theta + \alpha)}{\cos\theta},\; \frac{\cos(\theta - \alpha)}{\cos\theta}\right] \approx f_D\,(1 \pm \alpha\tan\theta) \]
(29.7)
\(\alpha\)
aperture half-angle seen from the gate, rad
\(F_{\#}\)
F-number (focal depth / aperture width)
\(f,\ f_D\)
frequency of a spectral component and the Doppler shift of (29.1), Hz
\(\theta\)

\(F_{\#}\) = 2, \(\theta\) = 60°: \(\alpha\) = 14.0°, the spectrum extends to 1.39 \(f_{D,\max}\) (1.42 linearized). Reading the "maximum velocity" off the spectral envelope then overestimates it by 20–40 %. The mean frequency is unchanged to first order.

The transit-time picture gives the same answer: a scatterer crossing a beam of width \(W\) at speed \(v\sin\theta\) is seen for \(W/(v\sin\theta)\) seconds, and a signal of that duration has a bandwidth of order \(v\sin\theta/W\). Newhouse and colleagues showed in 1980 that geometric and transit-time broadening are one phenomenon described two ways. For a velocity estimator the consequence is the same: broadening scales with \(v\), so it is a fixed fraction of \(f_D\) set by the aperture and \(\tan\theta\).

29.4The PW signal chain: burst and gate, direction, and time sharing

Burst and gate. A PW system makes three choices that look independent but are not: the transmit burst (\(M\) cycles at \(f_0\)), the receive gate (how long the demodulated echo is integrated around the delay \(2z_g/c\)), and the transmit frequency. The range weighting of the sample volume is the convolution of the transmitted envelope with the receive gate, so

\[ L_{sv} \approx \frac{c}{2}\,(\tau_b + \tau_g), \qquad \tau_b = \frac{M}{f_0}, \qquad \frac{\Delta f_D}{f_D} \sim \frac{\lambda}{2 L_{sv}} \]
(29.9)
\(L_{sv}\)
axial extent (support) of the sample volume for a rectangular burst and gate, m
\(\tau_b\)
transmit burst duration, s
\(\tau_g\)
receive gate (integration) duration, s
\(M\)
number of cycles in the burst
\(f_0\)
transmit frequency, Hz
\(\Delta f_D / f_D\)
relative width of the Doppler spectrum caused by axial transit through the sample volume (order of magnitude)
\(\lambda\)
wavelength \(c/f_0\), m
\(c\)
speed of sound, m/s
5 MHz, \(M\) = 8, \(\tau_g\) = 2 µs: \(L_{sv}\) = 2.77 mm and \(\lambda/(2L_{sv})\) = 5.6 %. A B-mode-like 2-cycle burst with a 0.4 µs gate gives 0.62 mm and 25 %. Tapered bursts and gates give a −6 dB length shorter than this support.

The burst sets three things at once. Energy: at a fixed peak pressure the echo energy grows as \(M\), +3 dB per doubling (the SNR table of 29.12). Bandwidth: a rectangular \(M\)-cycle burst has a −6 dB (amplitude) bandwidth of \(1.21 f_0/M\). Long bursts are narrowband, and Doppler wants that: every RF frequency \(f\) in the echo band produces its own shift \(2vf\cos\theta/c\), so a fractional bandwidth \(B/f_0\) spreads the Doppler spectrum by the same fraction. This is the axial counterpart of the aperture broadening of (29.7), and the last relation of (29.9) is the same statement in the transit-time picture. Sample volume: \(L_{sv}\) grows with both \(\tau_b\) and \(\tau_g\); the operator's "gate size" usually changes both. A small gate on the axis isolates the fast core (narrow spectrum, but sensitive to placement and vessel motion); a gate covering the lumen gives the whole-lumen spectrum needed for volume flow (Section 29.6) and includes more wall signal. Because PW fires long bursts repeatedly along one line, its time-averaged intensity is high, and spectral Doppler typically has the highest thermal index of the standard modes (Module 22).

Direction. Quadrature demodulation (Module 08) gives a complex slow-time signal \(z = I + jQ\) whose spectrum is not symmetric: with the sign convention of (29.1), flow toward the probe rotates \(z\) counter-clockwise (positive \(f_D\)) and flow away rotates it clockwise. The spectrogram separates the directions simply by plotting positive frequencies above the baseline and negative ones below. The loudspeakers need two real signals instead, one per direction; the phasing-filter method shifts \(Q\) by 90° with a Hilbert transformer and adds or subtracts it:

\[ s_{\mathrm{fwd}}(t) = I(t) - \mathcal{H}\{Q\}(t), \qquad s_{\mathrm{rev}}(t) = I(t) + \mathcal{H}\{Q\}(t) \]
(29.10)
\(I,\ Q\)

\(\mathcal{H}\)

\(s_{\mathrm{fwd}},\ s_{\mathrm{rev}}\)

For \(z = e^{j2\pi f_D t}\) with \(f_D > 0\): \(I = \cos\), \(Q = \sin\), \(\mathcal{H}\{Q\} = -\cos\), so \(s_{\mathrm{fwd}} = 2\cos(2\pi f_D t)\) and \(s_{\mathrm{rev}} = 0\); for \(f_D < 0\) the roles swap. In general \(s_{\mathrm{fwd}} = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\{z_+\}\), twice the real part of the positive-frequency half of \(z\). The opposite demodulation sign convention swaps the labels.

The mirror artifact. A copy of the spectrum reflected about the baseline has three possible causes. In an analog quadrature receiver, unequal channel gains or a quadrature phase error leave an image at \(-f_D\):

\[ \frac{P_{\mathrm{mirror}}}{P_{\mathrm{true}}} = \frac{1 - 2g\cos\phi + g^{2}}{1 + 2g\cos\phi + g^{2}} \]
(29.11)
\(g\)
gain of the Q channel relative to the I channel (1 when matched)
\(\phi\)
quadrature phase error, the deviation from 90°, rad
\(P_{\mathrm{mirror}}/P_{\mathrm{true}}\)

With \(I = \cos\omega t\) and \(Q = g\sin(\omega t + \phi)\), \(z = A e^{j\omega t} + B e^{-j\omega t}\) with \(A = (1 + g e^{j\phi})/2\) and \(B = (1 - g e^{-j\phi})/2\). \(g\) = 1.01 and \(\phi\) = 1° put the image 40.0 dB down; \(g\) = 1.05 and \(\phi\) = 3° only 28.9 dB, visible on a 40 dB display.

Digital demodulation of sampled RF makes \(g\) and \(\phi\) exact by construction (Module 08), so in a modern system a mirror usually has another origin. Near \(\theta\) = 90° it is physical: the aperture sees the flow from both sides of the perpendicular, the interval \(\theta \pm \alpha\) of (29.7) straddles 90°, and genuine positive and negative Doppler components exist; changing the angle removes it. Excessive Doppler gain, or saturation of the receiver or ADC, creates image and intermodulation components through the nonlinearity; lowering the gain removes it. The analog mechanism of (29.11) remains relevant to CW and older analog receivers.

Duplex and triplex: sharing the transmit time. A live B-mode (duplex) or B-mode plus colour (triplex) image needs transmits of its own, and they come out of the same time budget as the Doppler pulses. If every PW transmit is followed by \(n_I\) imaging transmits,

\[ \mathrm{PRF}_{\mathrm{PW}} = \frac{\mathrm{PRF}_{tx}}{1 + n_I}, \qquad F_{\mathrm{img}} = \frac{n_I}{1 + n_I}\,\frac{\mathrm{PRF}_{tx}}{N_{tx}} \]
(29.12)
\(\mathrm{PRF}_{tx}\)

\(n_I\)

\(\mathrm{PRF}_{\mathrm{PW}}\)

\(N_{tx}\)

Imaging to 6 cm allows \(\mathrm{PRF}_{tx}\) ≤ 12.8 kHz. With \(\mathrm{PRF}_{tx}\) = 12 kHz and \(n_I\) = 1, \(\mathrm{PRF}_{\mathrm{PW}}\) = 6 kHz (\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.92 m/s at 5 MHz and 60°, half of the 1.85 m/s of PW alone); a 128-line B-mode updates at 46.9 Hz, and adding 32 colour lines × 10 pulses (448 transmits per frame) brings the triplex frame rate to 13.4 Hz.

Pulse-by-pulse interleaving keeps the Doppler record continuous at the cost of PRF and frame rate. The alternative is to interleave in blocks: Doppler runs alone for a while, then pauses while imaging frames are acquired. The PRF is preserved, but the spectrogram has gaps and the audio clicks; gap filling (interpolation or prediction from the neighbouring spectra) hides them, and many systems offer an "update" mode that freezes the image while the spectrum runs. Spectral estimators that need fewer contiguous samples (Section 29.5) relax this trade-off, which was one motivation for the adaptive estimators of Gran, Jakobsson and Jensen.

Key takeaways
  • Sample volume Lsv ≈ c(τb + τg)/2: 8 cycles and a 2 µs gate at 5 MHz give 2.77 mm and 5.6 % transit broadening; 2 cycles and 0.4 µs give 0.62 mm and 25 %.
  • Longer bursts add energy (+3 dB per doubling) and narrow the band (1.21 f₀/M), which Doppler wants.
  • I/Q imbalance g = 1.01, φ = 1° leaves a mirror 40.0 dB down; g = 1.05, φ = 3° only 28.9 dB. With digital demodulation a mirror near θ = 90° is physical.
  • Duplex sharing: PRFPW = PRFtx/(1 + nI); 12 kHz with nI = 1 halves vNyq to 0.92 m/s at 5 MHz and 60°.

29.5Spectrogram estimation and display

Section 29.3 introduced the spectrogram and its resolution (29.8). In full, each column is the squared magnitude of a windowed FFT of the slow-time record:

\[ S(m, k) = \frac{1}{U}\left\lvert \sum_{n=0}^{N_w-1} w[n]\, z[mH + n]\, e^{-j2\pi k n/N_f} \right\rvert^{2}, \qquad f_k = \frac{k\,\mathrm{PRF}}{N_f}, \qquad t_m = \frac{mH + N_w/2}{\mathrm{PRF}} \]
(29.13)
\(z[n]\)
slow-time IQ samples of the gate after the wall filter
\(w[n]\)
taper (window) of \(N_w\) samples, \(n = 0 \dots N_w - 1\)
\(U\)
\(\sum_n w[n]^{2}\), so that white noise of power \(P_n\) gives \(E[S] = P_n\) for any taper
\(H\)
hop between columns, samples; the overlap is \(1 - H/N_w\)
\(N_f\)
FFT length, \(N_f \ge N_w\) (zero padding interpolates the spectrum, it does not resolve it)
\(m,\ k\)
column (time) index and bin index, \(k = -N_f/2 \dots N_f/2 - 1\)
\(f_k,\ t_m\)

\(S(m,k)\)

\(\mathrm{PRF}\)

PRF 10 kHz, Hann, \(N_w\) = 128, \(H\) = 20 (84 % overlap): 12.8 ms per column, 500 columns/s, −6 dB width 2.00 PRF/\(N_w\) = 156 Hz, i.e. 0.048 m/s along a vessel at 5 MHz and 60°. Overlap does not sharpen anything; it only samples the (smooth) time axis densely enough for a sweeping display.

Taper. The window trades main-lobe width against leakage: peak sidelobes of −13.3 dB (rectangular), −31.5 dB (Hann) and −58.1 dB (Blackman) for −6 dB widths of 1.21, 2.00 and 2.30 bins of PRF/\(N_w\) (m29_spectrogram_indices.m). A spectrogram is displayed over 30–50 dB, so rectangular sidelobes of the bright systolic band, or of residual clutter near 0 Hz, fill dark parts of the display and bias an envelope tracer upward. Window length. \(\Delta t\) and \(\Delta f\) trade as in (29.8), but during systolic acceleration the frequency also moves within the window. The total smear is roughly \(\lvert\dot f\rvert T_w + 2/T_w\) (chirp plus Hann resolution), smallest at \(T_w \approx \sqrt{2/\lvert\dot f\rvert}\). The demo waveform peaks at \(dv/dt\) = 13.7 m/s², i.e. \(\dot f\) = 44.6 kHz/s at 5 MHz and 60°, which gives \(T_w \approx\) 6.7 ms (67 samples at 10 kHz): a rule of thumb that explains why spectral Doppler uses windows of the order of 5–20 ms.

Speckle in the spectrogram, and Welch averaging. Blood echoes are a Gaussian process, so each periodogram bin is exponentially distributed: its standard deviation equals its mean whatever \(N_w\) is. The grainy texture of a spectrogram is this variance, not noise. Averaging reduces it:

\[ \hat S_{W}(f) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{q=0}^{K-1} \hat S_q(f), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{std}\,\hat S_W(f)}{E\,\hat S_W(f)} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\left[1 + 2\left(1 - \frac{1}{K}\right)\rho_1\right]} \]
(29.14)
\(\hat S_q\)
periodogram of segment \(q\) (length \(L\), consecutive segments overlapping by \(L/2\)) inside the analysis window
\(K\)
number of averaged segments (\(K = 1\): plain periodogram, bracket = 1)
\(\rho_1\)
correlation of adjacent periodograms, \(\big(\sum_n w[n]\,w[n + L/2]\big)^{2}/\big(\sum_n w[n]^{2}\big)^{2}\); 0.028 for Hann at 50 % overlap
\(\hat S_W\)
Welch estimate
\(\mathrm{std},\ E\)
standard deviation and expectation over realizations
Valid for a stationary Gaussian process, bins away from 0 and ±PRF/2. Inside a fixed 128-sample window, \(K\) = 1, 2, 4, 8 segments (\(L\) = 128, 85, 51, 28) give std/mean 1.02, 0.71, 0.52, 0.36 on noise (m29_spectrogram_indices.m), against 1, 0.72, 0.51, 0.36 predicted, while the frequency resolution worsens as \(1/L\).

Welch therefore trades resolution for variance within a fixed observation time. Averaging adjacent columns, or display persistence, trades time resolution instead; averaging over several depth samples of a larger gate trades nothing in time or frequency, which is why spectral systems average over depth when they can.

Short-window adaptive estimators. The periodogram's resolution is fixed by the window, roughly \(\mathrm{PRF}/N_w\). Minimum-variance (Capon) estimators instead design, for every frequency, the filter that passes that frequency with unit gain and minimizes the output power of everything else, using a covariance matrix estimated from the data:

\[ \hat P_{C}(f) = \frac{1}{\mathbf{a}^{H}(f)\,\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{a}(f)}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{R}} = \frac{1}{N_d\,(N_w - L + 1)} \sum_{d=1}^{N_d}\sum_{n=0}^{N_w - L} \mathbf{z}_{d,n}\,\mathbf{z}_{d,n}^{H}, \qquad \mathbf{a}(f) = \big[1, e^{j2\pi f/\mathrm{PRF}}, \dots, e^{j2\pi f (L-1)/\mathrm{PRF}}\big]^{T} \]
(29.15)
\(\mathbf{z}_{d,n}\)
sub-vector \([z_d[n], \dots, z_d[n + L - 1]]^{T}\) of \(L\) consecutive slow-time samples at depth sample \(d\)
\(L\)
sub-vector (filter) length, \(L < N_w\)
\(N_d\)

\(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\)

\(\mathbf{a}(f)\)

\(\hat P_C(f)\)

\(N_w,\ \mathrm{PRF}\)

\(\hat P_C(f)\) is the output power of the filter \(\mathbf{h} = \hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}/(\mathbf{a}^{H}\hat{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\mathbf{a})\): a data-dependent window for every frequency. Check: for white noise, \(\mathbf{R} = \sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}\) and \(\hat P_C = \sigma^{2}/L\) at every \(f\).

Gran, Jakobsson and Jensen (IEEE TUFFC 2009) adapted two such estimators to spectral Doppler: the blood power spectral Capon method (BPC), a Capon estimator of the form (29.15) with the covariance averaged over slow time and depth, and the blood amplitude and phase estimation method (BAPES), which estimates the spectrum with a bank of matched (APES-type) filters. In flow-rig measurements, Field II simulations of femoral-like pulsatile flow and in vivo carotid data, both outperformed Welch's averaged periodogram when the observation window was short. The price is an \(L\times L\) inversion per gate and column, enough depth samples for a stable covariance, and power levels that need care before they are compared with a periodogram display. They are described here, not implemented in the demos.

29.6From the spectrogram to clinical numbers: envelope, indices and volume flow

Most spectral Doppler measurements are read from a trace of the maximum frequency \(f_{\max}(t)\), the outline of the spectrogram, or of the intensity-weighted mean \(\bar f(t)\). Envelope tracers come in two families. Threshold methods take the highest frequency at which the power exceeds a threshold set above the noise floor, usually with a run-length test (several consecutive bins above threshold) and a temporal median filter. Percentile methods take the frequency below which a fraction \(p\) of the signal power lies, after subtracting the noise floor; geometric variants find the knee of the integrated spectrum. Both are biased, in opposite and instructive ways:

  • Noise and gain. A threshold fixed relative to the noise reads higher on a stronger signal, because the skirt of the window and the finite transit time of each scatterer push power above the true edge. A threshold too close to the noise traces noise. Percentile methods are almost insensitive to gain, but underestimate a flat spectrum by construction (\(p\) = 95 % reads 0.95 \(f_{\max}\) before any smear) and become undefined where a column holds no signal.
  • Intrinsic (aperture) broadening. Multiplies the envelope by up to \(\cos(\theta - \alpha)/\cos\theta\) (29.7), so threshold tracers inflate peak velocities; the bias depends on the angle, the F-number and the system.
  • Wall filter. Removes the low end of the spectrum: harmless to \(f_{\max}\) in systole, fatal in diastole if the end-diastolic Doppler falls below the cut-off.
  • Window. Smears the envelope in time during acceleration (Section 29.5), lowering the peak and delaying the upstroke.

From the trace, the standard indices are

\[ \mathrm{PI} = \frac{V_s - V_d}{\mathrm{TAMAX}}, \qquad \mathrm{RI} = \frac{V_s - V_d}{V_s}, \qquad S/D = \frac{V_s}{V_d}, \qquad \mathrm{TAMAX} = \frac{1}{T_c}\int_{0}^{T_c} v_{\max}(t)\,dt \]
(29.16)
\(v_{\max}(t)\)

\(V_s\)

\(V_d\)
end-diastolic velocity (EDV), \(v_{\max}\) at the end of the cycle, m/s
\(T_c\)
duration of the cardiac cycle, s
\(\mathrm{TAMAX}\)

\(\mathrm{PI}\)
pulsatility index (Gosling)
\(\mathrm{RI}\)
resistive (resistance) index (Pourcelot)
\(S/D\)
systolic-to-diastolic velocity ratio
Gosling and King defined the pulsatility index with the peak-to-peak excursion, maximum minus minimum over the cycle; systems commonly use EDV instead, which differs when the waveform has a notch or reversed flow. With reversed diastolic flow \(V_d < 0\), RI exceeds 1 and S/D loses its meaning. The indices are ratios of velocities, so any multiplicative error cancels: the angle correction, the transmit frequency, the sound speed, and to first order the aperture broadening. That is their clinical appeal: they describe the shape of the waveform (downstream resistance) without an accurate angle. Additive errors do not cancel: a threshold offset or a noise-filled diastole raises \(V_d\) and lowers RI; a wall filter above the diastolic Doppler sets \(V_d\) to zero and RI to 1. Velocity integrals and volume flow need the absolute values:

\[ \mathrm{TAMEAN} = \frac{1}{T_c}\int_{0}^{T_c} \bar v(t)\,dt, \qquad \mathrm{VTI} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v_{\mathrm{env}}(t)\,dt, \qquad Q = \mathrm{TAMEAN}\cdot\frac{\pi d^{2}}{4} \]
(29.17)

\(\mathrm{VTI}\)

\(v_{\mathrm{env}}(t)\)

PW tissue Doppler

The same chain with the roles reversed. Tissue velocities are angle dependent like any Doppler velocity, which is why they are measured where the motion is along the beam (longitudinal annular motion from apical views).

29.7From signal to clinical number: the spectral Doppler examination

Sections 29.1–29.6 built the chain from the echo to an envelope. A clinical report compresses it into a handful of numbers, a peak systolic velocity in cm/s, a resistive index, a mean gradient in mmHg, and compares them with thresholds published by a guideline. Each step of the examination either fixes a multiplicative factor, adds an offset, or chooses which part of the flow is measured, and the thresholds inherit all of it. The examination runs in a fixed order, and it is worth following once with the error sources in view.

  1. Find the vessel or the jet on B-mode and colour flow. The colour map shows where the velocity is highest (the aliased core of a stenosis, the narrowest point of a jet), which is where the gate must go.
  2. Place and size the gate. For a peak velocity the gate is small, typically 1–2 mm, and is walked through the narrowing until the highest velocity is found: the 2003 consensus of the Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) asks for the sample volume within the area of greatest stenosis. For volume flow the gate must instead cover the whole lumen (29.17).
  3. Set the angle. In vascular work the operator steers the beam and aligns the angle cursor with the vessel wall or, in a stenosis, with the visible jet, keeping the insonation angle at or below 60° (the SRU wording is "less than or equal to 60°"). In echocardiography the beam is aligned with the jet and no correction is applied.
  4. Set scale, baseline, wall filter and gain. The scale (PRF) is set so that the waveform fills most of the display without aliasing, the baseline is moved to give the forward flow the room, the wall filter is kept as low as the wall thump allows (a 100 Hz cut-off at 5 MHz and 60° already removes everything slower than 3.1 cm/s along the vessel), and the gain is raised until the background just starts to speckle and then lowered slightly.
  5. Acquire several cardiac cycles, freeze, and let the software trace the envelope: the maximum velocity for PSV, EDV, TAMAX, RI, PI and S/D, the intensity-weighted mean for TAMEAN and volume flow, and in the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) the modal velocity. The operator checks that the trace follows the edge of the spectrum and not the noise or a mirror image.
  6. Read the numbers over several beats and compare them with the criteria. The 2017 aortic stenosis update of the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) and the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) (Baumgartner et al.) asks for three or more beats in sinus rhythm and at least five consecutive beats in atrial fibrillation.

The numbers that come out of steps 5 and 6 are not the velocities of 29.1. They are the true envelope multiplied by the angle factor of (29.2), inflated by whatever the system adds to the edge of the spectrum, and shifted by any additive error of the tracer. A useful measurement model is

The Doppler peak gradient is instantaneous, while the catheter laboratory often quotes the peak-to-peak difference between the left-ventricular and aortic pressure peaks, which do not occur at the same time, so the Doppler peak is the larger of the two and the mean gradients are the numbers to compare. And the Doppler gradient is the drop at the vena contracta; pressure recovery downstream makes the net drop smaller, an effect the update considers relevant mainly when the ascending aorta is narrower than 30 mm.

Reporting. A Doppler number is interpretable only with its acquisition conditions, so the report gives the units (cm/s in vascular work, m/s and mmHg in cardiac work), the angle or the statement that none was applied, the vessel segment and side, whether a gradient is peak or mean, the number of beats averaged and the rhythm, and for indices which envelope was traced (maximum or mean) and whether PI uses EDV or the minimum velocity (29.16). For follow-up the useful habit is to repeat the previous system, preset, angle and gate position, because every term of (29.20) except the true velocity is then held fixed and a change in the number reflects a change in the patient.

29.8Colour flow: short ensembles and the autocorrelation estimators

Colour flow imaging cannot afford a spectrum per pixel. It fires a short ensemble (packet) of \(N\) = 6–16 pulses along each line, and estimates three numbers per pixel from the slow-time sequence: the mean Doppler frequency, the power and the spectral width. The hidden frequency. (29.23) uses \(f_0\), the demodulation frequency. 29.9Wall filters: removing tissue 40 dB above the blood

Before any estimator, the tissue echo (the vessel wall, myocardium, liver) has to go. It is 20–40 dB stronger than blood in most organs and more near strong walls, and it moves slowly: breathing, pulsation and probe motion give tissue Doppler of a few to tens of hertz. Left in place it drags \(\arg\hat R(1)\) toward the tissue velocity and floods the power map. The wall filter is a high-pass filter in slow time, but with \(N\) = 8–16 samples there is no room for a long FIR or for IIR transients. The standard answer is the polynomial regression filter (Module 09): project out the best-fitting polynomial of degree \(p\) over the ensemble,

\[ \mathbf{y} = \left(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\right)\mathbf{z}, \qquad f_{-3\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx (0.44 + 0.41\,p)\,\frac{\mathrm{PRF}}{N} \]
(29.26)
\(\mathbf{z},\ \mathbf{y}\)

\(\mathbf{Q}\)

\(p\)

\(\mathbf{I}\)

\((\cdot)^{T}\)

\(N\) = 12, PRF 2 kHz: cutoffs 75, 145, 210 and 278 Hz for \(p\) = 0 … 3 (measured in m29_color_flow_wallfilter.m; the rule of thumb gives 73, 142, 210 and 278). At 5 MHz and \(\theta\) = 60° the \(p\) = 2 filter removes all flow slower than 6.5 cm/s along the vessel.

The filter has \(N - p - 1\) degrees of freedom left, so it also removes \((p+1)/N\) of the white noise, and every degree of \(p\) raises the minimum detectable velocity. The design choice is a trade between residual clutter (too low a cutoff: flash artefacts and velocities pulled toward zero) and lost slow flow (too high a cutoff: holes near the vessel walls where the parabolic profile is slow). 29.10Power Doppler, ultrafast Doppler and functional ultrasound

Power Doppler displays \(\hat R(0)\), the energy left after the wall filter, and ignores the phase. It has no aliasing and no angle dependence of its value (only of whether the flow survives the wall filter), and the power is proportional to the number of moving red cells in the resolution cell: a blood-volume map rather than a velocity map. Its sensitivity is set by how well the power estimate separates a weak vessel from the noise floor, and that improves with the number of independent samples.

Ultrafast Doppler (Bercoff et al., IEEE TUFFC 2011) replaces line-by-line packets with tilted plane waves that insonify the whole field, coherently compounded into frames at 0.5–2 kHz (Figure 29.C). Every pixel then has a continuous slow-time record: the ensemble is set by the acquisition time, not by the number of lines, and \(N\) = 200–400 is routine. Two things follow. First, the wall filter can use space: tissue motion is spatially coherent (the whole organ moves together), blood is not, so the Casorati matrix of pixels × time separates them by rank (Demené et al., IEEE TMI 2015; Module 06):

\[ \mathbf{S} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sigma_k\, \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^{H}, \qquad \mathbf{S}_f = \mathbf{S} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sigma_k\, \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^{H} = \mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{V}_K\mathbf{V}_K^{H}\right) \]
(29.27)

\(K\)

\(\mathbf{V}_K\)

The temporal vectors \(\mathbf{v}_k\) are the eigenvectors of the \(N\times N\) matrix \(\mathbf{S}^{H}\mathbf{S}\), so the filter is a data-adaptive projection in slow time, chosen by spatial coherence. Tissue moving at the same Doppler frequency as slow blood is removed; slow blood is kept.

Second, the power estimate gets \(N - K\) degrees of freedom. For complex Gaussian noise of power \(P_n\) the background power Doppler value has mean \(P_n (N-K)/N\) and standard deviation \(P_n\sqrt{N-K}/N\); a vessel adds \(P_b (N-K)/N\). The contrast-to-noise ratio of the power map is therefore

\[ \hat P(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \lvert s_f(\mathbf{r}, n)\rvert^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{CNR} = \frac{\bar P_{\mathrm{vessel}} - \bar P_{\mathrm{bg}}}{\mathrm{std}(P_{\mathrm{bg}})} \approx \frac{P_b}{P_n}\sqrt{N - K} \]

Sensitivity grows as \(\sqrt{N}\): four times the ensemble doubles the CNR (m29_ultrafast_power_doppler.m measures ×1.98 from 100 to 400 frames). With \(P_b/P_n\) = −6 dB a vessel crosses CNR = 3 near \(N\) = 150. Coherent compounding of \(n_a\) angles adds up to \(10\log_{10} n_a\) dB to \(P_b/P_n\) on top.

The assumption hidden in \(\sqrt{N-K}\) is that blood decorrelates within the ensemble. Blood in a 1 mm/s microvessel at 15 MHz has a Doppler of 19.5 Hz and stays correlated for tens of milliseconds; over a 25-frame ensemble it looks as static as tissue and part of it is projected out with the clutter. The script shows this: at \(N\) = 25 the 1 mm/s vessel reaches a CNR of 0.41 against 1.2 predicted, at \(N\) = 400 it is within 16 % of theory.

Functional ultrasound (Macé et al., Nature Methods 2011) uses exactly this sensitivity: ultrafast plane-wave power Doppler images transient changes of cerebral blood volume, including in vessels too small for conventional Doppler, and those changes during a stimulus (neurovascular coupling) are the functional signal. The first paper mapped whisker-evoked activity in the rat cortex and thalamus and followed the spread of epileptic seizures. ULM (Module 31) takes the same SVD-filtered data and, instead of integrating power, localizes the individual microbubbles in it.

29.11Vector flow: measuring the component the beam cannot see

Every estimator so far measures \(v\cos\theta\). Flow perpendicular to the beam gives zero Doppler, and the operator's angle correction assumes the flow direction is known (parallel to the vessel wall), which fails in bifurcations, stenoses, aneurysms and the heart. Spatial unwrapping chooses \(m\) so that neighbouring pixels differ by less than \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) (Itoh's condition, as in 2D phase unwrapping). Dual-PRF methods use the phase difference \(4\pi v(T_2 - T_1)/\lambda\): with \(T_2 = 1.5\,T_1\) the extended limit is \(2v_{\mathrm{Nyq},1}\).

Spatial unwrapping works when the velocity field is smooth over the pixel grid and the region is connected; it fails at the vessel wall (noise) and in jets narrower than a few pixels. Robust implementations unwrap along paths of high power and low variance, which is the same quality-guided strategy used for phase unwrapping in MRI and interferometry.

Turbulence and spectral broadening. Disturbed flow downstream of a stenosis has a wide range of velocities in the sample volume, so its spectrum widens and the colour "variance" rises. Two other effects widen it too and must be subtracted before calling it turbulence: the geometric broadening of 29.3 (a fixed fraction \(\approx 2\alpha\tan\theta\) of \(f_D\)) and noise (29.25). A velocity gradient across the sample volume (the near-wall shear) also widens the spectrum without any turbulence.

29.16Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Where does the factor 2 in the Doppler equation come from, and how large is the shift for carotid flow? · screen

The scatterer is a moving receiver on the way in and a moving source on the way back, so the path changes at \(2v\cos\theta\) and the echo phase rotates at \(f_D = 2vf_0\cos\theta/c\). For 1 m/s peak systolic flow at 60° and 5 MHz: 3.25 kHz. Follow-up: "is it a frequency shift of the echo?" In PW it is a phase change from pulse to pulse, sampled at the PRF; the shift of the RF spectrum within one pulse (\(v/c \approx 10^{-3}\)) is far below the bandwidth and is not what is measured.

I2Why does PW Doppler alias and CW not? Give the maximum unaliased velocity for a carotid at 3 cm, 5 MHz, 60°. · screen

PW samples the slow-time signal at the PRF; CW demodulates continuously. \(\mathrm{PRF}_{\max} = c/(2z)\) = 25.7 kHz at 3 cm, so \(v_{\max} = c^{2}/(8f_0\cos\theta\,z)\) = 3.95 m/s (29.4): no problem for a normal carotid, but a severe stenosis jet can exceed it. Follow-up: "what if the gate were at 15 cm?" 0.79 m/s; use baseline shift, lower \(f_0\), high-PRF or CW.

I3Sketch the Doppler spectrum of laminar flow in a vessel and explain why the maximum-velocity envelope overestimates. · onsite

Uniform insonation of a parabolic profile gives a flat spectrum from 0 to \(f_{D,\max}\) (equal area per velocity bin); a thin beam or a central gate tilts it toward the maximum. The aperture spreads each velocity over \(f_D[\cos(\theta + \alpha), \cos(\theta - \alpha)]/\cos\theta\): at F# 2 and 60° the edge reaches 1.39 \(f_{D,\max}\), so an envelope tracer reads 20–40 % high. The mean is unbiased to first order. Follow-up: "how would you correct it?" Calibrate the envelope for the aperture, use a smaller receive aperture, or use the mean.

I4Derive the Kasai estimator. Why is it the mean frequency, and when is it biased? · onsite

\(R(T) = E[z_{n+1}z_n^{*}] = \int S(f)e^{j2\pi fT}df\); for a spectrum symmetric about \(\bar f\), \(R(T) = e^{j2\pi\bar fT}\int S(f')\cos(2\pi f'T)\,df'\), so \(\arg R(T) = 2\pi\bar fT\) (29.23). Biases: asymmetric spectra (small), clutter residue (pulls toward tissue), aliasing, and the RF downshift: using \(f_{\mathrm{dem}}\) instead of the mean RF frequency scales \(\hat v\) by \(\bar f_{\mathrm{RF}}/f_{\mathrm{dem}}\), −10 % for 4.5 vs 5 MHz. Follow-up: Loupas 2D autocorrelation (29.24), which also lowers the variance by normalizing each estimate by the local mean RF frequency of the same block (depth averaging of \(\hat R(1)\) helps both estimators).

I5The variance map lights up in a vein with laminar flow. What is going on? · onsite

Low SNR: noise is white, adds to \(R(0)\) only and reduces \(\lvert R(1)\rvert/R(0)\) by \(\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})\) (29.25). At PRF 4 kHz and 10 dB SNR a 150 Hz spectrum reads 306 Hz. Slow venous flow is also close to the wall filter, which shapes the residual spectrum. Fixes: subtract a noise estimate from \(R(0)\), threshold the variance by power, average over more samples. Follow-up: geometric broadening and shear also widen the spectrum without turbulence.

I6Design a wall filter: N = 10, PRF 3 kHz, 5 MHz, tissue moving at 5 mm/s, clutter 40 dB above blood. · onsite

Tissue Doppler is 32.5 Hz. Regression filters attenuate it by 14 dB (\(p\) = 0), 36 dB (\(p\) = 1) and 61 dB (\(p\) = 2), so \(p\) = 2 is needed to bring the residue below blood. Its cutoff is 380 Hz (rule: 378 Hz), i.e. flow slower than 0.117 m/s along a vessel at 60° is lost. To recover slow flow: longer \(N\) (cutoff ∝ PRF/N), or a lower PRF if the Nyquist limit allows, or an adaptive (SVD, eigen) filter. Follow-up: "why not an IIR filter?" Its transient eats most of a 10-sample ensemble unless it is initialized (projection initialization).

I7Why does an SVD filter beat a high-pass filter for microvascular imaging, and how do you pick K? · research

Slow microvascular flow and tissue motion overlap in Doppler frequency (1 mm/s at 15 MHz is 19.5 Hz, breathing and pulsatility give tens of Hz), so no frequency cutoff separates them. They differ in space: tissue moves coherently and is low rank in the Casorati matrix, blood is not (29.27). A short-ensemble high-pass also leaks motion through the window sidelobes (m29_ultrafast_power_doppler.m: background 11.5 dB above noise). K: from the knee of the singular spectrum, from the spatial similarity of singular vectors (Baranger et al.), or by a power threshold; too small leaves flash, too large removes slow blood that has not decorrelated. Follow-up: large fields with non-uniform motion need block-wise SVD; the noise subspace can also be cut at the high end.

I8Derive how power Doppler sensitivity scales with ensemble length. What stops you from making N arbitrarily large? · research

The background value is a mean of \(N - K\) exponential variables: mean \(P_n\), relative spread \(1/\sqrt{N-K}\); hence \(\mathrm{CNR} \approx (P_b/P_n)\sqrt{N-K}\) (29.28), ×2 per ×4 in \(N\). Limits: the stationarity of the scene (motion moves vessels between pixels and raises the clutter rank), the temporal resolution needed (fUS wants a power value every 0.2–1 s), data rate and memory (\(P\times N\) complex samples per block) and SVD cost (\(\propto PN^{2}\)). Follow-up: compounding adds \(10\log_{10}n_a\) dB to \(P_b/P_n\) at the cost of frame rate.

I9How does cross-beam vector Doppler work, and why is the lateral component noisier? Compare with transverse oscillation. · onsite

Each beam pair measures a projection \(f_k\) (29.29); least squares gives \(\mathbf{v}\) with covariance \(\sigma_f^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\). For \(\pm\gamma\) the lateral row coefficient is \(\propto\sin\gamma\), the axial \(\propto\cos\gamma\): ratio \(\cot\gamma\), 5.7 at 10°. Transverse oscillation creates a lateral wavelength \(\lambda_x = 2\lambda z/d\) (1.85 mm at 30 mm with \(d\) = 10 mm, six times \(\lambda\)), so the lateral phase per unit motion is six times smaller, the same geometric penalty in another form. Follow-up: both degrade with depth because the usable angle shrinks as \(d/z\).

I10How do sound-speed errors and aberration enter Doppler, and how is Doppler used in ULM? · research

\(v = cf_D/(2f_0\cos\theta)\) scales with the assumed \(c\) (a 3 % error is a 3 % velocity error), refraction changes the true \(\theta\), and defocusing (wrong \(c\), aberrating layers) widens the beam, so more angles contribute (more broadening) and the compounded SNR drops (Modules 32, 33). Phase aberration that is constant over the ensemble cancels in \(\arg R(1)\), which is why Doppler is robust to it while B-mode is not. In ULM the SVD filter of 29.10 isolates bubbles; the sign of the bubble's Doppler (or the direction of its track) separates up- and down-going flow, and track velocities give the velocity maps. Follow-up: motion between compounded angles creates a velocity-dependent phase error that biases both Doppler and ULM localization.

I11Choose the STFT for a carotid spectral display at PRF 10 kHz. What limits the window, and what do overlap and Welch averaging buy? · onsite

Hann \(N_w\) samples give \(\Delta t = N_w/\mathrm{PRF}\) and a −6 dB width of 2.00 PRF/\(N_w\) (29.13); during systolic acceleration the frequency also sweeps by \(\lvert\dot f\rvert T_w\) within the window, so the total smear \(\lvert\dot f\rvert T_w + 2/T_w\) is smallest near \(T_w = \sqrt{2/\lvert\dot f\rvert}\): 6.7 ms for 44.6 kHz/s, about 67 samples at 10 kHz. Overlap adds columns, not resolution. Welch segments inside the window cut the speckle (std/mean 1 → 0.36 for 8 segments, (29.14)) at the cost of resolution; averaging over depth samples is free. Follow-up: "how would you get a good spectrum from 16 samples?" Adaptive (Capon/APES-type) estimators averaged over depth (29.15), as in Gran, Jakobsson and Jensen.

I12Why do PI and RI survive a wrong angle correction but not a high wall filter? What does an envelope tracer do to them at high gain? · screen

They are ratios of velocities (29.16): any factor common to all velocities (\(\cos\theta\), \(f_0\), \(c\), to first order the aperture broadening) cancels. A wall filter above the end-diastolic Doppler removes the diastolic signal: \(V_d \to 0\), RI → 1, S/D → ∞. A threshold tracer at high gain adds a roughly constant offset (it catches the window skirt and the noise), which raises \(V_d\) more in relative terms than \(V_s\) and lowers RI: in m29_spectrogram_indices.m a threshold 10 dB above noise at 30 dB SNR reads EDV 0.239 for 0.203 m/s and RI 0.764 for 0.797, while the same threshold at 10 dB SNR reads 0.201 and 0.794. Follow-up: percentile tracers are almost gain-independent but underestimate a flat spectrum (p95 reads about 0.94 of PSV).

I13Explain high-PRF range ambiguity. How would you convince yourself that a jet in the spectrum comes from the gate? · onsite

Above \(\mathrm{PRF} = c/(2z_g)\) the sample at delay \(\tau\) contains echoes of earlier pulses from \(z_m = (c/2)(\tau + mT)\) (29.5); the spectrum is the sum of all volumes, and shallow ones are less attenuated (19.3 dB for 4.3 vs 12 cm at 2.5 MHz). Checks: look at the B-mode/colour image along every marked volume; change the PRF slightly, which moves the extra volumes but not the gate, and see whether the line survives; compare with CW (which shows the same jet if it is real but cannot localize it) and with conventional PW at lower velocity scale (the jet aliases but its timing in the cycle stays). Follow-up: the sampling instant must not coincide with a transmit, which excludes some PRFs.

I14A faint copy of the spectrum appears below the baseline. List the causes and how to tell them apart. · screen

(1) Physical bidirectional Doppler near 90°: the aperture spans \(\theta \pm \alpha\) across the perpendicular (29.7); changing the angle removes it. (2) Too much gain or receiver/ADC saturation: nonlinearity creates image and intermodulation components; lowering the gain removes it. (3) I/Q gain or phase imbalance in an analog quadrature receiver, image at \(-f_D\) with relative power \((1 - 2g\cos\phi + g^{2})/(1 + 2g\cos\phi + g^{2})\) (29.11): 40 dB down for 1 % and 1°; constant relative level whatever the angle and gain. Digital demodulation (Module 08) removes (3). Follow-up: the audio channels separate direction with the phasing filter (29.10).

I15A follow-up carotid study reads an internal carotid PSV of 205 cm/s on scanner B; six months ago scanner A read 240 cm/s. Has the stenosis regressed from ≥ 70 % to 50–69 %? · onsite

Not on this evidence. The ratio 240/205 = 1.17 lies inside the 0–29 % spread in maximum-velocity overestimation that Hoskins (1996) measured between systems on a string phantom, and inside the angle term of (29.20): a cursor 5° steeper at 60° alone gives ×1.18. Check what can be checked: the angle and gate position on both studies, the plaque on grey-scale and colour, the EDV, and above all the internal-to-common carotid PSV ratio, which cancels the system factor \(\beta\) and the cardiac output; if the ratio is unchanged (say 3.4 on both), the difference is the system. Follow-up: "how would you set up the laboratory to avoid this?" Same system and preset for follow-up, a fixed angle convention, and thresholds checked against the laboratory's own correlation data.

I16Why can the mean aortic gradient not be computed from the mean jet velocity, and why does the Doppler peak gradient exceed the catheter peak-to-peak gradient? · screen

The gradient is \(4v^{2}\) at each instant (29.21), and the mean of a square exceeds the square of the mean: for a rounded 4 m/s envelope \(v_{\max}\sin^{1/2}(\pi t/T_{ej})\) the mean gradient is 40.7 mmHg but \(4\bar v^{2}\) is 37.2 mmHg, enough to move a patient across the 40 mmHg line; the 2017 ASE/EACVI update says explicitly that the mean gradient cannot be calculated from the mean velocity. The Doppler peak is the maximum instantaneous pressure difference; the catheter peak-to-peak gradient subtracts the aortic peak from the ventricular peak, which occur at different times, so it is smaller. Mean gradients are the comparable quantity. Follow-up: pressure recovery makes the catheter (net) gradient smaller still when the ascending aorta is narrow, below about 30 mm.

Q17.5 MHz, θ = 45°, measured \(f_D\) = 2 kHz. What is v?

\(v = cf_D/(2f_0\cos\theta)\) \(= 1540 \times 2000\,/\,(2 \times 7.5\times 10^{6} \times 0.707)\) = 0.29 m/s.

Q2A gate at 8 cm, 3.5 MHz, θ = 0. Maximum PRF and unaliased velocity?

\(\mathrm{PRF}_{\max} = c/(2z)\) = 9.63 kHz; \(v_{\max} = c^{2}/(8f_0 z)\) = 1540²/(8 × 3.5 × 10⁶ × 0.08) = 1.06 m/s.

Q3Plug versus parabolic flow, same centreline speed: which has the higher mean Doppler frequency under uniform insonation, and by how much?

Plug: mean = \(f_{D,\max}\); parabolic: \(f_{D,\max}/2\). Plug is twice as high (29.6).

Q4The lag-one phase is 120° at PRF 2 kHz and 5 MHz. Velocity? What if the true velocity were larger than \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\)?

\(\hat v = c\,\mathrm{PRF}\,\varphi/(4\pi f_0)\) \(= 1540 \times 2000 \times (2\pi/3)\,/\,(4\pi \times 5\times 10^{6})\) = 0.103 m/s (\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.154 m/s). The same phase also fits \(0.103 + 2m \times 0.154\) m/s, e.g. 0.411 or −0.205 m/s.

Q5N = 16, PRF 4 kHz, regression order 1. Cutoff, and the slowest axial velocity kept at 5 MHz?

\((0.44 + 0.41)\times 4000/16\) = 213 Hz; \(v = cf/(2f_0)\) = 3.3 cm/s axial (29.26).

Q6Blood-to-noise is −10 dB per sample after compounding and K = 5. What ensemble gives a power Doppler CNR of 3?

\(0.1\sqrt{N - 5} = 3\) → \(N\) = 905 frames, about 0.9 s at 1 kHz (29.28). Compounding 9 angles instead of 1 would add up to 9.5 dB (\(P_b/P_n\) = 0.9) and bring it to \(N\) = 16, at a ninth of the frame rate for the same PRF.

Q7Cross-beam with ±15° (receive steered), axial precision 2 mm/s. Lateral precision?

\(\cot 15°\) = 3.73, so 7.5 mm/s; with receive fixed at 0°, \(\cot 7.5°\) = 7.6, i.e. 15.2 mm/s (29.29).

Q8Gate at 10 cm, PRF 12 kHz. Sampling delay, sample-volume depths within 20 cm, and the gain in Nyquist velocity over conventional PW?

\(T\) = 83.3 µs, \(2z_g/c\) = 129.9 µs, \(m_g\) = 1, \(\tau\) = 46.5 µs; \(z_m\) = 3.6, 10.0 and 16.4 cm (29.5). Conventional PW allows 7.70 kHz, so \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) rises by 12/7.70 = 1.56 (at 3 MHz, \(\theta\) = 0: 0.99 → 1.54 m/s).

Q9TAMEAN 0.20 m/s in a 5.0 mm artery, whole lumen insonated. Volume flow, and the error from a 0.25 mm diameter error?

\(A = \pi d^{2}/4\) = 19.6 mm², \(Q\) = 0.20 × 19.6 × 10⁻⁶ × 6 × 10⁷ = 236 mL/min (29.17). \(2\delta d/d\) = 10 % (exactly +10.25 / −9.75 %) (29.18).

Q10A CW jet of 3.5 m/s. Peak pressure drop by the simplified Bernoulli equation, and the reading if the beam is 25° off the jet?

\(4 \times 3.5^{2}\) = 49 mmHg (29.19). At 25° the measured velocity is \(3.5\cos 25°\) = 3.17 m/s and the gradient 40.2 mmHg, 17.9 % low (\(\cos^{2}25°\) = 0.821).

Q11LVOT diameter 2.1 cm, LVOT VTI 18 cm, aortic jet VTI 80 cm, heart rate 75 beats/min. Stroke volume, cardiac output, valve area and velocity ratio? How would you grade it?

LVOT area \(\pi \times 2.1^{2}/4\) = 3.46 cm²; stroke volume 3.46 × 18 = 62.3 mL; cardiac output 62.3 × 75 = 4.68 L/min (29.22). AVA = 3.46 × 18/80 = 0.78 cm², velocity ratio 18/80 = 0.225: both in the severe range of the 2017 update (< 1.0 cm², < 0.25). A diameter read 1 mm small (2.0 cm) would give 0.71 cm², which is why the ratio, free of the diameter, is reported alongside.
