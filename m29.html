Doppler and flow imaging

Every flow mode measures one thing: how the phase of a backscattered echo changes from one transmit to the next. Spectral, colour, power, ultrafast and vector Doppler differ in how they sample that slow-time signal, how they remove the tissue that sits 40 dB above it, and which estimator they apply. The same slow-time signal, filtered by SVD, is what ULM tracks and what sound-speed and aberration estimators must not corrupt.

You will be able to

  • Derive the Doppler equation, the pulsed-Doppler Nyquist limit and the range–velocity product \(v_{\max} z_{\max} = c^{2}/(8 f_0\cos\theta)\), and quote the numbers at 3, 5 and 15 MHz
  • Predict the shape of a Doppler spectrum from the velocity profile, the gate, the elevation beam and the aperture (geometric broadening)
  • Write the Kasai and Loupas autocorrelators, their bias and variance, and the noise bias of the variance (turbulence) estimate
  • Design a wall filter (regression or SVD) for a given ensemble and tissue motion, and explain why ultrafast power Doppler gains sensitivity as \(\sqrt{N}\)
  • Estimate 2D velocity vectors from multi-angle Doppler or transverse oscillation, and predict the lateral precision penalty \(\cot\gamma\)

29.1The Doppler equation and why the angle dominates the error budget

A scatterer moving with velocity component \(v\cos\theta\) along the beam shortens (or lengthens) the round-trip path by \(2v\cos\theta\) per second. The echo phase \(\varphi = -4\pi f_0 z/c\) therefore rotates at the Doppler frequency

\[ f_D = \frac{2\,v\,f_0\cos\theta}{c} \]
(29.1)
\(f_D\)
Doppler shift, Hz (positive for flow toward the probe)
\(v\)
scatterer speed, m/s
\(f_0\)
transmit (centre) frequency, Hz
\(\theta\)
angle between the beam and the velocity vector
\(c\)
speed of sound, 1540 m/s
The factor 2 is the round trip. At 5 MHz, 1 m/s along the beam gives 6.49 kHz: Doppler shifts of blood are audio frequencies, \(v/c \approx 10^{-3}\) of the carrier.

The equation is exact to first order in \(v/c\); the second-order term \((v/c)^{2}\) is of order \(10^{-6}\) and never matters. What matters is \(\cos\theta\). The scanner measures \(f_D\), the operator sets \(\theta\), and the velocity is \(v = c f_D/(2 f_0\cos\theta)\). An angle error \(\delta\theta\) produces

\[ \frac{\delta v}{v} \approx \tan\theta\;\delta\theta, \qquad \frac{v_{\mathrm{est}}}{v} = \frac{\cos\theta_{\mathrm{true}}}{\cos\theta_{\mathrm{set}}} \]
(29.2)
\(\theta\)
beam-to-flow angle, rad
\(\delta v,\ \delta\theta\)
velocity error (m/s) and angle error (rad)
\(v_{\mathrm{est}},\ v\)
estimated and true speed, m/s
\(\theta_{\mathrm{true}},\ \theta_{\mathrm{set}}\)
true beam-to-flow angle and the angle-correction setting on the scanner, rad
At 60° a 5° error is 15 % (linear) or 18.3 % (exact, \(\cos 60°/\cos 65°\)); at 30° it is 5.7 %. Because the error scales as \(\tan\theta\), it grows steeply above 60° and diverges as \(\theta \to 90°\), which is why clinical protocols cap the insonation angle at 60°.

CW versus PW. A continuous-wave probe transmits on one half of the aperture and receives on the other. The Doppler signal comes from everything in the overlap of the two beams: no range resolution, but no sampling either, so no aliasing (the limit is the analog bandwidth). Pulsed-wave Doppler transmits a short burst every \(T = 1/\mathrm{PRF}\) and samples the demodulated echo once per pulse at the delay \(2z/c\): this range gate buys depth selectivity at the price of sampling the Doppler signal at the PRF. Everything in 29.2 follows from that sampling.

29.2Pulsed Doppler: the PRF, the Nyquist limit and the range–velocity trade-off

Between two pulses the scatterer moves \(vT\cos\theta\) toward the probe and the gated IQ sample rotates by \(\Delta\varphi = 4\pi f_0 v T\cos\theta/c = 2\pi f_D T\). The slow-time sequence \(z_n\) is a complex sinusoid at \(f_D\) sampled at the PRF, so it is unambiguous only while \(\lvert\Delta\varphi\rvert < \pi\):

\[ \lvert f_D\rvert < \frac{\mathrm{PRF}}{2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lvert v\rvert < v_{\mathrm{Nyq}} = \frac{c\,\mathrm{PRF}}{4 f_0\cos\theta} \]
(29.3)
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)
pulse repetition frequency (slow-time sampling rate), Hz
\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\)
Nyquist (aliasing) velocity, m/s
\(f_D,\ v,\ f_0,\ \theta,\ c\)
as in (29.1)
5 MHz, PRF 4 kHz, \(\theta\) = 0: \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.308 m/s. A complex (IQ) sequence distinguishes ±\(f_D\), so the full band is one PRF wide, from −PRF/2 to +PRF/2.

The next pulse cannot be fired until the echo from the gate has returned, \(T \ge 2z/c\). Combining \(\mathrm{PRF} \le c/(2z_{\max})\) with (29.3) removes the PRF:

\[ z_{\max} = \frac{c}{2\,\mathrm{PRF}}, \qquad v_{\max}\, z_{\max} = \frac{c^{2}}{8 f_0\cos\theta} \]
(29.4)
\(z_{\max}\)
deepest unambiguous gate depth, m
\(v_{\max}\)
largest unaliased speed at that depth, m/s
\(c,\ f_0,\ \theta\)
as in (29.1)
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)
pulse repetition frequency, Hz
The range–velocity product depends only on \(f_0\) and the angle: 0.0593 m²/s at 5 MHz and \(\theta\) = 0 (0.59 m/s at 10 cm), 0.0988 m²/s at 3 MHz (0.99 m/s at 10 cm). Halving \(f_0\) doubles it; that is why cardiac Doppler of fast jets uses 2–2.5 MHz and still needs CW.

When the flow exceeds \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) the spectrum wraps: the peak of a systolic waveform reappears at the opposite edge of the display. Three remedies, in order of preference: shift the baseline (the band is still one PRF wide but can be placed asymmetrically, up to nearly \(2v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) in one direction), raise the PRF (until \(z_{\max}\) reaches the gate; beyond that "high-PRF" mode accepts several gates in range), or lower \(f_0\) or increase \(\theta\) (the last one trades aliasing for angle error).

29.3Spectral Doppler: what shapes the spectrum

The Doppler power spectrum is, to first order, the histogram of Doppler frequencies of the scatterers in the sample volume, weighted by how strongly the beam and gate insonate each one:

\[ S(f) \propto \int_{\mathrm{lumen}} W(\mathbf{r})\; \delta\!\left(f - \frac{2 f_0\cos\theta}{c}\, v(\mathbf{r})\right) d^{2}\mathbf{r} \]
(29.5)
\(W(\mathbf{r})\)

\(v(\mathbf{r})\)
local speed along the vessel axis, m/s
\(S(f)\)

\(\propto\)

\(\delta\)

\(f_0,\ \theta,\ c\)

For laminar Poiseuille flow, \(v = v_{\max}(1 - r^{2}/a^{2})\), and uniform insonation of the whole lumen, the area between radii \(r\) and \(r + dr\) maps linearly onto \(v\): the spectrum is flat from 0 to \(f_{D,\max}\) and its mean is \(f_{D,\max}/2\).

Three things change that shape. Profile: plug flow gives a narrow line at \(f_{D,\max}\); a blunted profile, \(1 - (r/a)^{4}\), puts the mean at \(\tfrac23 f_{D,\max}\). Gate and elevation beam: a small gate on the axis keeps only the fast core; a thin elevation beam samples one diameter rather than the disk, which tilts the spectrum toward \(f_{D,\max}\) (mean \(\tfrac23\) for the full diameter). The aperture: each element sees the scatterer at a slightly different angle, so a single velocity produces a band of frequencies (geometric or "intrinsic" spectral broadening). With transmit and receive angles spread over \(\theta \pm \alpha\), where \(\alpha = \arctan(1/(2F_{\#}))\),

\[ f \in f_D\left[\frac{\cos(\theta + \alpha)}{\cos\theta},\; \frac{\cos(\theta - \alpha)}{\cos\theta}\right] \approx f_D\,(1 \pm \alpha\tan\theta) \]
(29.6)
\(\alpha\)
aperture half-angle seen from the gate, rad
\(F_{\#}\)

\(f,\ f_D\)
frequency of a spectral component and the Doppler shift of (29.1), Hz
\(\theta\)

\(F_{\#}\) = 2, \(\theta\) = 60°: \(\alpha\) = 14.0°, the spectrum extends to 1.39 \(f_{D,\max}\) (1.42 linearized). Reading the "maximum velocity" off the spectral envelope then overestimates it by 20–40 %. The mean frequency is unchanged to first order.

The transit-time picture gives the same answer: a scatterer crossing a beam of width \(W\) at speed \(v\sin\theta\) is seen for \(W/(v\sin\theta)\) seconds, and a signal of that duration has a bandwidth of order \(v\sin\theta/W\). Newhouse and colleagues showed in 1980 that geometric and transit-time broadening are one phenomenon described two ways. The point for a velocity estimator is the same: broadening scales with \(v\), so it is a fixed fraction of \(f_D\) set by the aperture and \(\tan\theta\).

The spectrogram is a short-time Fourier transform. The display is \(\lvert\mathrm{STFT}\rvert^{2}\) of the slow-time IQ sequence with a window of \(N_w\) samples:

\[ \Delta t = \frac{N_w}{\mathrm{PRF}}, \qquad \Delta f_{-6\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{2\,\mathrm{PRF}}{N_w}\ \ (\text{Hann}), \qquad \Delta f\,\Delta t \approx 2 \]
(29.7)
\(\Delta t\)
observation time of one spectrum, s
\(N_w\)
number of pulses in the analysis window
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)
pulse repetition frequency, Hz
\(\Delta f_{-6\,\mathrm{dB}}\)

PRF 5 kHz, \(N_w\) = 128: 25.6 ms per spectrum, −6 dB resolution 79 Hz, i.e. 12 mm/s at 5 MHz and \(\theta\) = 0. Shorter windows follow systolic acceleration better but blur velocity; vendors use 5–20 ms windows with overlap.

29.4Colour flow: short ensembles and the autocorrelation estimators

Colour flow imaging cannot afford a spectrum per pixel. It fires a short ensemble (packet) of \(N\) = 6–16 pulses along each line, and estimates three numbers per pixel from the slow-time sequence: the mean Doppler frequency, the power and the spectral width. The estimator, due to Kasai and colleagues (1985), is the phase of the lag-one autocorrelation, the same estimator you met for displacement in Module 11:

\[ \hat R(1) = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=0}^{N-2} z_{n+1}\, z_n^{*}, \qquad \hat v = \frac{c\,\mathrm{PRF}}{4\pi f_0}\,\arg \hat R(1) \]
(29.8)

\(N\)

\(\hat R(1)\)

\(\arg\)

\(c,\ f_0\)
as in (29.1)
\(\mathrm{PRF}\)

\(\hat v\)
estimated axial velocity, m/s
\((\cdot)^{*}\)
complex conjugate
\(n\)
pulse (slow-time) index
Because \(R(T) = \int S(f)\,e^{j2\pi f T}\,df\), its phase equals \(2\pi \bar f T\) exactly for a symmetric spectrum and approximately otherwise: the Kasai estimate is the mean Doppler frequency, with no FFT. Averaging \(\hat R(1)\) over a few depth samples (and lateral pixels) before taking the phase is cheap and reduces the variance.

The hidden frequency. (29.8) uses \(f_0\), the demodulation frequency. The received echo is centred lower, because attenuation downshifts the spectrum (Module 27, frequency-dependent attenuation) and each scatterer weights frequencies differently. Since the Doppler phase per pulse is \(4\pi f v T/c\) at every RF frequency \(f\), the measured phase reflects the mean RF frequency \(\bar f_{\mathrm{RF}}\): using \(f_0\) biases \(\hat v\) by \(\bar f_{\mathrm{RF}}/f_0\). Loupas, Powers and Gill (1995) estimate \(\bar f_{\mathrm{RF}}\) from the lag-one correlation along depth on the same data block:

\[ \hat v = \frac{c}{2}\;\frac{\dfrac{\mathrm{PRF}}{2\pi}\arg \hat R(0,1)}{f_{\mathrm{dem}} + \dfrac{f_s}{2\pi}\arg \hat R(1,0)}, \qquad \hat R(m', n') = \sum_{m,n} z_{m+m',\,n+n'}\; z_{m,n}^{*} \]
(29.9)
\(z_{m,n}\)

\(f_s\)

In m29_kasai_loupas.m a 4.5 MHz echo demodulated at 5 MHz biases Kasai by −10 % (measured −9.6 to −10.4 %), while Loupas stays within 0.3 % and has a lower standard deviation (0.0100 vs 0.0121 m/s at \(N\) = 16). Both estimators sum the same 8-sample depth block; Loupas is less noisy because it divides by each block’s own mean RF frequency, which fluctuates from one speckle realization to the next together with the Doppler phase.

\[ \hat\sigma_\omega^{2} = \frac{2}{T^{2}}\left(1 - \frac{\lvert \hat R(1)\rvert}{\hat R(0)}\right), \qquad \frac{\lvert R(1)\rvert}{R(0)}\Big|_{\mathrm{noisy}} = \frac{\mathrm{SNR}}{1 + \mathrm{SNR}}\; \frac{\lvert R(1)\rvert}{R(0)}\Big|_{\mathrm{clean}} \]
(29.10)

29.5Wall filters: removing tissue 40 dB above the blood

Before any estimator, the tissue echo (the vessel wall, myocardium, liver) has to go. It is 20–40 dB stronger than blood in most organs and more near strong walls, and it moves slowly: breathing, pulsation and probe motion give tissue Doppler of a few to tens of hertz. Left in place it drags \(\arg\hat R(1)\) toward the tissue velocity and floods the power map. The wall filter is a high-pass filter in slow time, but with \(N\) = 8–16 samples there is no room for a long FIR or for IIR transients. The standard answer is the polynomial regression filter (Module 09): project out the best-fitting polynomial of degree \(p\) over the ensemble,

\[ \mathbf{y} = \left(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{T}\right)\mathbf{z}, \qquad f_{-3\,\mathrm{dB}} \approx (0.44 + 0.41\,p)\,\frac{\mathrm{PRF}}{N} \]
(29.11)
\(\mathbf{z},\ \mathbf{y}\)
ensemble before and after filtering, \(N\times 1\) complex
\(\mathbf{Q}\)
\(N\times(p+1)\) orthonormal basis of polynomials of degree \(\le p\) in slow time
\(p\)
regression order (0 = mean removal)
\(\mathbf{I}\)
identity matrix
\((\cdot)^{T}\)
transpose
\(f_{-3\,\mathrm{dB}}\)
−3 dB cut-off of the wall filter, Hz
\(\mathrm{PRF},\ N\)

\(N\) = 12, PRF 2 kHz: cutoffs 75, 145, 210 and 278 Hz for \(p\) = 0 … 3 (measured in m29_color_flow_wallfilter.m; the rule of thumb gives 73, 142, 210 and 278). At 5 MHz and \(\theta\) = 60° the \(p\) = 2 filter removes all flow slower than 6.5 cm/s along the vessel.

The filter has \(N - p - 1\) degrees of freedom left, so it also removes \((p+1)/N\) of the white noise, and every degree of \(p\) raises the minimum detectable velocity. The design choice is a trade between residual clutter (too low a cutoff: flash artefacts and velocities pulled toward zero) and lost slow flow (too high a cutoff: holes near the vessel walls where the parabolic profile is slow). The demo shows this on a simulated colour-flow frame. Sections 29.6 and the SVD filter remove the trade-off by using space as well as time.

29.6Power Doppler, ultrafast Doppler and functional ultrasound

Power Doppler displays \(\hat R(0)\), the energy left after the wall filter, and ignores the phase. It has no aliasing and no angle dependence of its value (only of whether the flow survives the wall filter), and the power is proportional to the number of moving red cells in the resolution cell: a blood-volume map rather than a velocity map. Its sensitivity is set by how well the power estimate separates a weak vessel from the noise floor, and that improves with the number of independent samples.

Ultrafast Doppler (Bercoff et al., IEEE TUFFC 2011) replaces line-by-line packets with tilted plane waves that insonify the whole field, coherently compounded into frames at 0.5–2 kHz (Figure 29.B). Every pixel then has a continuous slow-time record: the ensemble is set by the acquisition time, not by the number of lines, and \(N\) = 200–400 is routine. Two things follow. First, the wall filter can use space: tissue motion is spatially coherent (the whole organ moves together), blood is not, so the Casorati matrix of pixels × time separates them by rank (Demené et al., IEEE TMI 2015; Module 06):

\[ \mathbf{S} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \sigma_k\, \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^{H}, \qquad \mathbf{S}_f = \mathbf{S} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sigma_k\, \mathbf{u}_k \mathbf{v}_k^{H} = \mathbf{S}\left(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{V}_K\mathbf{V}_K^{H}\right) \]
(29.12)

The temporal vectors \(\mathbf{v}_k\) are the eigenvectors of the \(N\times N\) matrix \(\mathbf{S}^{H}\mathbf{S}\), so the filter is a data-adaptive projection in slow time, chosen by spatial coherence. Tissue moving at the same Doppler frequency as slow blood is removed; slow blood is kept.

Second, the power estimate gets \(N - K\) degrees of freedom. For complex Gaussian noise of power \(P_n\) the background power Doppler value has mean \(P_n (N-K)/N\) and standard deviation \(P_n\sqrt{N-K}/N\); a vessel adds \(P_b (N-K)/N\). The contrast-to-noise ratio of the power map is therefore

\[ \hat P(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \lvert s_f(\mathbf{r}, n)\rvert^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{CNR} = \frac{\bar P_{\mathrm{vessel}} - \bar P_{\mathrm{bg}}}{\mathrm{std}(P_{\mathrm{bg}})} \approx \frac{P_b}{P_n}\sqrt{N - K} \]
(29.13)

Sensitivity grows as \(\sqrt{N}\): four times the ensemble doubles the CNR (m29_ultrafast_power_doppler.m measures ×1.98 from 100 to 400 frames). With \(P_b/P_n\) = −6 dB a vessel crosses CNR = 3 near \(N\) = 150. Coherent compounding of \(n_a\) angles adds up to \(10\log_{10} n_a\) dB to \(P_b/P_n\) on top.

The assumption hidden in \(\sqrt{N-K}\) is that blood decorrelates within the ensemble. Blood in a 1 mm/s microvessel at 15 MHz has a Doppler of 19.5 Hz and stays correlated for tens of milliseconds; over a 25-frame ensemble it looks as static as tissue and part of it is projected out with the clutter. The script shows this: at \(N\) = 25 the 1 mm/s vessel reaches a CNR of 0.41 against 1.2 predicted, at \(N\) = 400 it is within 16 % of theory.

Functional ultrasound (Macé et al., Nature Methods 2011) uses exactly this sensitivity: ultrafast plane-wave power Doppler images transient changes of cerebral blood volume, including in vessels too small for conventional Doppler, and those changes during a stimulus (neurovascular coupling) are the functional signal. The first paper mapped whisker-evoked activity in the rat cortex and thalamus and followed the spread of epileptic seizures. ULM (Module 31) takes the same SVD-filtered data and, instead of integrating power, localizes the individual microbubbles in it.

29.7Vector flow: measuring the component the beam cannot see

Every estimator so far measures \(v\cos\theta\). Flow perpendicular to the beam gives zero Doppler, and the operator's angle correction assumes the flow direction is known (parallel to the vessel wall), which fails in bifurcations, stenoses, aneurysms and the heart. Vector flow imaging measures two (or three) components. Four families, reviewed by Jensen, Nikolov, Yu and Garcia (IEEE TUFFC 2016):

  • Multi-angle (cross-beam) Doppler. Transmit and/or receive from \(K \ge 2\) directions; each pair \((\alpha_k, \beta_k)\) measures a different projection of the velocity:

29.12Interview and qualifying-exam questions

I1Where does the factor 2 in the Doppler equation come from, and how large is the shift for carotid flow? · screen

The scatterer is a moving receiver on the way in and a moving source on the way back, so the path changes at \(2v\cos\theta\) and the echo phase rotates at \(f_D = 2vf_0\cos\theta/c\). For 1 m/s peak systolic flow at 60° and 5 MHz: 3.25 kHz. Follow-up: "is it a frequency shift of the echo?" In PW it is a phase change from pulse to pulse, sampled at the PRF; the shift of the RF spectrum within one pulse (\(v/c \approx 10^{-3}\)) is far below the bandwidth and is not what is measured.

I2Why does PW Doppler alias and CW not? Give the maximum unaliased velocity for a carotid at 3 cm, 5 MHz, 60°. · screen

PW samples the slow-time signal at the PRF; CW demodulates continuously. \(\mathrm{PRF}_{\max} = c/(2z)\) = 25.7 kHz at 3 cm, so \(v_{\max} = c^{2}/(8f_0\cos\theta\,z)\) = 3.95 m/s (29.4): no problem for a normal carotid, but a severe stenosis jet can exceed it. Follow-up: "what if the gate were at 15 cm?" 0.79 m/s; use baseline shift, lower \(f_0\), high-PRF or CW.

I3Sketch the Doppler spectrum of laminar flow in a vessel and explain why the maximum-velocity envelope overestimates. · onsite

Uniform insonation of a parabolic profile gives a flat spectrum from 0 to \(f_{D,\max}\) (equal area per velocity bin); a thin beam or a central gate tilts it toward the maximum. The aperture spreads each velocity over \(f_D[\cos(\theta + \alpha), \cos(\theta - \alpha)]/\cos\theta\): at F# 2 and 60° the edge reaches 1.39 \(f_{D,\max}\), so an envelope tracer reads 20–40 % high. The mean is unbiased to first order. Follow-up: "how would you correct it?" Calibrate the envelope for the aperture, use a smaller receive aperture, or use the mean.

I4Derive the Kasai estimator. Why is it the mean frequency, and when is it biased? · onsite

\(R(T) = E[z_{n+1}z_n^{*}] = \int S(f)e^{j2\pi fT}df\); for a spectrum symmetric about \(\bar f\), \(R(T) = e^{j2\pi\bar fT}\int S(f')\cos(2\pi f'T)\,df'\), so \(\arg R(T) = 2\pi\bar fT\) (29.8). Biases: asymmetric spectra (small), clutter residue (pulls toward tissue), aliasing, and the RF downshift: using \(f_{\mathrm{dem}}\) instead of the mean RF frequency scales \(\hat v\) by \(\bar f_{\mathrm{RF}}/f_{\mathrm{dem}}\), −10 % for 4.5 vs 5 MHz. Follow-up: Loupas 2D autocorrelation (29.9), which also lowers the variance by normalizing each estimate by the local mean RF frequency of the same block (depth averaging of \(\hat R(1)\) helps both estimators).

I5The variance map lights up in a vein with laminar flow. What is going on? · onsite

Low SNR: noise is white, adds to \(R(0)\) only and reduces \(\lvert R(1)\rvert/R(0)\) by \(\mathrm{SNR}/(1+\mathrm{SNR})\) (29.10). At PRF 4 kHz and 10 dB SNR a 150 Hz spectrum reads 306 Hz. Slow venous flow is also close to the wall filter, which shapes the residual spectrum. Fixes: subtract a noise estimate from \(R(0)\), threshold the variance by power, average over more samples. Follow-up: geometric broadening and shear also widen the spectrum without turbulence.

I6Design a wall filter: N = 10, PRF 3 kHz, 5 MHz, tissue moving at 5 mm/s, clutter 40 dB above blood. · onsite

Tissue Doppler is 32.5 Hz. Regression filters attenuate it by 14 dB (\(p\) = 0), 36 dB (\(p\) = 1) and 61 dB (\(p\) = 2), so \(p\) = 2 is needed to bring the residue below blood. Its cutoff is 380 Hz (rule: 378 Hz), i.e. flow slower than 0.117 m/s along a vessel at 60° is lost. To recover slow flow: longer \(N\) (cutoff ∝ PRF/N), or a lower PRF if the Nyquist limit allows, or an adaptive (SVD, eigen) filter. Follow-up: "why not an IIR filter?" Its transient eats most of a 10-sample ensemble unless it is initialized (projection initialization).

I7Why does an SVD filter beat a high-pass filter for microvascular imaging, and how do you pick K? · research

Slow microvascular flow and tissue motion overlap in Doppler frequency (1 mm/s at 15 MHz is 19.5 Hz, breathing and pulsatility give tens of Hz), so no frequency cutoff separates them. They differ in space: tissue moves coherently and is low rank in the Casorati matrix, blood is not (29.12). A short-ensemble high-pass also leaks motion through the window sidelobes (m29_ultrafast_power_doppler.m: background 11.5 dB above noise). K: from the knee of the singular spectrum, from the spatial similarity of singular vectors (Baranger et al.), or by a power threshold; too small leaves flash, too large removes slow blood that has not decorrelated. Follow-up: large fields with non-uniform motion need block-wise SVD; the noise subspace can also be cut at the high end.

I8Derive how power Doppler sensitivity scales with ensemble length. What stops you from making N arbitrarily large? · research

The background value is a mean of \(N - K\) exponential variables: mean \(P_n\), relative spread \(1/\sqrt{N-K}\); hence \(\mathrm{CNR} \approx (P_b/P_n)\sqrt{N-K}\) (29.13), ×2 per ×4 in \(N\). Limits: the stationarity of the scene (motion moves vessels between pixels and raises the clutter rank), the temporal resolution needed (fUS wants a power value every 0.2–1 s), data rate and memory (\(P\times N\) complex samples per block) and SVD cost (\(\propto PN^{2}\)). Follow-up: compounding adds \(10\log_{10}n_a\) dB to \(P_b/P_n\) at the cost of frame rate.

I9How does cross-beam vector Doppler work, and why is the lateral component noisier? Compare with transverse oscillation. · onsite

Each beam pair measures a projection \(f_k\) (29.14); least squares gives \(\mathbf{v}\) with covariance \(\sigma_f^{2}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\). For \(\pm\gamma\) the lateral row coefficient is \(\propto\sin\gamma\), the axial \(\propto\cos\gamma\): ratio \(\cot\gamma\), 5.7 at 10°. Transverse oscillation creates a lateral wavelength \(\lambda_x = 2\lambda z/d\) (1.85 mm at 30 mm with \(d\) = 10 mm, six times \(\lambda\)), so the lateral phase per unit motion is six times smaller, the same geometric penalty in another form. Follow-up: both degrade with depth because the usable angle shrinks as \(d/z\).

I10How do sound-speed errors and aberration enter Doppler, and how is Doppler used in ULM? · research

\(v = cf_D/(2f_0\cos\theta)\) scales with the assumed \(c\) (a 3 % error is a 3 % velocity error), refraction changes the true \(\theta\), and defocusing (wrong \(c\), aberrating layers) widens the beam, so more angles contribute (more broadening) and the compounded SNR drops (Modules 32, 33). Phase aberration that is constant over the ensemble cancels in \(\arg R(1)\), which is why Doppler is robust to it while B-mode is not. In ULM the SVD filter of 29.6 isolates bubbles; the sign of the bubble's Doppler (or the direction of its track) separates up- and down-going flow, and track velocities give the velocity maps. Follow-up: motion between compounded angles creates a velocity-dependent phase error that biases both Doppler and ULM localization.

29.14Self-check

Q17.5 MHz, θ = 45°, measured \(f_D\) = 2 kHz. What is v?

\(v = cf_D/(2f_0\cos\theta)\) \(= 1540 \times 2000\,/\,(2 \times 7.5\times 10^{6} \times 0.707)\) = 0.29 m/s.

Q2A gate at 8 cm, 3.5 MHz, θ = 0. Maximum PRF and unaliased velocity?

\(\mathrm{PRF}_{\max} = c/(2z)\) = 9.63 kHz; \(v_{\max} = c^{2}/(8f_0 z)\) = 1540²/(8 × 3.5 × 10⁶ × 0.08) = 1.06 m/s.

Q3Plug versus parabolic flow, same centreline speed: which has the higher mean Doppler frequency under uniform insonation, and by how much?

Plug: mean = \(f_{D,\max}\); parabolic: \(f_{D,\max}/2\). Plug is twice as high (29.5).

Q4The lag-one phase is 120° at PRF 2 kHz and 5 MHz. Velocity? What if the true velocity were larger than \(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\)?

\(\hat v = c\,\mathrm{PRF}\,\varphi/(4\pi f_0)\) \(= 1540 \times 2000 \times (2\pi/3)\,/\,(4\pi \times 5\times 10^{6})\) = 0.103 m/s (\(v_{\mathrm{Nyq}}\) = 0.154 m/s). The same phase also fits \(0.103 + 2m \times 0.154\) m/s, e.g. 0.411 or −0.205 m/s.

Q5N = 16, PRF 4 kHz, regression order 1. Cutoff, and the slowest axial velocity kept at 5 MHz?

\((0.44 + 0.41)\times 4000/16\) = 213 Hz; \(v = cf/(2f_0)\) = 3.3 cm/s axial (29.11).

Q6Blood-to-noise is −10 dB per sample after compounding and K = 5. What ensemble gives a power Doppler CNR of 3?

\(0.1\sqrt{N - 5} = 3\) → \(N\) = 905 frames, about 0.9 s at 1 kHz (29.13). Compounding 9 angles instead of 1 would add up to 9.5 dB (\(P_b/P_n\) = 0.9) and bring it to \(N\) = 16, at a ninth of the frame rate for the same PRF.

Q7Cross-beam with ±15° (receive steered), axial precision 2 mm/s. Lateral precision?

\(\cot 15°\) = 3.73, so 7.5 mm/s; with receive fixed at 0°, \(\cot 7.5°\) = 7.6, i.e. 15.2 mm/s (29.14).
